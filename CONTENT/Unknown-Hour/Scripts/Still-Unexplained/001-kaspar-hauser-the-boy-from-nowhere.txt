## hook

[steady] On the 26th of May 1828, a teenage boy appeared in a square in the German city of Nuremberg, unable to answer almost any question, and holding a letter. This is the case of Kaspar Hauser.

## story

[measured] He was about sixteen. He could walk only stiffly. A shoemaker took him to the house of a cavalry captain, to whom the letter was addressed. The letter claimed he had been left as a baby with a poor labourer, and said that if the captain did not want him, he should hang him. Given a pen, the boy wrote a name: Kaspar Hauser. Offered meat, he recoiled and would eat only bread and water.

[thoughtful] As he learned to speak, he told a story that made him famous across Europe. For as long as he could remember, he said, he had lived alone in a small dark cell. He woke to find bread and water beside him, and he had toy wooden horses to play with. Shortly before his release, a man he never saw came in, and taught him to write his name and to walk. Anselm von Feuerbach, a senior judge, took up his case, and wrote a book about him. Rumours spread that Kaspar was the lost heir to the royal house of Baden, hidden away as a baby to keep him from a throne.

[steady] On the 14th of December 1833, he staggered home in the town of Ansbach with a deep stab wound in his chest. He said a stranger had lured him to a park and attacked him. He died three days later. Police searched the snow, and reported only one set of footprints. A small purse was found in the park with a note in mirror writing.

## question

[measured] The best-supported explanation today is that the Baden story is false. A 2024 genetic study led by Walther Parson, using his hair and other relics, found that his mother's line did not match the Baden family. Many historians, from the time onwards, thought he was a troubled young man who invented much of his past, and who may have wounded himself for attention, and gone too deep. That still does not explain the letter, or how a boy with no past reached that square. Who was he?
