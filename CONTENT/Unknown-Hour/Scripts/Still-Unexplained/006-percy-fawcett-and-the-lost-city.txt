## hook

[measured] In 1925 a famous British explorer walked into the Brazilian Amazon with his son and his son's best friend, to find a lost city he called Z, and after one last letter none of them was ever seen again.

[steady] The explorer was Colonel Percy Fawcett, and this is the story of what we actually know about his final journey into the forests of Mato Grosso, in central Brazil.

## story

[warmly] Fawcett was fifty-seven, a former artillery officer who had spent twenty years mapping the borders of Bolivia and Brazil for the Royal Geographical Society. He was tough, stubborn, and convinced that somewhere in the forest stood the ruins of an ancient city. He took two young men with him: his elder son Jack, who was twenty-one, and Jack's friend from school, Raleigh Rimell.

[steady] Fawcett was secretive about his route. He feared rivals would steal his discovery. He even left instructions that if they did not come back, no rescue party should follow, because it would only cost more lives. They set off on horseback and on foot in April 1925, with two Brazilian helpers, mules and dogs.

[measured] On the 29th of May 1925, at a place they named Dead Horse Camp, Fawcett wrote to his wife. The letter survives. He said the party was in good health, that they were about to go beyond the known region, and he wrote, "You need have no fear of any failure." The helpers turned back with the letters. The three men walked on, into the forest, and that was the last anyone outside it heard.

## question

[thoughtful] The best-supported explanation is that they died within months, of illness, hunger, or at the hands of one of the peoples who lived in that forest and had every reason to fear outsiders. The Kalapalo people, who met them, have long said the three walked east and were killed by another group further on. Bones produced in 1951 as Fawcett's were tested and found not to be his. Over the following decades, many expeditions went in after them, and some searchers never came out.

[steady] Here is the part that has changed. Archaeologists working in that same region since the 1990s have found the remains of large ancient settlements, with roads, bridges and earthworks, hidden under the forest. Fawcett was wrong about a single city of stone, but not entirely wrong about the forest. The question still open is the simplest one: where, in all that green, did the three of them stop walking?
