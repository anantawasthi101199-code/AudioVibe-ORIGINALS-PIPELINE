## hook

[measured] On the 31st of January 1921, a five-masted ship ran aground on the sandbanks off North Carolina with all her sails up, food in the galley, and not one person on board.

[steady] She was the Carroll A. Deering, a big wooden cargo schooner, coming home empty from Rio de Janeiro to Virginia. She had eleven men aboard when she sailed, and the US government spent most of that year trying to work out what had happened to them.

## story

[steady] Her captain was Willis Wormell, sixty-six, a respected old seaman brought out of retirement for the voyage. His first mate was a younger man called Charles McLellan. On the way out, in Barbados, the two quarrelled badly, and according to the US investigators, McLellan was overheard in a bar complaining about his captain and saying he would get him before they reached home.

[measured] On the 29th of January 1921, the Deering sailed past the Cape Lookout lightship, a floating lighthouse off the coast. Its keeper, Captain Thomas Jacobson, later told investigators that a red-haired man with a foreign accent called across that the ship had lost her anchors in a storm. Jacobson noticed something odd. The crew seemed to be milling about on the deck where the officers usually stood. His radio was broken, so he could not pass the message on.

[steady] Two days later the coastguard at Cape Hatteras spotted the Deering stuck fast on the Diamond Shoals, the dangerous sands that have wrecked hundreds of ships. Heavy seas kept rescuers away for four days. When they finally climbed aboard, they found the lifeboats gone. The logbook, the navigation instruments and the crew's personal belongings were gone too. In the galley, according to the boarding party, food had been set out for the next day's meal.

## question

[thoughtful] Five US government departments looked into it, including the Navy and the Justice Department, and their reports did not agree. The explanation most historians now find best supported is that the crew left the ship themselves, after a mutiny or in a panic on the shoals, and were lost in the lifeboats in the winter sea. A bottle with a note found that spring, claiming the ship had been taken by pirates, was later admitted to be a fake, written by the man who found it.

[measured] Some people later added the Deering to the legend of the Bermuda Triangle, though she was found more than six hundred miles from it. That belief explains nothing a storm and a frightened crew cannot.

[steady] What none of the theories explains is the one thing the record cannot settle: what happened to eleven men in two lifeboats, off one of the busiest coasts in America, without a single body or a scrap of wood ever coming ashore.
