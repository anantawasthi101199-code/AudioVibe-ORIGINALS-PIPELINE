## hook

[steady] On the 18th of August 1590, a man named John White stepped ashore on an island off the coast of America, on his granddaughter's third birthday, and found that everyone he had left there was gone.

[measured] The island was Roanoke, in what is now North Carolina. About a hundred and fifteen English men, women and children had settled there three years earlier. There were no bodies, no graves, no signs of a fight. There was only one word, cut into a wooden post: CROATOAN.

[thoughtful] White never found them, and nobody has since. This episode will take one question seriously: what does the evidence really say happened to the people of Roanoke? So let's go back to the start.

## story

[measured] John White was an artist, a careful and gentle one by the look of his work, and his watercolours of the Algonquian peoples of the coast are still among the best records we have of them. In 1587 he became governor of a new colony for Sir Walter Raleigh, the English courtier who held a royal licence to settle this coast. He brought his pregnant daughter, Eleanor Dare, and her husband Ananias.

[steady] They were not meant to stop at Roanoke at all. They were meant to go further north, to Chesapeake Bay. But in July 1587, the ship's captain refused to take them any further. So they landed on an island where an earlier English outpost had already failed, and where relations with local people were bad. English soldiers there had burned a village and killed a local leader, a man called Wingina, the year before.

[thoughtful] On the 18th of August 1587, Eleanor gave birth to a girl. She was named Virginia, the first English child born in the Americas. Within days, there was trouble. A colonist was killed while he was out alone catching crabs. Supplies were short and it was too late in the year to plant. The colonists begged White to sail home and bring help, and he agreed, reluctantly. By his own account, he worried about his belongings and his reputation if he left. He sailed nine days after his granddaughter's birth.

[measured] Before he went, they settled on a signal. If they moved, they would carve the name of the place they had gone to on a tree or a post. If they were in danger, they would carve a cross above it.

[steady] White reached England in late 1587, and walked into a national emergency. The Spanish Armada was coming, and every good ship was held for the war. He tried once in 1588, in two small boats, and was attacked by French pirates and turned back. It was March 1590 before he could get a passage, aboard a privateer, a ship more interested in raiding Spanish vessels than in finding settlers. By then the colonists had been on their own for nearly three years.

[thoughtful] He reached the island on the 18th of August. On a tree on the shore, he found the letters C R O. Then he came to the settlement. The houses had been taken down, not burned. A tall fence of logs had been put up around the place. On one of the main posts at the entrance, with the bark cut away, was the word CROATOAN, in capital letters. White looked for the cross. There was none.

[pause] He was glad. In his own report he says so. Croatoan was an island about fifty miles to the south, today part of Hatteras. It was the home of the people of Manteo, an Algonquian man who had been to England and was a friend to the colonists. White also found chests that had been buried and dug up, with his own books and maps rotted in the rain. A storm blew up, the anchors were lost, and the captain would not risk a trip to Croatoan. White sailed away. He never came back.

## theories

[measured] The first explanation is the simple one, and it starts with White himself: they went to Croatoan. The best case is the carved word and the missing cross. The biggest problem is size. A small island could not easily feed a hundred people. Since the 2000s, a team led by the archaeologist Mark Horton has dug on Hatteras and reported English items, including part of a sword and pieces of lead. The problem is that these could have come from trade.

[steady] The second idea is that they split up, and joined local peoples further inland. In 2012, researchers from the First Colony Foundation looked again at one of White's own maps, and found a small patch covering a hidden symbol of a fort, at the top of Albemarle Sound. Digs near there, at a site in Bertie County, have turned up English pottery of the right kind. The best case is that it fits the map. The biggest problem is that pottery cannot give us names, and it might be from slightly later traders.

[thoughtful] The third is hunger. In 1998, a team of scientists led by David Stahle read the tree rings of ancient bald cypress trees in the area. They found that 1587 to 1589 was the worst drought in about eight hundred years. That would explain why the colonists could not simply stay. It does not say where they went.

[measured] The fourth is violence. The English at Jamestown, twenty years later, were told by people of the Powhatan leader Wahunsenacawh that the lost colonists had been killed. That was reported second-hand, and it may have served those telling it. And the Spanish? They hunted for the colony in 1588 and found nothing at all, so they were not the cause.

[steady] Most historians now lean towards a mix: drought and hunger forced the colonists to break into small groups, and those groups moved in with neighbours, on Croatoan and inland. Several communities in North Carolina, including people of the Lumbee nation, have long oral traditions of English ancestors. No DNA study has yet shown a direct link.

## close

[thoughtful] What we know is that the colonists took their houses apart and left in good order, and that they carved the name of a friendly place. What we do not know is where they ended up, or how long any of them lived.

[steady] John White retired to Ireland, and in 1593 he wrote a last letter, saying he had done what he could and that he left his family to God. Eleanor Dare and Virginia are not heard of again. On a birthday in August, her grandfather stood in front of a wooden post, reading one word, and looking for a cross that was not there.
