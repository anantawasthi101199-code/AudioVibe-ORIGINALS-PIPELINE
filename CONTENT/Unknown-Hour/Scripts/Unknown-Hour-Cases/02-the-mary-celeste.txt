## hook

[measured] On the 4th of December 1872, a sailing ship was found moving across the Atlantic on her own, in good weather, with six months of food on board, and not one of the ten people who had sailed on her.

[steady] Her name was the Mary Celeste. She was an American merchant ship, about a hundred feet long, on her way from New York to Genoa, in Italy, with a cargo of industrial alcohol. The crew of another ship, the Dei Gratia, found her between the Azores, the islands far out in the Atlantic, and the coast of Portugal. On board, there should have been a captain, his wife, their two-year-old daughter and seven crew. There was nobody.

[thoughtful] Over the years this story has collected a great many details that never happened. So the question here is a plain one. Using only what was written down at the time, what made ten people climb into a small boat and leave a good ship behind? So let's go back to the start.

## story

[steady] The captain was Benjamin Briggs, thirty-seven years old, from Massachusetts. He came from a family of sea captains, he didn't drink, and people who sailed with him called him fair. His wife Sarah came with him, as captains' wives sometimes did. So did their daughter, Sophia, who was two. They left their seven-year-old son, Arthur, at home with his grandmother, so that he could go to school. Sarah packed her sewing machine and a small melodeon, a kind of little organ, for the long evenings.

[measured] The crew were seven men: a first mate, Albert Richardson, a second mate, a cook, and four sailors, mostly from Germany. Briggs wrote to his mother before leaving that he had a good crew, and he hoped a pleasant passage. The hold carried one thousand, seven hundred and one barrels of alcohol, made for factories, not for drinking. The Mary Celeste sailed out of New York on the 7th of November 1872.

[steady] We know a little about the next three weeks from her log. The last entry on the log slate, a board the mate chalked notes on, is dated the 25th of November, at eight in the morning. It says the ship had passed the island of Santa Maria, in the Azores, about six miles away. After that, nothing.

[thoughtful] Nine days later, on the 4th of December, the Dei Gratia, a Canadian ship sailing the same route, saw a vessel ahead moving oddly, her sails half set, wandering off course. The Dei Gratia's captain, David Morehouse, knew Briggs. They had reportedly dined together in New York before the voyage. He watched her for two hours. Nobody was at the wheel. Then he sent his first mate, Oliver Deveau, across in a small boat with two men.

[measured] Deveau's account, given under oath, is our best record of what they found. The ship was wet and untidy, but sound. There was about three and a half feet of water in the bottom of the hold, a lot, but not dangerous for a ship of that kind. One of the pumps had been taken apart. A sounding rod, the measuring stick used to check how much water is in the hold, was lying on the deck. Two hatches were open. The cargo was in place. There was plenty of food and fresh water. The crew's clothes and boots were still in their sea chests, and their pipes were still there. The ship's small boat, the only lifeboat, was gone. So were the captain's sextant and chronometer, the instruments for finding your position, and most of the ship's papers.

[pause] Deveau did not report a hot meal on the table, or warm cups of tea, or a cat asleep on a bunk. Those details came later, and we will come to where they came from.

[steady] The Dei Gratia's crew sailed the Mary Celeste to Gibraltar, and claimed a reward for saving her. That led to a hearing in the British court there. The court's attorney general, Frederick Solly-Flood, suspected murder. He pointed to marks on the rail and stains on a sword found in the cabin. Tests showed the stains were not blood. In the end the court found no evidence of a crime, but gave the salvors only about a fifth of the value, a sign that it was never fully at ease. When the cargo was finally unloaded in Genoa, nine of the barrels were empty.

[thoughtful] In 1884, a young doctor called Arthur Conan Doyle, before he wrote Sherlock Holmes, published a short story inspired by the case. He called the ship the Marie Celeste. He invented a survivor, a crazed plot and a warm meal left on the table. Many readers took it as fact, and those invented details are still told as part of the real story.

## theories

[measured] There are three serious explanations, and they share one idea: that something made Briggs believe his ship was in danger, and that he ordered everyone into the lifeboat, meaning to come back.

[steady] The first is the alcohol. It was proposed early, and pushed by the maritime historian Charles Edey Fay in 1942. Alcohol barrels can leak, and the fumes can build up in a closed hold. Perhaps Briggs opened the hatch, smelled fumes, or heard a rumble, and feared an explosion. In 2006 a chemist at University College London, Andrea Sella, built a model hold and lit alcohol vapour inside it. It made a sudden, frightening burst of flame that left no scorch marks behind. The best case is the nine empty barrels. The biggest problem is that the barrels that leaked were made of a different wood, red oak, and nobody on board noticed a smell when Deveau went down there.

[thoughtful] The second is a waterspout, a tornado over the sea, which could have sucked water up and made the gauges and the ship's movement look as if she was sinking. The problem is that there is no record of one, and the damage was light.

[measured] The third came from the researcher Anne MacGregor in 2007. She replotted the ship's route and found that Briggs's chronometer was probably wrong, putting him about a hundred and twenty miles off where he thought he was. The ship had just carried coal, the dust may have clogged the pumps, and one pump had been taken apart, as Deveau found. Her idea is that Briggs, with water rising, pumps failing and land in sight, took his family off towards Santa Maria. The problem is that it still asks us to believe a careful captain left a sound ship.

[steady] Investigators today mostly favour some mix of the first and third: a real fear on board, a quick decision, and a small boat that never made it to land. Mutiny and piracy have little support. Nothing of value was taken.

## close

[thoughtful] Here is what we know. The ten people on the Mary Celeste left in her lifeboat, in a hurry, with their instruments and without their belongings. We do not know what frightened them, or what became of the boat.

[steady] Benjamin and Sarah Briggs, their daughter Sophia, and their seven crew were never seen again. Their son Arthur grew up in Massachusetts without them. And somewhere off the Azores, on a clear morning in late November, a small open boat pulled away from a ship that would sail on without them for nine more days.
