## hook

[steady] In 2019, scientists tested the bones of the dead at a lake in the Indian Himalayas, and found that people lying side by side had died about a thousand years apart.

[measured] The lake is Roopkund, a small, frozen glacial pool in the state of Uttarakhand, about five thousand metres above the sea. The remains of several hundred people lie around it and under its ice. Some of the bones are local. Some, the DNA shows, belong to people whose closest genetic relatives live on the far side of Asia, on the Greek islands.

[thoughtful] The question this episode will take seriously is a plain one. How did they get there, and what killed them? So let's go back to the start.

## story

[measured] In 1942, in the middle of the Second World War, a forest ranger called H. K. Madhwal was walking in this high country when he came to a lake filled with human bones. It was a hard place to reach, a walk of days from the nearest village, across steep slopes and rock. When the snow melted, he could see the skulls lying in the clear shallow water. Some of the remains still had hair and scraps of flesh, kept by the cold.

[steady] The British in India were at war, and their first fear was that this was a party of Japanese soldiers, who had sneaked across the mountains and died of cold. A team was sent to look. They soon saw that the bones were far too old.

[thoughtful] Roopkund does lie on a real route. Every twelve years or so, thousands of pilgrims walk through these mountains on a journey called the Nanda Devi Raj Jat, in honour of the Hindu goddess Nanda Devi. A folk song of the region tells of a king from the plains, Jasdhaval of Kannauj, who brought his pregnant queen and a party of dancers on that walk. The goddess, offended by his noisy procession, sent down hail as hard as iron, and it killed them all. People in the villages believed this was who lay at the lake.

[measured] For decades, that was close to the scientific view as well. Studies dated some bones to around the 800s. In 2004 a team examined skulls with short, deep cracks on the top of the head and shoulders, and suggested they were struck from above by something round, the size of a cricket ball. It seemed the song might be literally true: one party, one storm. Local tourists had by then carried off many of the bones, and the site was badly disturbed, with no one left to say which body lay where.

[steady] Then came the study that broke the story. A team of researchers, led by Eadaoin Harney and with scientists from India, Germany and the United States, published their work in Nature Communications in 2019. They took DNA and carbon dates from thirty-eight of the dead.

[pause] There was not one group. There were three.

[thoughtful] The first group, twenty-three people, had ancestry typical of South Asia today. They were men and women, and they had not come as one family, since they were from different groups across the subcontinent. They died at various times from around the year 800. The second group, fourteen people, were a different thing altogether. Their DNA was closest to people in the eastern Mediterranean today, especially Crete and Greece. Chemical traces in their bones pointed to a different diet as well. And they died about a thousand years later, around 1800. The third group was one person, with ancestry linked to South East Asia, who died at about the same time as the Mediterranean group.

## theories

[measured] The hailstorm idea came from the people who studied the skulls in 2004, and its best evidence is the wounds. Its biggest problem is that it can cover one event at most. A single storm cannot have killed people who lived a thousand years apart. It may still be right for some of the first group, or for one of several disasters. The 2019 paper found no sign of a disease in the DNA, so an epidemic looks unlikely.

[steady] The pilgrimage idea, put forward by earlier Indian researchers, is the best explanation for the South Asian dead. These were adults, in good health, with no sign of fighting, on a known route. Accidents on such a walk would happen more than once, in different centuries. The older bones may be from several separate deaths, spread over time, as the first group's dates suggest. The problem is that no record tells us so.

[thoughtful] The Mediterranean group is the true puzzle. The paper's authors considered a few possibilities: a community settled for some time in the East, or travellers who joined a pilgrimage. But the first problem is plain: there is no record of Mediterranean people coming to this lake, and Roopkund was not on any trade route. Nor does Hindu pilgrimage draw many from there. The authors say plainly that it is unexplained, and that every idea so far is unproven.

[measured] The best supported view is that Roopkund was not one disaster at all. It was many deaths, at different times, in a cold place that keeps its dead, and some of them have no explanation yet.

## close

[thoughtful] What we know is who the dead were related to, how they ate, and that they came in at least two waves a thousand years apart. What we do not know is why people from the Mediterranean, around the year 1800, were on a high Himalayan path, or what stopped them.

[steady] Nobody has their names. The ranger's report is the earliest record we have, and the song is older still. Each summer the snow pulls back and the bones appear again at the edge of the cold water, the Greek and the Indian side by side, as if they had walked there together.
