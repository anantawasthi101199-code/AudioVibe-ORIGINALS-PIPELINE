## opening

[warmly] What actually happens to your lunch after you swallow it? Where does it go, how long does it take, and how much of it does your body really keep?

[curious] It's one of those things everyone does several times a day and almost nobody has ever had explained properly. And this is one of the best understood parts of the human body. Two hundred years of experiments, and modern scans and swallowable sensors, have told us a great deal.

[gently] So let's take one ordinary lunch, a cheese sandwich and an apple, and follow it all the way through.

## belief

[thoughtful] Most of us carry a rough picture of digestion. Food goes down into the stomach, sits there for a long while, slowly breaks down, and then whatever is left comes out the other end. [with a smile] Some people also believe that a big trip to the toilet means you absorbed less of your food, or that swallowed chewing gum stays inside you for seven years.

[warmly] That picture isn't silly. The stomach really is a bag where food is held for a while. And when you eat more, more does come out. For most of history, nobody could see inside a living person's gut, so a simple idea of a bag and a pipe was the best anyone had.

[curious] It took an accident, in 1822, for someone to finally look.

## evidence

[serious] In June 1822, on an island in the Great Lakes of North America, a young fur trader called Alexis St Martin was accidentally shot in the side with a musket, at very close range. An army surgeon, William Beaumont, treated him. St Martin survived. [with wonder] But the wound never fully closed. It healed into a small opening, straight through his skin and into his stomach. You could look inside.

[curious] Beaumont saw a chance no doctor had ever had. Over about ten years, he carried out more than two hundred experiments on St Martin. He tied small pieces of food to silk threads, lowered them into the stomach through the opening, and pulled them out every so often to see how far they had broken down. He collected the stomach's juice in jars and found that it could dissolve meat even outside the body, in a warm glass. [thoughtful] He showed that digestion was a chemical process, driven by a powerful acid juice, and not simply food rotting in a warm bag. He published it all in 1833, and it changed medicine.

[thoughtful] Beaumont also timed how long food stayed in the stomach. Most meals had left within a few hours. That was the first clear blow to the idea of food sitting there for a day.

[curious] Later, scientists found better ways to measure. Patients swallowed tiny pellets that show up on X-rays, and were scanned over several days to track them. More recently, people have swallowed capsules the size of a large vitamin pill, packed with sensors that measure pressure, temperature and acidity as they travel, and report back wirelessly.

[knowingly] These studies give a fairly consistent picture. In a healthy adult, the stomach usually empties in roughly two to five hours, depending on the meal: liquids faster, fatty food slower. The small intestine takes a few more hours. [slowly] And then the large intestine is where most of the time goes, often a day or more. Altogether, from eating to the toilet, it's typically somewhere between one and three days, and it varies a lot from person to person.

[curious] There's a famous claim that if you spread out the inside of your small intestine, it would cover a tennis court. In 2014, two Swedish researchers decided to check. They measured the intestines carefully, including all the tiny folds and finger-shaped bumps in the lining. [with a smile] Their answer was about thirty square metres. Roughly the size of a small flat, or half a badminton court. Big, but nowhere near a tennis court.

[thoughtful] And the idea that what comes out is mostly undigested food? When researchers analysed stool, they found it is about three quarters water. Of the solid part, a large share is bacteria, alive and dead, along with fibre the body cannot digest, and old cells from the gut lining. So the size of a trip to the toilet says more about fibre, water and gut bacteria than about how well you absorbed your lunch.

[softly] As for chewing gum: it isn't digested, but it moves through and comes out with everything else, in days, not years.

## mechanism

[warmly] So, the sandwich and the apple. Let's follow them.

[curious] In your mouth, your teeth do the first job, crushing food into small pieces so the chemicals can get at it. Saliva wets it, and starts digesting it straight away. It contains an enzyme, a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction, which begins breaking the starch in the bread into sugar. [lightly] That's why bread tastes slightly sweet if you chew it long enough.

[thoughtful] You swallow, and the food goes down your oesophagus, the muscular tube to your stomach. It doesn't fall. Waves of muscle squeeze it down, which is why you can swallow lying down, or even upside down.

[serious] The stomach is a stretchy, muscular bag. Its lining makes hydrochloric acid, strong enough to kill most germs in food, along with an enzyme that starts breaking down protein, like the cheese. The stomach walls churn and squeeze, mixing everything into a thick paste. Then a ring of muscle at the bottom, called the pylorus, lets that paste out a little at a time.

[building] Next comes the small intestine, the real centre of digestion. It's a narrow tube about six metres long, coiled up in your belly. As the paste enters, two more organs join in. The pancreas, a gland behind the stomach, squirts in enzymes that break down fats, proteins and carbohydrates. And the liver sends bile, stored in the gallbladder, which breaks fat into tiny droplets, the way washing-up liquid breaks up grease. [with wonder] The lining of the small intestine is covered in millions of tiny finger-like bumps called villi, and those are covered in even tinier ones. Through them, the broken-down sugars, proteins, fats, vitamins and minerals pass into your blood. This is where most of your lunch is actually absorbed.

[thoughtful] What's left, mostly water, fibre from the apple skin and bread, and things the body cannot digest, moves into the large intestine, or colon. It's wider and shorter, about one and a half metres. Here, the body pulls back water and salts. And here live trillions of bacteria, which feed on the fibre your own body could not break down, and make useful substances from it. [softly] Over a day or so, the remains become stool, and leave.

[warmly] Each station has a different job, and they cannot swap. The stomach cannot absorb your food, and the colon cannot digest a sandwich.

## disagreement

[thoughtful] On the basic journey, scientists broadly agree. Where they differ is in the details, and especially in what counts as normal.

[curious] Take how often you go to the toilet. Many people believe once a day is the rule. Doctors generally say normal ranges much more widely, from about three times a day to three times a week, as long as it is comfortable and regular for you. Some researchers argue that even that range is too broad, and that very slow transit in otherwise healthy people may matter for long-term health. Others say there isn't enough evidence yet to worry people about it.

[thoughtful] Transit time itself is hard to pin down. It changes with the meal, with sleep, with exercise, with stress and with hormones, and the methods give slightly different answers. So you'll find different numbers in different sources, all honestly measured.

[curious] And the gut bacteria are the newest argument of all. Researchers agree they matter a great deal for digestion. But exactly how much they shape the rest of our health, our weight, our mood and our immune system, is still being worked out, and there are bold claims out there that run well ahead of the evidence.

## honest

[warmly] So here is the honest answer. Your lunch is not a lump sitting in a bag. It goes through a line of specialised stations, each with its own job: crushing, acid and churning, chemical breakdown and absorption, and finally water and bacteria. Most of what you eat is absorbed within hours, in the small intestine, and the whole trip usually takes one to three days.

[thoughtful] What we don't fully know is how the bacteria in the last stretch affect the rest of the body, and what the healthiest pace for digestion really is for each person.

[serious] And one practical thing matters more than any of the numbers. Your own normal matters more than anyone else's. If that changes and stays changed, see a doctor. The NHS advises getting checked if you have blood in your stool, a lasting change in your bowel habits, losing weight without trying, or pain that keeps coming back. Those are not things to wait on.

## close

[warmly] So think back to that cheese sandwich and apple. A few seconds in your mouth. A few hours in your stomach. A few more in six metres of narrow tube, where nearly all of it slips into your blood. Then a slow day or so with the bacteria, before the rest moves on.

[softly] Two hundred years ago, a surgeon learned the first of this by lowering bits of food on silk threads into a hole in a man's side. Now you know it too.
