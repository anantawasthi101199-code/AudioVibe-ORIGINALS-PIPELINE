## hook
[warmly] You step out of a warm shower into a cold bathroom, and your arms are suddenly covered in tiny bumps. [curious] Later, the same thing happens when your favourite song hits the chorus. So what are goosebumps actually for?

## story
[thoughtful] The answer is at the root of every hair on your body. Each hair grows from a little pocket in the skin. Attached to the side of each one is a muscle, so small you can't see it, called the arrector pili, which means the hair raiser.

[measured] When that tiny muscle tightens, it pulls the hair upright, and it tugs the skin around it into a little bump. Thousands of them at once, and your arm looks like the skin of a plucked goose. That's where the name comes from.

[curious] But why would your body do that in the cold? [knowingly] For our very distant, furry ancestors, it worked. Think of a bird fluffing up its feathers on a winter morning. When fur stands up, it traps a thick layer of warm air next to the skin, like a duvet. We lost most of our fur long ago, but we kept the muscles. So back in that cold bathroom, your body is still trying to fluff up a coat you don't have.

[thoughtful] So why do they also come with fear, or a song? Because the muscles are switched on by the same system that gets you ready for danger, the one that releases adrenaline, the body's alarm chemical. In furry animals, fur standing on end makes them look bigger to an enemy. Picture a frightened cat, puffed up to twice its size. Strong emotion, including the thrill of music, can trip that same old switch.

[measured] There may be one more use. In 2020, a study in mice found that the nerve and muscle behind goosebumps also help to wake up the cells that grow new hair, especially in long cold spells. It's an early finding, in animals, but it suggests the system still does some work.

## verdict
[warmly] So why do we get goosebumps? Because tiny muscles lift each hair, a leftover from fur that once kept us warm and made us look bigger when frightened. [thoughtful] Goosebumps themselves are harmless. The NHS advises that if someone is cold and shivering and can't warm up, or has pale cold skin, slurred speech or confusion, it could be hypothermia, and to call 999 in an emergency. [softly] So the next time your arms prickle when that chorus hits, it's an old furry reflex, still doing its best.
