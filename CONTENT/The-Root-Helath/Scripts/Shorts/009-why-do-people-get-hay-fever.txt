## hook

[warmly] It's a beautiful June day. Everyone else is in the park, and you're sneezing for the twelfth time in a row, with itchy, streaming eyes and a nose like a tap. [curious] Pollen is just dust from plants. So why does your body react as if it's under attack?

## story

[thoughtful] The answer is a case of mistaken identity. Your immune system's job is to spot invaders, like viruses and bacteria, and fight them off. In people with hay fever, it makes a mistake. It decides that tiny grains of pollen, which do no harm at all, are a threat.

[curious] The first time it meets the pollen, nothing much happens. But the immune system makes antibodies, little markers made to recognise pollen. These settle on special cells in the lining of your nose and eyes. [knowingly] Think of them as smoke alarms, fitted all over the house, each one set to go off at the smell of pollen.

[serious] The next spring, the pollen arrives, and the alarms go off. The cells release a chemical called histamine. Histamine makes the tiny blood vessels leak, and the nose swells and runs. It irritates the nerves, so you itch and sneeze. Your eyes water to wash the pollen away. [thoughtful] It's a powerful defence, aimed at nothing that can hurt you.

[warmly] Which pollen sets you off depends on the season. Trees release theirs in spring, grasses in early summer, which is why that June day in the park is the worst for most people.

[thoughtful] Why some people's immune systems make this mistake and others don't isn't fully known. It runs in families, and it's more common in people with asthma or eczema. There's no way to prevent it for good, but the symptoms can be controlled well.

## verdict

[warmly] So why do you get hay fever? Because your immune system mistakes harmless pollen for an attacker, and the histamine it releases causes the sneezing and itching. [thoughtful] The NHS suggests antihistamine tablets or drops and steroid nasal sprays, which you can get from a pharmacist, plus a little Vaseline around your nostrils, wraparound sunglasses, and a shower after being outside. It advises seeing a GP if these don't help. [softly] So next June in the park, the alarms in your nose are only doing their job, at the wrong time.
