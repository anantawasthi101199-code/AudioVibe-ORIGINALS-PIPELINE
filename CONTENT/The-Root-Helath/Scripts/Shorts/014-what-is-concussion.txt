## hook

[warmly] A ball in the face, a clash of heads, or a slip on the ice. You're up in a few seconds, saying you're fine. Your head feels a bit foggy, though. Do you actually need to stop? And what has just happened in there?

## story

[measured] Your brain doesn't sit tight against your skull. It floats in a clear fluid, a little like jelly in a plastic box. If the box stops suddenly, or spins, the jelly carries on moving, and it hits the inside.

[curious] That sudden jolt is what concussion is, and it's a mild form of traumatic brain injury, an injury to the brain from a blow or a shake. The important part is that it often doesn't show on a scan. The brain's nerve cells are stretched rather than broken. For a while, they struggle. Chemicals rush in and out of the cells, and the brain has to work hard to restore the balance, at a time when the blood flow to it may have dropped.

[thoughtful] That's where the foggy feeling comes from. It's why people say they're seeing stars, or feel sick or dizzy, and find it hard to remember the score. Most people, despite what the films show, don't black out at all. So, when you stood up saying you were fine, your brain was already busy trying to get back in balance.

[measured] That energy gap is why timing matters. Studies in animals, and studies of athletes, suggest that a second knock, while the brain is still recovering, can make things worse and slow the recovery. In rare cases, mostly in young people, it can be very serious. Large reviews also show that total rest for weeks doesn't help. The experts who wrote the 2022 international consensus on sport concussion say a day or two of relative rest, then a gradual return to light activity, is best.

## verdict

[steady] Concussion is a real brain injury, even when you feel all right, and even if you never blacked out. The UK's guidance for grassroots sport puts it in six words: if in doubt, sit them out. That guidance says no return to the game that day, and no competition for at least twenty-one days.

[measured] The NHS says symptoms usually pass within two weeks. It suggests rest, no alcohol, and asking somebody to stay with you for the first twenty-four hours. It advises going to A&E after a head injury for repeated vomiting, a headache that keeps getting worse, a black-out, a fit, clear fluid from the nose or ears, or increasing drowsiness.

[warmly] So next time you're up in a few seconds, saying you're fine, remember the jelly in the box, and give it a chance to settle.
