## hook

[curious] Will reading in dim light ruin your eyes? [with a smile] It's what parents have told children for generations, usually when they caught them under the covers with a torch.

## story

[thoughtful] The idea makes sense. Reading in low light is hard work. Your eyes strain, they feel tired and dry, and you might get a headache. [warmly] But studies of eye health have found that this strain is temporary. Rest, and it goes away. There's no good evidence that dim light causes lasting damage.

[curious] Meanwhile, something else really is happening to children's eyes. Short sight, where faraway things look blurry, has become much more common around the world, especially in East Asia, where in some cities most young adults are now short-sighted. [serious] Researchers looked for the cause. Genes play a part. Lots of close-up work, like reading and screens, may play a part. [thoughtful] But the clearest finding was about time outdoors.

[warmly] In a large trial in Guangzhou, in China, some schools added an extra forty minutes of outdoor activity to every school day. Over three years, fewer of those children became short-sighted than in the schools that didn't. [curious] Scientists think bright daylight helps the eye grow to the right shape. Many studies point the same way.

## verdict

[warmly] So, does reading in dim light ruin your eyes? No. It can make them tired, but it doesn't cause lasting harm. [thoughtful] The NHS encourages children to spend time outdoors, and eye tests are free for children in the UK. If a child is struggling to see the board or squinting a lot, an optician is the person to ask.
