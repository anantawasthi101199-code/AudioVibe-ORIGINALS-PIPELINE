## hook

[warmly] You wake at three in the morning, shivering under the duvet, with your teeth chattering. The thermometer says thirty-nine degrees. You feel freezing, and you are burning up. Why would your body do this on purpose?

## story

[measured] In a healthy adult, the temperature inside the body stays near thirty-seven degrees. The setting is held by a small area at the base of the brain called the hypothalamus. Think of it as the thermostat on your wall at home.

[curious] When a virus or bacterium gets in, your immune cells, the body's defenders, release chemical messages into the blood. Those messages reach the hypothalamus, and they turn the thermostat up. So your body acts as if the room has gone cold. The blood vessels in your skin tighten, and your muscles shiver to make heat. That's why you feel so cold at three in the morning. Your temperature is rising, and your brain thinks it is still too low.

[thoughtful] So why turn it up? In 1975 an American scientist called Matthew Kluger studied desert iguanas. Cold-blooded animals warm themselves by moving to a hotter place. Infected iguanas moved to warmer spots, giving themselves a fever. Those that were kept cooler were more likely to die. Since then, laboratory work has shown that many immune cells work better at a higher temperature, and some germs grow more slowly. Studies in people are less clear. It's fair to say the fever looks like part of the defence, and that is less certain than the iguanas make it sound.

[measured] When the infection passes, the thermostat is turned back down. Now your body is too warm for the setting. So you sweat, and you throw off the duvet. That's the fever breaking.

## verdict

[steady] A fever is mostly your body fighting an infection, and it isn't the illness itself. The NHS says a high temperature in adults is usually thirty-eight degrees or over. It says most fevers ease in a few days with rest and plenty of fluids, and that paracetamol or ibuprofen can help you feel better, following the packet.

[measured] The NHS also lists what needs urgent help. Call 999 or go to A&E for a stiff neck with a rash that doesn't fade under a glass, trouble breathing, confusion, or a fit. A baby under three months with a temperature of thirty-eight or more needs a doctor the same day, and so does anyone whose fever lasts or who seems very unwell.

[warmly] So at three in the morning, under the duvet, those chattering teeth are your own thermostat, doing the job it was built for.
