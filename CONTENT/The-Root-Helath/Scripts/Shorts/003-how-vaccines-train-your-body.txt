## hook

[curious] A vaccine is a small injection, and then you get on with your day. So how does that one tiny dose protect you, sometimes for the rest of your life?

## story

[warmly] Your immune system is like a security team for your body. When a germ gets in, it has to work out what the germ looks like before it can fight it properly. That first time takes days, and while it's figuring it out, you get ill.

[thoughtful] But once it has beaten an infection, the immune system keeps something behind: memory cells. They remember exactly what that germ looked like. If it ever comes back, they recognise it at once, and the body makes the right weapons, called antibodies, fast enough to stop you getting ill. [knowingly] That's why you usually only get chickenpox once.

[curious] A vaccine uses that memory, without the danger. It shows the immune system a harmless preview of the germ: a weakened version, a killed version, just one piece of it, or a set of instructions so your own cells make that piece for a little while. Your body practises on it, and builds memory cells, without you catching the disease.

[thoughtful] For some germs, the memory fades over time, or the germ changes, which is why some vaccines need a booster, and the flu jab is offered every year.

[serious] And when enough people are protected, a germ struggles to spread, which helps protect people who can't be vaccinated, like newborn babies.

## verdict

[warmly] So, how do vaccines work? They give your immune system a safe practice run. [serious] The WHO and the NHS describe vaccines as one of the most effective ways to prevent serious disease. Side effects like a sore arm are common and mild, and serious ones are rare.
