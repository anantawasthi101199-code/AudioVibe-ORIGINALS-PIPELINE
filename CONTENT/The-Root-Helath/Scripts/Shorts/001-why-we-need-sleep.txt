## hook

[curious] You spend about a third of your life asleep. That's roughly twenty-five years. So what is all that sleep actually for?

## story

[warmly] In 1964, a seventeen-year-old in San Diego called Randy Gardner tried to stay awake for as long as he could, for a school science project. He made it eleven days. [thoughtful] By the end he couldn't concentrate, struggled to remember things, saw things that weren't there, and his speech slurred. Then he slept for about fourteen hours, and recovered. Nobody is allowed to repeat an experiment like that now.

[curious] So scientists have studied sleep more carefully. They found the brain does not switch off at night. It goes through cycles, from light sleep to deep sleep to dreaming sleep and back, several times a night. [knowingly] And during those cycles, it is busy. It replays and stores what you learned during the day, so memories stick. That's one reason a bad night makes it hard to remember things.

[with wonder] In 2013, researchers studying mice found something else. During sleep, the space between brain cells opened up, and fluid washed through, carrying away waste that builds up during the day. Think of it as the brain's night-time cleaning shift. [thoughtful] It was found in mice, and scientists are still working out exactly how it happens in people.

[warmly] Sleep also helps your immune system fight infections, keeps hunger hormones in balance, and lets your body repair itself.

## verdict

[warmly] So, why do we need sleep? For memory, for clearing out the brain, for the immune system and for repair. Sleep isn't wasted time. It's when your body and brain do jobs they can't do while you're awake. [softly] The NHS says most adults need between seven and nine hours a night.
