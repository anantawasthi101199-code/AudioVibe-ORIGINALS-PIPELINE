## hook

[warmly] It's two in the morning. You've got an early start, and you're still scrolling. The next day, everything feels harder. You forget a name, you snap at someone, and you want biscuits by ten o'clock. [curious] So what is sleep actually doing that one bad night can undo all that?

## story

[thoughtful] In 1964, a seventeen-year-old in San Diego called Randy Gardner tried to stay awake for as long as he could, for a school science project. He lasted eleven days. By the end he couldn't concentrate, struggled to remember things, saw things that weren't there, and his speech slurred. Then he slept for about fourteen hours, and recovered. Nobody would be allowed to repeat that today.

[curious] So scientists began watching sleeping brains instead. And they found the brain doesn't switch off at night. It works through cycles, light sleep, deep sleep and dreaming sleep, around four or five times a night. [knowingly] During deep sleep, the brain replays what happened during the day and files it away, a bit like saving a document before you close it. That's why, after a short night, the name you heard yesterday just isn't there.

[with wonder] Then, in 2013, a team led by Maiken Nedergaard found something else, in mice. During sleep, the gaps between brain cells widened, and fluid washed through, carrying away waste that had built up during the day. Think of it as a night-time cleaning shift. [thoughtful] That was in mice, and scientists are still working out how much the same happens in people. But it fits with why a tired brain feels foggy.

[warmly] And the biscuits? Large studies have found that short sleep shifts the hormones that control hunger. One that makes you feel hungry goes up, and one that tells you you're full goes down. [softly] So the snacking isn't weak willpower. It's your body running short of rest.

## verdict

[warmly] So why do we need sleep? Because it's when the brain files memories, clears itself out, and keeps hunger and mood in balance. [thoughtful] The NHS says most adults need between seven and nine hours a night, and suggests going to bed and getting up at the same times each day, and keeping screens out of the hour before bed. It advises seeing a GP if poor sleep goes on for three months or more. [softly] So at two in the morning, putting the phone down isn't losing time. It's giving tomorrow back to yourself.
