## hook
[warmly] It's mid-afternoon at your desk. You glance at the water bottle you filled this morning, and it's still almost full. [curious] Eight glasses a day, everyone says. So are you quietly drying out?

## story
[thoughtful] The eight-glasses rule seems to go back to 1945, in America. A national food board advised adults to take in about two and a half litres of water a day. [knowingly] But the very next sentence said most of that is already in the food we eat. That part was quietly forgotten, and the number stuck.

[curious] So where does all that water come from, if not the glass? About a fifth of it is in food. Fruit and vegetables are mostly water. So are soup, yoghurt, rice and pasta once they're cooked. [warmly] Then come drinks, and here's the surprise for most people: tea, coffee, milk and squash all count too. The small drying effect of the caffeine in a cup of tea is far smaller than the water in it.

[thoughtful] So back to that full bottle on your desk. If you've had a cup of tea, a glass of milk and a lunch with some fruit in it, you've had a fair amount already.

[measured] How does your body keep track? Deep in your brain, a small area works like a thermostat for water. When your blood gets a little too concentrated, it does two things. It makes you feel thirsty. And it tells your kidneys to hold on to water, so your pee gets darker. [knowingly] That's why the colour of your pee is such a useful clue. Pale straw means you're fine. Dark and strong-smelling means you're running low.

[thoughtful] The amount does change. You lose more on hot days, when you exercise, and when you're ill with a fever, sickness or diarrhoea. Older people can feel less thirsty than they should, so they can get low without noticing. For most healthy adults, though, large studies agree that thirst is a good guide.

## verdict
[warmly] So, do you need exactly eight glasses of water? No. Eight is a rough guide to all your drinks, and food counts too. [thoughtful] The NHS suggests six to eight cups or glasses of fluid a day, and water, lower-fat milk, and sugar-free drinks including tea and coffee all count. It advises drinking more in hot weather or when exercising, and speaking to a pharmacist or calling 111 if you feel dizzy, are peeing very little, or the signs of dehydration don't ease. [softly] So that bottle on your desk is fine to sip whenever you're thirsty, and the tea counts too.
