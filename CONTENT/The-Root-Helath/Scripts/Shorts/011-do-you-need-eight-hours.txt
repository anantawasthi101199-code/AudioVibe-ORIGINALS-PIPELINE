## hook
[warmly] Your alarm goes off. You do the maths: six and a half hours again. [curious] Everyone says eight hours is the magic number. So are you running on empty, or is eight hours just a number?

## story
[thoughtful] The neat idea of eight hours has an unusual history. In the 1800s, factory workers campaigning for shorter days used a slogan: eight hours of work, eight hours of rest, eight hours for what we will. [knowingly] It was about fair working hours. It sounded so tidy that it stuck as a rule for sleep too.

[measured] So what does the research say? In 2015, two large American sleep science societies brought experts together to look at all the studies they could find. They concluded that adults need seven hours or more, regularly, to stay healthy. Below that, over months and years, the studies link short sleep to more weight gain, higher blood pressure, more accidents and a lower mood.

[curious] But why seven or more, and not exactly eight? Because people differ. Sleep need is partly in your genes, a bit like height. Some people feel their best after seven hours. Others need nine. A very small number of people carry rare gene changes that let them thrive on about six, but they are far rarer than the people who think they are one of them.

[thoughtful] So back to that alarm. The question isn't really the number on the clock. It's how you feel the next day. Think of sleep like charging a phone. Each phone needs its own amount of time on the charger, and you find out by seeing whether the battery lasts the day.

[measured] The signs your battery is low are quite clear. You can't get up without hitting snooze several times. You need caffeine to get going. You feel sleepy in the afternoon, or doze off in front of the television, or snap at people more than usual.

## verdict
[warmly] So, do you really need eight hours? Not exactly. Most adults need between seven and nine, and the right amount is the one that leaves you alert all day. [thoughtful] The NHS says most adults need between six and nine hours, and suggests going to bed and getting up at the same times every day, winding down before bed and keeping the bedroom dark and quiet. It advises seeing a GP if poor sleep lasts for months or affects your daily life. [softly] So the next time that alarm goes, the number on the clock matters less than how you feel by the afternoon.
