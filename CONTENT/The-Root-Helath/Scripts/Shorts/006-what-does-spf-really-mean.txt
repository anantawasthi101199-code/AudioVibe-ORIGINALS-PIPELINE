## hook

[warmly] You're on the beach. You bought factor fifty, the strongest one in the shop, and you put some on in the morning. By the evening, your shoulders are pink and sore. [curious] So what does the number on the bottle actually mean?

## story

[thoughtful] SPF stands for sun protection factor, and the number comes from a lab test. Volunteers have a measured amount of sun cream put on a small patch of skin, which is then lit by a lamp that copies sunlight. The test compares how long the skin takes to start reddening with the cream and without it.

[curious] But that test only measures one kind of sunlight, UVB, the rays that mainly cause sunburn. There's another kind, UVA, which goes deeper into the skin. It ages skin, and like UVB, it raises the risk of skin cancer. [warmly] In the UK, that's what the star rating on the bottle is for, from one star up to five. A UVA logo in a circle means it meets the European standard.

[knowingly] And the numbers aren't as far apart as they look. Factor thirty blocks about ninety-seven per cent of UVB. Factor fifty blocks about ninety-eight. So fifty is a little better, not nearly twice as good.

[serious] Here's the real problem, and it's probably what happened on that beach. The lab test uses a thick layer, much thicker than most people put on. Studies have found that people usually apply between a quarter and a half of that amount, so they get far less protection than the number promises. [thoughtful] Then add the patches you missed, sweat, towelling off, and a swim. By the afternoon, there's very little left on your shoulders at all.

## verdict

[warmly] So is factor fifty almost twice as good as thirty? No. Both give strong protection, but only if you use enough and keep putting it on. [thoughtful] The NHS recommends at least factor thirty with at least four stars, about two teaspoons for your head, arms and neck, and two tablespoons for your whole body in a swimming costume. It suggests putting it on again often, including straight after swimming, and staying in the shade between eleven and three. [softly] So next time on the beach, it's less about the number on the bottle, and more about how much ends up on your shoulders.
