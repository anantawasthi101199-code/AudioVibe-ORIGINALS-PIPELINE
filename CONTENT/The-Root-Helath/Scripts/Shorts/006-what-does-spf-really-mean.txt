## hook

[curious] What does the number on your sun cream actually mean? [warmly] Is factor fifty really almost twice as good as factor thirty?

## story

[thoughtful] SPF stands for sun protection factor. The number comes from a lab test. Volunteers have a measured amount of sun cream put on a patch of skin, which is then exposed to a lamp copying sunlight. The number compares how long it takes their skin to redden with and without it.

[curious] But SPF only measures one kind of sunlight, UVB, the rays that mainly cause sunburn. There's another kind, UVA, that goes deeper into the skin, ages it and can also raise the risk of skin cancer. [warmly] In the UK, that's what the star rating on the bottle is for, from one star up to five, and a UVA logo in a circle shows it meets the European standard.

[thoughtful] And the numbers aren't as far apart as they look. Factor thirty blocks about ninety-seven per cent of UVB. Factor fifty blocks about ninety-eight. [serious] The bigger problem is how much we use. The lab test uses a thick layer. Studies have found that most people put on between a quarter and a half of that, so they get much less protection than the number on the bottle.

## verdict

[warmly] So, is factor fifty almost twice as good as thirty? No. Both give strong protection, if you use enough. [thoughtful] The NHS recommends at least factor thirty with at least four stars of UVA protection, about two teaspoons for your head, arms and neck, and putting it on again often, including after swimming.
