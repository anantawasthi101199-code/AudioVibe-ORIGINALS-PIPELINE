## hook

[warmly] You're at the start of a park run. Everyone around you is pulling a heel up to their bottom, or leaning against a tree to stretch their calves. You do the same, for about thirty seconds, because that's what you do. But does stretching before exercise actually stop you getting injured?

## story

[steady] For most of the twentieth century, coaches and PE teachers said yes. The idea made sense: a muscle that's stretched is longer and looser, so surely it's less likely to tear. So scientists set out to test it properly.

[thoughtful] One of the biggest tests was in Australia, in the late 1990s, with over one and a half thousand army recruits during their basic training. Half did a set of stretches before every exercise session. Half did their warm-up without them. Over twelve weeks of running and marching, the two groups had almost exactly the same number of injuries. Since then, reviews that combine many studies have come to much the same answer. Stretching on its own, before exercise, makes little or no difference to your chances of getting hurt.

[warmly] So why doesn't it work? Partly because most injuries don't happen to a muscle that was too short. They happen when a muscle, tendon or joint is asked to take more force than it's ready for. Think of your muscles like the springs in a car. Stretching makes a spring a bit longer. What protects it is a spring that's strong enough for the load. That's what the studies found, too. In a large review in 2014, programmes that built strength cut sports injuries by roughly two thirds, while stretching barely moved them.

[steady] So back to that park run. Holding a long stretch for a minute right before you set off can even make your muscles slightly less powerful for a little while. What does seem to help is a proper warm-up, the kind where you start gently and build up: a brisk walk, then a jog, then some leg swings and strides, so your muscles get warmer and your heart rate climbs. Football programmes built like that have been found to cut injuries in young players by around a third.

## verdict

[warmly] So stretching before exercise doesn't do much to prevent injury. A gradual warm-up helps a bit, and getting stronger helps a lot.

[steady] Stretching is still useful if you want to be more flexible, and many people enjoy it after exercise. The NHS advises warming up before you exercise and cooling down afterwards, and the UK's chief medical officers recommend activities that build muscle strength on at least two days a week. If an injury doesn't get better within a few weeks, a GP or physiotherapist is the person to ask.

[warmly] So next time you're at the start of the park run, swap the toe touch for a few minutes of jogging that slowly gets faster.
