## hook

[warmly] Your nan puts a heap of carrots on your plate. Eat those up, she says, and you'll be able to see in the dark. You've been stumbling to the bathroom at night ever since, and you still walk into the door. So were the carrots ever going to help?

## story

[measured] The idea got famous during the Second World War. In 1940, British night fighter pilots suddenly began to shoot down German bombers in the dark. One of them, John Cunningham, became known in the papers as Cat's Eyes. The press was told about the pilots' fine night vision, and that they ate lots of carrots.

[curious] A popular version says this was a deliberate cover story, to hide the real secret, which was radar fitted inside the planes. Historians differ over how much the carrot tale was aimed at the Germans. The British Ministry of Food certainly pushed it, for a separate reason. Sugar and other foods were rationed, carrots were plentiful, and it wanted people to eat them. It printed posters with a cartoon, Doctor Carrot, and it said carrots would help you see in the blackout.

[thoughtful] Now, here's where the truth comes in. Carrots really do hold something connected to your eyes. They're rich in beta-carotene, the orange pigment, which your body turns into vitamin A. At the back of your eye is the retina, a thin layer of cells that works like the film in a camera. To work in dim light, those cells need a pigment made from vitamin A. Without enough of it, the pigment runs out, and the first sign is often trouble seeing in the dark.

[measured] That's real. It's called night blindness, and the World Health Organization says it still affects many children and pregnant women in low-income countries, where vitamin A is scarce. In those people, more vitamin A truly restores night vision. But your body's tank has a limit. Once you have enough, more carrots don't improve your sight, in the same way that overfilling a car's tank doesn't make it go faster. So, when you walked into that door, it wasn't a shortage of carrots.

## verdict

[steady] Carrots won't give you night vision, unless you're short of vitamin A to begin with. Then they can help. In countries like the UK, that's uncommon.

[measured] The NHS says most people can get all the vitamin A they need from a varied diet, including cheese, eggs, oily fish, and yellow, red and green vegetables. It says adults need about seven hundred micrograms a day for men and six hundred for women. It also advises that very high amounts of the kind in liver and some supplements can be harmful, especially in pregnancy. And if your night vision gets worse, it suggests seeing an optician.

[warmly] Your nan was right that carrots are good for you. As for the bathroom door, leave a small light on.
