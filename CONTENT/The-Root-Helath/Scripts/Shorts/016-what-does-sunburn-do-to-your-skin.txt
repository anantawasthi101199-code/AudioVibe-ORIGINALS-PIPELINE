## hook

[warmly] You fell asleep in the garden on the first hot Saturday of the year. Now your shoulders are bright red, the shower stings, and by Wednesday they're peeling in little white flakes. What has actually happened to your skin?

## story

[steady] It starts with the sun's ultraviolet light, which you can't see or feel. There are two kinds that reach us: UVB, which mostly burns, and UVA, which goes deeper and ages the skin. When UVB light hits the outer layer of your skin, it gets right inside the cells, and it damages their DNA.

[thoughtful] So what's DNA got to do with a red shoulder? Every cell carries DNA as its instruction book, the recipe for how to be a skin cell. UV light causes tiny misprints in that recipe. Your cells are good at spotting misprints, and they try to repair them. But if a cell is too badly damaged, it does something rather noble. It switches itself off and dies, so that it can't copy the mistake.

[warmly] And that's where the redness comes from. When lots of cells are damaged at once, your skin sends out chemical alarm signals. Blood vessels nearby widen to bring in repair crews, so the skin goes red, hot and tender. That's why your shoulders felt fine in the garden, and only started to burn hours later, at their worst around the next day. The peeling, a few days on, is your skin shedding the layer of cells that died.

[steady] Here's the part that matters long after the sting has gone. A few damaged cells don't die, and don't repair properly either. They carry their misprints on. Over years, misprints can build up in the same cell, and that's how skin cancer can start. Cancer Research UK says that getting sunburnt just once every two years can triple your risk of melanoma, the most serious kind of skin cancer, compared with never burning. Large studies agree that sunburn, especially in childhood, raises the risk. Even a tan is a sign of damage: it's your skin making a dark pigment to shield the DNA from more harm.

## verdict

[warmly] So sunburn is damage to the DNA in your skin cells, and the red is your body's repair response, not just a bad tan.

[steady] The NHS suggests that if you're burnt, you get out of the sun, cool the skin with a cool shower or a damp towel, use an after-sun or aloe vera, drink plenty of water, and ask a pharmacist about painkillers. It says to call 111 or see a doctor if the burn blisters over a large area, or comes with a high temperature, chills, dizziness or sickness, or if a baby or young child is burnt. To prevent it, the NHS suggests sunscreen of at least SPF 30 with four or five stars for UVA protection, and staying in the shade between eleven and three in the British summer.

[warmly] So next time you're tempted to doze off on that first hot Saturday, take the deckchair into the shade.
