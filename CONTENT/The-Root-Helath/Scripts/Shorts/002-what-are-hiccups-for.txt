## hook

[curious] Hic. Everyone gets them. They come out of nowhere, and they won't stop. But do hiccups actually do anything?

## story

[warmly] A hiccup starts with the diaphragm, the big dome-shaped muscle under your lungs that pulls air in when you breathe. Something irritates the nerves that control it, and it suddenly twitches. [lightly] You gulp in a sharp breath. And a split second later, the flap at the top of your windpipe snaps shut. That snap is the hic.

[thoughtful] Common triggers are eating too fast, fizzy drinks, a very full stomach, a sudden change in temperature, or excitement.

[with wonder] Most bouts stop within minutes. But in 1922, an American farmer called Charles Osborne started hiccupping while lifting a pig, and didn't stop for sixty-eight years. He still married, had children, and lived a normal life.

[curious] So why do we have them? Nobody knows for sure. [knowingly] One idea is that hiccups are left over from our very distant ancestors. Tadpoles use a similar movement to push water over their gills while closing their airway. Another idea is that hiccups help babies, who hiccup a lot, even in the womb, to push swallowed air out of their stomachs while feeding.

[wry] As for remedies, holding your breath, sipping cold water, or being given a fright all might help, but there's little good evidence for any of them.

## verdict

[warmly] So, what are hiccups for? Probably nothing much in adults. They may be a leftover reflex. [serious] The NHS says most hiccups go away on their own within a few minutes, but see a doctor if they last more than two days.
