How to make the episodes work as audio
For your audio-first channel, I would use a consistent structure for most 12–15 minute episodes.

1. The hook
0:00–0:45
Start with a mystery, a shocking decision, a prophecy, or a dramatic moment from the story.




2. The world and characters
0:45–3:00
Explain who everyone is, what is at stake, and what the listener needs to know.




3. The main story
3:00–10:30
Narrate the events in sequence, with vivid descriptions, dialogue where appropriate, suspense, and clear transitions.




4. What the story means
10:30–13:30
Explain the symbolism, moral conflict, cultural importance, and major interpretations without pretending there is always one answer.




5. The closing connection
13:30–15:00
End with a memorable takeaway and, where appropriate, a natural teaser for the next episode.



These are approximate timings, not rigid requirements. A major battle might need more narration, while a philosophical story may need more explanation. Aim for roughly 1,600–2,000 spoken words, depending on your narration pace and pauses.
The chronology I would follow
There are two different kinds of chronology here, and keeping them separate will make the project much stronger.
- Narrative chronology: Follow events within an individual story. For example, the Ramayana should progress from Rama's birth through his exile, Sita's abduction, the search, the war, and the return.
- Mythological chronology: Use the broad traditional cosmic sequence: creation and cosmic cycles, divine lineages and ancient legends, the Ramayana's Treta Yuga setting, the Mahabharata's Dvapara Yuga setting, Krishna's departure, and Kali Yuga. These are traditional cosmological placements, not a universally agreed historical timeline.
The ten series above are arranged primarily for learning, rather than claiming that every story fits into one exact chronology.
A few rules that will protect the quality of the series
1. Name the source tradition. When useful, say whether a story comes from the Valmiki Ramayana, Mahabharata, Bhagavata Purana, Devi Mahatmya, or another source.
2. Distinguish versions. If a popular retelling differs from a source text, explain that briefly rather than blending them into one supposedly definitive account.
3. Do not reduce every story to a simple moral. Characters can be admirable and flawed at the same time. Those tensions are part of what makes the epics compelling.
4. Make the narration understandable without visuals. Explain what places look like, who is speaking, what a weapon or boon does, and why each event matters. Introduce Sanskrit names gradually.
5. Treat living beliefs respectfully. These are sacred narratives for many people, not just fantasy stories. You can explore difficult questions without dismissing religious interpretations.
