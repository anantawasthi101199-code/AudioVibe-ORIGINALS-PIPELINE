## opening

[warmly] This is the very first Norse myth: the story of what there was before anything existed, and how the world began in the meeting of ice and fire.

[curious] The Norse were the peoples of Scandinavia a thousand years ago and more, in what is now Norway, Sweden, Denmark and Iceland. The Vikings were among them. Their gods were Odin, Thor, Loki and many others, and their stories were told aloud for centuries, before Christian writers in Iceland finally wrote them down, in the twelve hundreds. Two collections matter most. The Poetic Edda, a set of old poems. And the Prose Edda, a handbook of the myths written by an Icelandic scholar called Snorri Sturluson.

[thoughtful] Snorri tells the beginning of everything in a wonderful way: as a story inside a story. So that is how we'll hear it today.

[gently] You'll meet a few names. Gylfi, a king of Sweden who goes looking for answers. Three mysterious figures on three thrones, who give them to him. And at the edge of everything, Surtr, the fire giant. That's all you need. This is the start of our journey through Norse mythology, and every story that comes later stands on this one. [softly] So let's go with Gylfi, and ask the oldest question there is.

## story

[warmly] Gylfi was a king in Sweden, long ago. He was clever and curious, and he had heard strange stories about a people called the Aesir, who seemed to have a kind of power no one else had. Everything went their way. Gylfi wanted to know their secret. Was it their own strength, or did the gods give it to them?

[slyly] So he disguised himself as an old wanderer, and gave himself a new name, Gangleri, which means something like the weary walker. And he set off to find them.

[with wonder] After a long road he came to a hall so tall he could barely see the top of it. Its roof was covered, not with tiles or turf, but with golden shields, laid one over another like the scales of a fish. At the door stood a man juggling seven knives at once, keeping them all in the air. He asked the traveller's name, and led him inside.

[hushed] Inside, the hall was full of people, feasting, drinking, playing games, and fighting. And at the far end, there were three thrones, one above another. On the lowest sat a figure called High. On the middle throne sat one called Just-as-High. And on the top throne sat one called Third.

[curious] High looked down at the weary traveller. You may stay, he said, and eat and drink with us. But you will only leave this hall safely if you are wiser than we are. [knowingly] So ask your questions.

[thoughtful] Gangleri began with the biggest question of all. What was there, he asked, at the very beginning, before anything existed?

[softly] High answered with the words of an old poem, the prophecy of a seeress. A seeress was a wise woman who could see the past and the future. And the poem goes something like this. In the beginning of time, there was no sand, and no sea, and no cool waves. There was no earth, and no sky above it. There was only a yawning gap. And grass grew nowhere.

[slowly] That yawning gap had a name. Ginnungagap. It means something like the mighty empty space. A gap that went down and down, with nothing in it at all. [pause] But it did not stand alone. On either side of it, there was something.

[ominous] To the north lay a world of cold, called Niflheim. A land of freezing mist and fog and darkness. In the middle of it bubbled a spring, and out of that spring flowed eleven icy rivers, dark and poisonous.

[intense] And to the south lay a world of fire, called Muspelheim. A world so hot and bright that nobody who did not belong there could ever enter it. It flickered and blazed with flames. And at its border, guarding it, sat a giant called Surtr, holding a sword made of fire. [low, steady] Gangleri would hear later that Surtr is waiting. At the very end of the world, he will ride out with his flaming sword, and burn everything. But that was far in the future. For now he simply sat at the edge of the fire, watching.

[curious] So there it was. Ice in the north. Fire in the south. And between them, the great empty gap. Gangleri waited for the rest.

[thoughtful] The icy rivers from the north, said Just-as-High, flowed a very long way from their spring. And as they flowed, the poison in them hardened, like the slag that comes out of a furnace, and became ice. When the ice stopped moving, the drizzle and mist that rose off it froze too, layer on layer, into frost. [slowly] And slowly, over ages, the frost filled the northern half of Ginnungagap. That side of the gap was heavy, cold and still, full of ice and gloom and howling wind.

[warmly] The southern side was different. Sparks and glowing embers flew out from the world of fire, and the air there was warm and bright.

[thoughtful] And the middle of the gap, said Third, was mild, like air on a windless day.

[building] Then came the moment that changed everything. Where the warm air from the south blew into the frost from the north, the ice began to thaw. It softened. It dripped. [with wonder] And the drops did not simply fall. Through the power of the heat that had melted them, they began to stir. To gather. To take on a shape.

[hushed] Out of the dripping ice, in the middle of the empty gap, a form was growing. Something huge. Something that looked like a person, lying on its back in the melting frost. [pause] The first living being in all the worlds.

## close

[softly] That being was a giant, called Ymir. He was the first of anything at all, older than the gods, older than the earth. And his story, and how the gods came from the same ice, is where the Norse myths go next.

[thoughtful] But think about where the Norse put the start of life. Not in a garden, or in light, but in the meeting of two harsh things. Ice from a dark, cold north. Fire from a burning south. Neither on its own could make anything. Only where they met, in the mild middle, did the frost begin to drip, and the drops begin to move.

[warmly] For people living through long Scandinavian winters, with ice on the fjords for half the year, that must have made a kind of sense. Life is what happens at the edge of the thaw.

[tender] Picture it. A yawning gap that goes down forever. Frost on one side, sparks on the other. And in the middle, where they touch, a single drop of meltwater, falling, and beginning, very slowly, to move.
