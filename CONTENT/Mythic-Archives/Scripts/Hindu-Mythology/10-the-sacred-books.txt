## opening

[warmly] Every story in this series comes from somewhere. Today, you'll hear where. [curious] Hindu stories come from a great family of sacred books, some of them more than three thousand years old, and many of them were never written down for most of that time.

[thoughtful] This is a journey from the oldest of them to the newest. The hymns called the Vedas. The teachings called the Upanishads. The two great epics. The books of old stories called the Puranas. And the songs of every region of India.

[gently] Along the way, you'll hear two short stories that the books tell about themselves. One about a father, a son and a bowl of salty water. And one about a sage, a god, and a broken tusk.

[softly] So let's start at the very beginning, with a sound, and hear it properly.

## story

[with quiet awe] The oldest of the books begins as sound. The Vedas are collections of hymns, verses sung to the gods, composed in Sanskrit, the ancient sacred language of India. Veda means knowledge. [thoughtful] Hindu tradition says no person wrote them. Great sages heard them, in deep meditation, as if listening to the universe, and then they recited what they had heard. So the Vedas are called shruti, which means that which is heard.

[curious] There are four Vedas. The oldest is the Rig Veda, more than a thousand hymns, to gods like Agni, the god of fire, and Indra, king of the gods, who brings the rain. Then the Sama Veda, the same kind of verses set to melody, for singing. The Yajur Veda, the words spoken by priests during rituals around the sacred fire. And the Atharva Veda, full of everyday prayers and charms, for healing, for love, for protection.

[thoughtful] For thousands of years, they were not written down at all. They were memorised, word for word, sound for sound. [with quiet awe] Boys training as priests learned them from a teacher by heart, over many years. They learned to chant each verse in several different patterns, forwards, in pairs, doubling back and forth, so that a single word dropped or changed would sound wrong straight away. [warmly] It's one of the most careful ways of preserving words ever invented, and families in India still chant the hymns that way today.

[curious] At the end of each Veda come the Upanishads. The name means sitting down near, because they are the teachings a student heard sitting close to his teacher. They ask the deepest questions. What am I, really? What is everything made of? [thoughtful] And their great answer is that the self inside each person, called atman, is one with the reality behind the whole universe, called Brahman. Here is how one Upanishad shows it.

[warmly] There was once a boy named Shvetaketu. At twelve, his father sent him away to study with a teacher. He came home at twenty-four, very pleased with himself, thinking he knew everything. [with a smile] His father, a wise man called Uddalaka, said: you seem very proud. Did you learn the thing that lets you know everything else? The boy had no idea what he meant.

[gently] So his father told him: put this lump of salt in a bowl of water tonight, and come to me in the morning. In the morning he said: bring me the salt. The boy looked. The salt was gone. [curious] Sip from the top, said his father. How does it taste? Salty. Sip from the middle. Salty. Sip from the bottom. Salty. [hushed] You cannot see it, said Uddalaka, but it is there, everywhere in the water. And the finest essence of everything, the thing the whole world is made of, is in you, just like that. That is reality. That is the self. [a beat] And you are that, Shvetaketu.

[thoughtful] After the Vedas came the second family of books, called smriti, that which is remembered, books of human authors, passed down and retold. The greatest are the two epics. The Ramayana tells of Prince Rama, his wife Sita and her rescue from a demon king. [curious] And the Mahabharata, one of the longest poems in the world, tells of a terrible war between two branches of one royal family, the Pandavas and the Kauravas, cousins fighting for a kingdom. In the middle of it is the Bhagavad Gita, the song of God, a conversation on a battlefield about how to live.

[warmly] The Mahabharata tells its own beginning. The sage Vyasa, who saw the whole story in his mind, needed someone to write it down. He asked Ganesha, the wise god with the head of an elephant. [knowingly] Ganesha agreed, on one condition. Vyasa must never stop speaking, not even for a moment. If he paused, Ganesha would put down his pen and leave.

[with a smile] Vyasa agreed, with a condition of his own. Ganesha must never write a single line without understanding it first. Then, whenever Vyasa needed time to think, he spoke a verse so twisted and difficult that even Ganesha had to stop and puzzle over it, and that gave the sage a moment to compose the next part. [tender] Partway through, Ganesha's pen broke. So he snapped off one of his own tusks, and kept writing with that. That is why he is shown with one broken tusk.

[thoughtful] Then come the Puranas, the old stories. There are eighteen great Puranas, and many smaller ones. They tell of the creation and destruction of the universe, of the gods and their families, of kings and sages and great battles between gods and demons. [warmly] Many put one god above the others. The Vishnu Purana praises Vishnu. The Shiva Purana praises Shiva. The Bhagavata Purana tells the life of Krishna, from a mischievous baby stealing butter to a prince and a teacher. Most of the stories people know best, today, come from the Puranas.

[curious] And then, the stories of every region. Poets in Tamil, in the far south, sang to Shiva and Vishnu in their own language more than a thousand years ago. There are retellings of the Ramayana in Tamil, Bengali, Hindi and dozens of other languages, each with its own flavour. [warmly] Every great temple has its own story, about the god who chose to live in that particular place. And grandmothers in villages tell versions you will not find in any book.

## close

[warmly] So when you hear a story in this series, it has come down that long river. A hymn heard by a sage in meditation. A teacher's lesson with a bowl of salt. Vyasa speaking without stopping, and Ganesha writing as fast as he could. [thoughtful] For the people who kept these books, the knowledge was too precious to lose, so they carried it in their memories, from teacher to student, for thousands of years.

[gently] And they still do. Somewhere in India this morning, a boy is sitting near his teacher, learning a hymn from the Rig Veda, one word at a time, chanted forwards and backwards, exactly the way it was chanted three thousand years ago.
