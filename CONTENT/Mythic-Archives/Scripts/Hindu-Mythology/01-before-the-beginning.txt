## opening

[warmly] This is the story of how everything began, as Hindu tradition tells it.

[curious] It starts with a question. One of the oldest hymns in the Rig Veda, the great collection of sacred songs that Hindus still chant today, looks all the way back to the very first moment. It asks what was there. And then it does something remarkable. [pause] [with quiet awe] It admits that maybe nobody knows.

[with wonder] Out of that question grows one of the most beautiful creation stories in the world. A dark ocean with no shore. A god asleep on a giant serpent, drifting on those waters. A lotus that rises out of his body, and inside the lotus, the one who will build the universe, piece by piece.

[gently] You'll meet three names today. Vishnu, the god asleep on the waters. Shesha, the endless serpent he rests on. And Brahma, the creator, who wakes up inside the lotus.

This is the very first episode of our journey through Hindu mythology, and everything that comes later stands on this one. [softly] So let's go back to the moment before the first moment, and hear it properly.

## story

[hushed] Picture darkness. Real darkness, the kind you get in a deep cave when the torch goes out. Now take away the cave. Take away the ground under your feet and the air around you. Take away up and down. That is where the hymn begins.

[softly] The hymn is called the Nasadiya Sukta. The name comes from its first words, which mean roughly "then there was neither being nor non-being." The poet is trying to describe the state of things before anything existed at all, and he does it the only way he can. There was no air yet, and no sky beyond it. There was no death, and so there was nothing immortal either, because immortal only means something once death exists. There was no sign to separate night from day. [slowly] Darkness was hidden inside darkness. And everything was water, deep water, with no light on it anywhere.

[hushed] Then the hymn gives us the first thing that actually is. It calls it simply That One. That One breathed, the poet says, without any wind, by its own power. Everything else was still waiting to happen. [pause] And then, inside That One, something stirred. The hymn calls it desire, the first seed of mind. A wanting. The very first movement in the whole of existence was a kind of longing to become something.

[thoughtful] The poet goes on. The wise, he says, searched in their own hearts, and there they found the thread that ties what exists to what does not. A cord was stretched across the dark. Above it and below it, the first powers began to take their places.

And then come the lines that have made this hymn famous for three thousand years. [slowly] Who really knows, the poet asks. Who can say where all this came from? Even the gods arrived after creation, so even they cannot tell us. The one who watches over it all from the highest heaven, he surely knows. [long pause] Or perhaps he does not know.

[reverently] That is how one of the holiest texts in Hinduism describes the beginning of everything. It ends with a question mark. And the people who sang it were perfectly comfortable with that. They placed the biggest question there is right at the heart of their sacred songs, and they left it open.

[thoughtful] Later storytellers took that dark water and gave it a picture. In the Puranas, the great books of old stories that grew up over many centuries after the Vedas, the same ocean appears. [warmly] And now somebody is floating on it.

[softly] He is Vishnu. Here, at the beginning, he has another name too, Narayana. In the Vishnu Purana the name is explained very simply. The waters are called nara, and ayana means a resting place. So Narayana is the one whose home is the waters. Before there is a world, there is Vishnu, lying on the endless ocean, and the ocean is his bed.

[awed] Picture him the way temple paintings across India still show him. His skin is dark blue, the colour of a rain cloud about to break. He has four arms. In his hands he holds a conch shell, a spinning disc, a mace and a lotus. He lies on his side, perfectly calm, his eyes closed. [softly] He is asleep. It is a special kind of sleep, called yoga nidra, a sleep that is also deep meditation. The whole of what will one day exist is resting inside him, folded up, the way a tree rests inside a seed.

[low, steady] And he is lying on a serpent. This is Shesha, sometimes called Ananta, which means "the endless." Shesha is vast. His body is coiled round and round on itself, coil upon coil, making a floating bed on the dark waters. And rising above Vishnu's head are his hoods. A thousand of them, the stories say, spread wide like a great umbrella, sheltering the sleeping god. Remember Shesha, because he matters later. When the universe has worlds in it, he is the one who holds them up.

[hushed] So there we are. Dark water in every direction. A blue god asleep on a coiled serpent. And total silence.

[building tension] Then the time for creation arrives. Out of Vishnu's navel, a stem begins to grow. It rises slowly through the dark, up and up, and at its top a bud forms. The bud opens. It becomes a lotus, enormous, golden, its petals glowing in the darkness like the first light the universe has ever seen.

[awed] And sitting in the middle of that lotus is Brahma.

[warmly] Brahma has four faces, one looking north, one south, one east, one west. The stories tell us why. When he first opened his eyes, he wanted to see what was around him, so he turned to look in every direction, and with each turn a new face appeared. And what did he see? Water. Only water, stretching out on all four sides, with no shore and no end.

[curious] Think about how strange that moment is. You wake up. You are sitting on a flower. You have no idea who you are, where you came from or what you are for. That is exactly how Brahma feels. In the Bhagavata Purana, another of the great story books, he decides to find out. He climbs down into the stalk of the lotus and follows it downwards through the dark water, looking for the place it grows from. He travels for what feels like an age, and he never reaches the bottom. Eventually he gives up and climbs back to his seat on the flower.

[hushed] [breathes in] Then he hears a voice. It speaks just one word, twice. Tapa. Tapa. It means heat, and it also means discipline, the inner heat you build up by sitting still and focusing your mind. So Brahma does exactly that. He sits in the lotus and meditates, for a very, very long time. And slowly, through that meditation, he begins to understand. He sees Vishnu lying beneath him, the source of the lotus and of himself. And he understands his job. He is here to make the world.

[thoughtful] Now, here is something lovely about how the Vishnu Purana tells what happens next. Brahma does not get it right the first time. Creating a universe turns out to be hard, even for a god.

[with a gentle smile] His first attempt produces darkness and confusion, a kind of heavy dullness. He is not happy with that. He tries again, and this time he creates the things that do not move: mountains, trees, plants and grasses. Better, but still not what he is looking for. Next come the animals, the creatures that move along the ground. Then the gods, beings of light and pleasure who live in the sky. Then human beings, who have a bit of everything in them, light and dark, effort and pain, and who are made to act. Each creation is a little closer to what the universe needs. You can almost hear him rolling up his sleeves.

[warmly] Then Brahma wants helpers. From his own mind he brings out four young sons, perfect and wise. Their names are Sanaka, Sanandana, Sanatana and Sanatkumara. Brahma asks them to help him fill the world with living things. [mischievously] And the four boys politely say no. They are only interested in meditation. They have no wish for families or worldly life at all. So they wander off to be holy, and they stay young forever.

[ominous] Brahma is furious. His anger is so strong that it bursts out of his forehead in a blaze, and out of that blaze comes a being. He is dark and fierce and shining, and he is crying out. Brahma names him Rudra, which can mean "the howler." This is a form of the god who will become one of the greatest in all of Hinduism. We will meet him properly in the next episode under his more famous name, Shiva.

[calmly] After that, Brahma takes a different approach. He brings forth more children from his mind, great sages who are happy to marry and have families of their own. From them come gods, demons, animals, serpents, birds and every kind of creature. And Brahma creates the first man, Manu, and the first woman, Shatarupa. Every human being, the stories say, is descended from them. In fact, one of the Sanskrit words for a human being, manava, means "child of Manu."

[lightly] There is just one problem. All these creatures need somewhere to stand. And the earth, at this point, is lying at the bottom of the ocean.

[building tension] So Vishnu acts again. In the Vishnu Purana, he takes the form of a gigantic boar, a wild pig as big as a mountain, and he dives into the waters. Down he goes into the depths, and there he finds the earth, sunk at the very bottom. He lifts her up on his great white tusk and carries her back to the surface. The sages watching from above sing his praises. And Vishnu sets the earth floating on top of the water, flat and steady, like an enormous ship.

[awed] Then the work of shaping begins. The mountains are put in their places. The land is divided into continents, with oceans in between. Rivers start to run. The sun, the moon and the stars take up their paths across the sky. Day and night begin to take turns. And the world fills up with life. [pause] The universe has begun.

[leaning in] But here is the part that surprises people hearing this story for the first time. The universe is only on loan. Brahma's working day, from waking to sleeping, lasts billions of human years, and when evening comes, he sleeps. When he sleeps, the worlds he made sink back into the dark water. Vishnu rests again on Shesha. Everything waits. And when Brahma wakes, the whole thing begins again. [softly] Remember that, because it matters later.

## close

[warmly] So Brahma sits on his lotus, Vishnu sleeps on the endless serpent, and the world floats on the water between them. That is where the story leaves them. The creator is at work. The preserver keeps watch underneath. And the universe they have made is young.

[thoughtful] For the people who told this story, it explained a lot. Why the world has order in it at all. Why humans carry light and darkness at the same time. And why everything seems to come round again, the seasons, the rains, the planting, each turn of the year. In this telling, creation itself works that way. It happens, it rests, and it happens again.

[tender] It also gave them permission to wonder. The oldest version of this story ends with the words "perhaps he does not know." Hindus have kept that line in their most sacred songs for three thousand years, and they still sing it today. A question was allowed to sit right at the centre of their faith.

[warmly] And the lotus is still everywhere. In temples and in paintings across India, Brahma still sits on it. It is a flower that grows up out of dark water and opens into light. [pause] It has been opening that way since the very first morning.
