## opening

[warmly] Today we meet the three great powers at the heart of Hindu mythology. Brahma, who creates the universe. Vishnu, who keeps it going. And Shiva, who brings it to an end, so that it can begin again.

Together they are called the Trimurti, which means "the three forms." Hindus have told stories about these three for well over a thousand years, and they still do, in temples and homes from Kathmandu to Bali.

First we'll get to know each of them properly: what they look like, where they live, and what they actually do. Then we'll hear one of the most famous stories ever told about them. It is a day when Brahma and Vishnu have a furious argument about which of them is greatest, and a column of fire appears out of nowhere to settle it.

By the end, you'll understand why one of these three has temples in every town in India, and another has barely a handful. [pause] So let's start with the creator, and hear it properly.

## story

In the last episode, we watched Brahma wake up inside a lotus, floating on the dark ocean at the start of time. The Vishnu Purana, one of the great story books of Hinduism, gives the bigger picture behind that moment. There is one supreme power behind everything, it says, and that power takes on three forms to do three different jobs. As Brahma, it creates. As Vishnu, it preserves. As Shiva, it destroys. [thoughtful] Think of it like the life of anything at all. A tree, a city, a person. It comes into being. It lasts for a while. And then it ends. The Trimurti is that pattern, given faces.

[warmly] Let's start with Brahma. Picture an old man with a long white beard, sitting calmly on a pink lotus. He has four faces, looking out to the four directions, and four arms. In his hands he holds a string of prayer beads, a water pot, a ladle used for pouring offerings into a sacred fire, and the Vedas, the oldest holy books, because all knowledge comes from him. When he travels, he rides a beautiful white swan, called a hamsa.

His wife is Saraswati, the goddess of learning, music and the arts. She dresses in white and plays the veena, a stringed instrument with a long neck and a round wooden body. That pairing makes perfect sense. Creation needs knowledge, and it needs imagination. Brahma lives in the highest of all the worlds, a heaven called Satyaloka, the world of truth.

[gently] Next, Vishnu. We met him floating on the cosmic ocean, but most of the time he lives in his own heaven, Vaikuntha, a place of perfect peace. His skin is blue, the deep blue of a storm cloud or the open sea. He has four arms holding a conch shell, a spinning disc with sharp edges called the Sudarshana chakra, a heavy mace and a lotus. He rides on Garuda, a giant eagle who is part bird and part man. And at his side is his wife, Lakshmi, the goddess of good fortune and wealth.

Vishnu's job is to keep the world running. He protects dharma, a word you'll hear a lot in this series. It means the right order of things: duty, justice, the way life ought to work. When dharma starts to fall apart, when demons take over heaven or cruel kings rule the earth, Vishnu comes down into the world in a new body to set things right. These descents are called avatars. He comes as a fish, a tortoise, a boar, a man-lion, and as the heroes Rama and Krishna. Every one of those is a story of its own, and we'll get to them.

[slowly] And then there is Shiva. Shiva is the one who stands apart from the other two. He lives far from any palace, high up on Mount Kailasa, a snowy peak in the Himalayas. He sits on a tiger skin in deep meditation. His body is smeared with white ash from the cremation grounds. His hair is long and matted and piled on top of his head, with a crescent moon tucked into it, and the holy river Ganga flowing out of it. A cobra coils around his neck. In the middle of his forehead is a third eye. When it opens, it can burn anything in the universe to ashes.

He carries a trident and a small drum shaped like an hourglass. His vehicle is a white bull named Nandi, who still sits outside almost every Shiva temple today, gazing patiently at the door. And his wife is the goddess Parvati, the daughter of the mountains.

[thoughtful] Shiva's job is destruction, and that sounds frightening at first. But in this way of seeing things, destruction is a kind of clearing. Old things end so that new things can grow. Winter ends, and spring arrives. A forest burns, and green shoots come up through the ash. Shiva is the one who makes that ending possible. So the three need one another. Without Brahma, nothing new. Without Vishnu, nothing lasts. Without Shiva, nothing ever makes room for what comes next.

[pause] So if all three are needed, which one is the greatest? That turns out to be a question the gods themselves once fought over. The story is told in the Shiva Purana, a great book of Shiva's legends.

[mischievously] It starts one day, after creation, when Brahma goes to visit Vishnu. He finds him resting on his serpent bed with Lakshmi at his side, and Vishnu doesn't get up to greet him. Brahma is offended. "I am the creator of everything," he says. "I made the world, and I made you. Show some respect." Vishnu opens his eyes, unbothered. "You were born from the lotus in my navel," he says. "I am your father. You should be the one bowing to me."

[building tension] The argument gets louder and angrier. Soon it turns into a real fight. The two gods take up their weapons, and the battle shakes the heavens. The other gods watch in terror, because if the creator and the preserver destroy each other, the whole universe goes with them.

[hushed] And then, right in the middle of the battlefield, between the two of them, something appears. A column of fire. It is blazing white and gold, and it is enormous. It rises straight up, higher than they can see, and straight down, deeper than they can see. It has no top and no bottom. It simply burns, silently, between them.

[in awe] Both gods stop and stare. Their weapons go quiet. Where did this come from? What is it? Then they have the same idea. Whoever can find the end of this pillar, they agree, must be the greater god.

[warmly] So Vishnu turns himself into a huge boar, the same form he once took to rescue the earth from the bottom of the ocean, and he starts to dig. Down he goes, tearing through the earth, through rock and darkness, following the pillar of fire downwards. Brahma turns himself into a swan, beats his white wings, and flies upwards, following the pillar into the sky.

They travel for thousands of years. Vishnu digs and digs. The pillar keeps going. Brahma flies higher and higher. The pillar keeps going. [slowly] Neither of them finds any end at all.

Finally Vishnu gives up. He climbs back to where he started, tired and humbled. He has found nothing, and he's honest about it.

[ominous] Brahma is less honest. Far up in the sky, as he's about to give up, he sees something floating down past him. It is a flower, a white ketaki flower, a kind of screwpine blossom with a strong, sweet scent. It has fallen from somewhere far above and has been drifting downwards for ages. Brahma has an idea. He asks the flower to come back with him and tell everyone that he reached the top of the pillar, and that he found the flower there. The flower agrees.

So Brahma flies back down with the ketaki flower and announces his victory. "I found the top," he says. "This flower was resting there. Ask her yourself." The flower confirms it.

[long pause] At that moment, the pillar of fire splits open. And out of it steps Shiva.

[reverently] The pillar was Shiva all along. It was his true form, with no beginning and no end, beyond the reach of either god. He turns first to Vishnu, and he is pleased with him. Vishnu told the truth, and so Shiva honours him as his equal, to be worshipped across the world.

Then he turns to Brahma. At this time, Brahma has five heads, the fifth one on top of the other four. It was that fifth head that spoke the lie. From Shiva's anger a fierce form called Bhairava appears, and with one stroke of his fingernail, he cuts the fifth head off. Brahma has had four heads ever since.

And Shiva pronounces a curse. Since Brahma lied to gain worship, he will barely be worshipped on earth. And the ketaki flower, who helped him lie, will never again be offered to Shiva.

[gently] Then the anger fades. Both gods bow to Shiva, and peace returns. The pillar of fire becomes the shape that Shiva is worshipped in to this day: the linga, a smooth, rounded stone pillar standing in the inner room of his temples, a reminder of the fire with no top and no bottom. In the town of Tiruvannamalai in South India, they still remember this story every year by lighting a huge fire on top of a sacred hill, which burns for days and can be seen for miles.

[thoughtful] And the curse holds. In all of India, there are very few temples to Brahma. The most famous is in Pushkar, in Rajasthan, beside a holy lake. Hindus honour Brahma as the creator, but the devotion, the festivals, the great temples, all go to Vishnu, to Shiva, and to the Goddess.

Now, you might notice this is a story where Shiva comes out on top. And that is because of who tells it. Over the centuries, Hinduism grew into several great family traditions, each devoted to one god as the supreme power. Vaishnavas worship Vishnu above all, and in their stories, Brahma is born from Vishnu's navel and Shiva himself comes from Vishnu. Shaivas worship Shiva above all, and they tell the pillar of fire. Shaktas worship the Goddess, Devi, and in their stories, she is the power behind all three, the energy that makes them move. [warmly] They are like people walking into the same great house through different doors. Each one describes the room from where they're standing.

And plenty of Hindus hold all three together. There is a beloved form called Harihara, a single figure who is Vishnu on one half and Shiva on the other, split right down the middle. One side blue, one side ash-white. One body, two gods, and no argument at all.

## close

So the pillar of fire closed, and Shiva stood there with the two gods bowing before him. Vishnu went back to his ocean, honoured for telling the truth. Brahma went back to his lotus, with four heads instead of five, and a creator's work still to do. And the three of them carried on doing their jobs, as they still do in this telling: making, keeping, and ending the world.

For the people who told it, the story answered a real question. Why is the creator of everything worshipped so little? Here was the reason. A lie, and a flower, and a curse. It also taught something they valued deeply. The two greatest gods fought over who was greatest, and the answer was bigger than both of them.

Every time a Shiva devotee leaves the ketaki flower out of the offerings, the story is still being told.

[softly] And every time you see a smooth stone linga in a temple, glistening with milk and water, you're looking at a column of fire. [pause] One that nobody, not even the gods, ever found the end of.
