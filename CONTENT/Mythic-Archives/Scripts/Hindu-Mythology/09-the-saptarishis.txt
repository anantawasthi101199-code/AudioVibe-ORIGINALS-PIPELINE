## opening

[warmly] Look up at the night sky in the north, and you'll see seven bright stars in the shape of a ladle, or a plough. In India, those seven stars are the Saptarishis, the seven great sages.

[curious] In Hindu tradition, the seven sages are among the oldest beings in the universe. They are the guardians of sacred knowledge. Each age of the world has its seven, and they carry the wisdom from one age to the next, so it is never lost.

[gently] Today you'll hear who they are, what their job is, and one story from the Mahabharata that shows what kind of people they were. There's a famine in it, a king's gold, a monster at a lake, and a very strange thief. [softly] So let's go back to the beginning, to where the sages came from, and hear it properly.

## story

[thoughtful] When Brahma, the creator, first began to make the world, he did not start with animals or people. He started with his mind. Out of his thoughts came his first sons, sages born from his mind, wise and holy from the moment they existed. [slowly] Their names were Marichi, Atri, Angiras, Pulastya, Pulaha, Kratu and Vasishtha, along with others. And they became the fathers of many of the races of the world, gods, humans and others.

[curious] Hindu tradition counts time in huge ages. Each one is ruled by its own Manu, the father of humankind for that age, and each has its own king of the gods. [matter-of-fact] And each has its own seven sages. When one great age ends and a new one begins, a new seven take their place.

[warmly] In our own age, the age of the Manu called Vaivasvata, the seven sages are these. Vasishtha, the calm and patient priest of kings. Kashyapa, the father of both the gods and the asuras. Atri, a great ascetic, a man of fierce discipline. Jamadagni, a sage famous for his fiery temper. Gautama, a teacher of deep learning. Vishvamitra, who was born a king and became a sage through sheer willpower. And Bharadvaja, a scholar who studied all his life. [softly] Seven names. You do not need to hold them all. Just know there are seven, and they travel together.

[thoughtful] Their job is to keep the Vedas, the oldest sacred hymns. Hindu tradition says the Vedas were never written by anyone. The sages heard them, in deep meditation, the way you might hear music in the silence. Then they recited them, and taught them to their students, who taught them to theirs, word for word, for thousands of years. [with quiet awe] The seven sages are the start of that chain. Without them, the knowledge would be lost.

[curious] Now, the story. It is told in the Mahabharata, and it begins with a terrible drought.

[grave] Once, there was no rain for twelve years. The crops died. The rivers dried up. People and animals were starving. And the seven sages, with Arundhati, the wife of Vasishtha, a holy woman as wise as any of them, wandered across the land looking for food, thin and weak, eating roots and leaves when they could find them.

[curious] A king named Vrishadarbha saw them. He was a powerful king, and he wanted the blessing of these famous holy men. So he offered them gifts. Food, cattle, villages, gold, anything they needed to survive. [serious] And the sages refused. Every one of them. Atri said: a gift from a king is sweet like honey at first, and it turns into poison. It ties you to him. We will starve before we take it.

[thoughtful] The king did not give up. He had his servants pick figs and fill them with gold, so that each fig was heavy, and leave them on the sages' path. [knowingly] But Atri picked one up, felt how heavy it was, and said, these are not figs. Put them down. And they walked on, hungry, and left the gold lying on the ground.

[darkly] Now the king was furious. He had been refused, twice, in public. So he lit a sacred fire, and out of it, through his anger, he made a monster, a fearsome demon woman named Yatudhani. And he sent her to kill the seven sages.

[curious] The sages found a lake full of lotus flowers. And lotus plants have long, crisp stalks that can be eaten. Food at last. [hushed] But Yatudhani was waiting beside the water. She said: no one eats from this lake without telling me his name, and what it means.

[thoughtful] One by one, the sages told her their names, and explained them, in long, clever riddles, full of word games. Each one so complicated she could not hold it in her head. [with a smile] Then it was the turn of a last traveller who had joined them on the road, a fat, cheerful wandering beggar called Shunahsakha. His name means friend of the dogs. He told her his name. She asked what it meant. [a beat] And instead of explaining, he hit her over the head with his walking stick, and she fell dead.

[warmly] So the sages went into the lake, and gathered a big pile of lotus stalks, and laid them on the bank. Then they went into the water to wash and pray before they ate. [pause] And when they came out, the lotus stalks were gone.

[serious] Someone had stolen the only food they had. And the thief had to be one of them. So each of the sages, and Arundhati, swore an oath: whoever stole the stalks, let this happen to him. [thoughtful] And each one wished on the thief the thing they thought was the worst fate in the world. To sell sacred knowledge for money. To serve a king. To grow rich and proud and forget what matters. To break a promise. To live a life of greed. For a holy person, these were the most terrible fates there were.

[with a smile] Then it was Shunahsakha's turn. He said: whoever stole the stalks, let him marry a lovely wife, and have fine children, and live in a comfortable house, and be rich and happy all his life.

[curious] The sages looked at each other. What kind of curse was that? [a beat] That is not a curse, they said. That is a blessing. You must be the thief.

[warmly] And he laughed and said yes. And he changed before their eyes. He was Indra, the king of the gods himself. He had stolen the stalks to test them. And they had passed.

## close

[warmly] Indra gave back the lotus stalks. He told the sages that because they would not sell their goodness, not for food, not for gold, not even to stay alive, they had won places among the stars forever. And he led them, with Arundhati, up into the sky.

[thoughtful] For the people who told this story, that was who the seven sages were. Not just wise men. People who would rather starve than be bought. That is why they could be trusted with the most precious knowledge in the world, and why every age needs seven like them.

[gently] And they are still up there. Look at the seven stars, and find the second star in the handle. Next to it, very faint, is a tiny star. That is Arundhati, by her husband's side. [softly] In many Indian weddings, the bride and groom are shown that little star, as a picture of a marriage that lasts.

[tender] Picture it one last time. Seven thin, hungry travellers, walking away from a pile of golden figs, and leaving them in the dust.
