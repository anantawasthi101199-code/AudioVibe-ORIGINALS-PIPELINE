## opening

[warmly] This is the story of how the universe ends, as Hindu tradition tells it. And it is told through one man, a sage who was there to see it.

[curious] It sounds like the darkest story there could be. The end of everything. But in Hindu mythology, an ending is never quite an ending. The universe is born, it lives, it dissolves, and then it is born again, over and over, like breathing in and breathing out. The word for the great dissolution is pralaya, which means melting away.

[gently] You'll meet four names today. Vishnu, the god who protects the universe. Shesha, the endless serpent he rests on. Brahma, the creator who builds each new world. And Markandeya, a holy man, a sage, who was blessed to live for an unimaginably long time, and who saw the end of the world with his own eyes.

[softly] We need no earlier story to follow this one. Everything you need, you'll hear as we go. So let's start with how long a universe lasts. And then let's watch it end.

## story

[thoughtful] First, the clock. Hindu tradition measures time in ages, four of them, from a golden age of truth down to the hardest age of all. Together those four ages make one great cycle, and that cycle lasts more than four million years. [slowly] Now take a thousand of those cycles, one after another. That is a single day in the life of Brahma, the creator. More than four billion years. When Brahma's day begins, he wakes and builds the worlds. When his day ends, the worlds he built come apart, and for a night just as long, there is nothing but darkness and water.

[with wonder] That is the ending this story is about. Not the final end, but the end of one day of the creator. And the old texts describe it very precisely, step by step.

[serious] It begins with a drought. A hundred years without rain. The rivers shrink to cracked mud. The fields turn to dust. Animals and people grow weak and lie down and do not get up. [darkly] Then Vishnu himself enters the sun, and drinks up every drop of water left in the world, from the oceans, the rivers, the wells and the bodies of every living thing. And the sun, full of all that water and all that power, splits into seven suns.

[intense] Seven suns, blazing in the sky at once. The heat is beyond anything. The mountains crack. The forests burn to ash in an instant. The earth glows red and then white, and becomes as smooth and bare as the back of a tortoise. [low, steady] From below, out of the underworlds, comes another fire, breathed out from the mouths of Shesha, the great serpent with a thousand heads, and it roars up through the lower worlds to meet the fire from the sky. The three worlds, earth, sky and the space between, burn together like one enormous furnace.

[hushed] And then the clouds come. Vast clouds, darker than any storm, some the colour of smoke, some like elephants, some like mountains. They cover the whole sky. And it starts to rain.

[slowly] It rains for a hundred years. Drops as thick as pillars. The rain puts out the fires, and keeps falling. It fills the valleys, then covers the hills, then rises over the highest mountains. At last there is no land anywhere, no sun, no moon, no stars. Only one dark ocean, still and endless, in a night that will last four billion years.

[tender] And on that ocean, Vishnu lies down on the coils of Shesha, and closes his eyes, and sleeps. Everything that has ever existed is folded up inside him, the way a seed holds a whole tree.

[thoughtful] And that is only the smaller ending. The texts describe a greater one too. Brahma, the creator, does not live forever either. He lives for a hundred of his own years, each made of those enormous days and nights. When his whole life is over, the dissolution goes all the way down, to the very stuff the world is made of.

[slowly] Hindu thought counts five elements: earth, water, fire, air, and space, the empty room that everything else sits in. At the great ending, they fold back into each other, one by one. [hushed] Earth loses its smell, and melts into water. Water loses its taste, and is swallowed by fire. Fire loses its light and its colour, and is blown out into air. Air loses its touch, and fades into space. And space itself, the last thing, loses even its sound, and dissolves into the first, unseen source that everything came out of. [softly] Nothing is left that has a name or a shape. Only that source, resting, before it all begins again with a new creator.

[gently] The texts even name a third kind of ending, a quiet one. It happens not to the universe, but to a single person, when they are set free from the cycle of being born and dying again. But that is the ending of a soul, and a story for another day.

[curious] Now, all of that is how the texts describe the end from far away. But one man saw it from inside. Markandeya.

[warmly] Markandeya was a sage, a holy man who spent his life in prayer. As a boy, he was fated to die at sixteen. But his devotion was so great that the gods protected him, and he was blessed to live on and on, through age after age. So when the end came, he was still alive. And for some reason that he did not understand, he was not burned, and he was not drowned. He found himself floating alone in that black water, after everything else had gone.

[hushed] Imagine it. No shore in any direction. No light. The water heavy and silent around him. He swam, and swam, for what felt like years, utterly alone, terrified and exhausted, with nothing to swim towards.

[building] And then, far off in the darkness, he saw something. A single tree, a banyan tree, rising out of the water. On one of its branches lay a leaf. And on that leaf lay a baby. [with wonder] A tiny boy, dark-skinned and glowing, lying perfectly calm on his back, with his big toe in his mouth, sucking it, the way babies do.

[curious] Markandeya swam closer. He could not understand it. Everything in the universe has been destroyed, he thought. How can a baby be lying here, happy, on a leaf? [pause] And as he came near, the baby breathed in. And Markandeya was sucked inside him.

## close

[with wonder] Inside the baby, Markandeya found the whole universe, unharmed. The mountains, the rivers, the oceans, the forests. Cities full of people. The sun and the moon and the stars. Every age of the world that had ever been, all safe, all whole, all held inside that small body. He wandered through it in amazement for a very long time.

[softly] Then the baby breathed out, and Markandeya found himself back in the dark water, beside the tree, beside the leaf. He knew now who the child was. It was Vishnu, the god who holds the universe safe even while it is dissolved. Nothing had really been lost. It was only waiting.

[thoughtful] For the people who told this story, that was the point. The universe does end, over and over. Fire takes it, and water covers it. But nothing that matters is ever truly destroyed. When the long night is over, a lotus will grow from Vishnu's navel, and Brahma will wake inside it, and he will build the worlds again, and the ages will begin once more.

[tender] Picture it one last time. A black ocean with no edge. A single tree. And on one leaf, a small child, smiling, with his toe in his mouth, holding the whole universe inside him while it sleeps.
