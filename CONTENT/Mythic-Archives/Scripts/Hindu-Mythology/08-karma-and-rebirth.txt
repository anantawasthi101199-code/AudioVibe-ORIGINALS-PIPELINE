## opening

[warmly] In Hindu tradition, when you die, you are born again. As a person, as an animal, sometimes as something else. And what you become depends on what you did. That idea is called karma, which simply means action. And the endless turning of birth, death and birth again is called samsara, the wandering.

[curious] Today you'll hear where that idea comes from, in some of the oldest stories in India, the Upanishads. These are ancient conversations between teachers and students about the deepest questions there are.

[gently] It starts with a proud young man who thinks he knows everything. His name is Shvetaketu. You'll meet his father too, a famous teacher, and a king who knows something neither of them does. [softly] So let's go back to the beginning, to the day Shvetaketu walked into the king's court, and hear it properly.

## story

[thoughtful] Shvetaketu was the son of a great teacher named Aruni, who was also called Gautama. When he was twelve, his father sent him away to study, as boys from priestly families did. He spent twelve years learning the Vedas, the sacred hymns, by heart. [with a smile] And he came home at twenty-four, very sure of himself, thinking he had learned everything worth knowing.

[curious] One day, Shvetaketu went to an assembly in the kingdom of Panchala, in the north of India. Wise men gathered there to debate. And the king of Panchala, a man named Pravahana, called him over.

[warmly] Young man, said the king, has your father taught you? [confidently] Yes, sir, said Shvetaketu. He has.

[thoughtful] Then tell me, said the king. When people die, where do they go? [a beat] Shvetaketu did not know. Do you know how they come back again? He did not know. Do you know where the two roads part, the road of the gods and the road of the fathers? He did not know. Do you know why the world over there never fills up, however many die? He did not know. [softly] And the king asked one more, a strange question about how the water poured in the fifth offering at the sacred fire comes to speak with a human voice. He did not know that either.

[grave] Shvetaketu went red. The king said gently, then how can you say you have been taught? A man who does not know these things is not educated. [hushed] Shvetaketu ran home to his father, angry and humiliated. You said you had taught me everything! The king asked me five questions, and I could not answer one.

[thoughtful] And his father Aruni was quiet for a moment. Then he said, my son, I do not know the answers either. If I knew them, I would have taught you. [warmly] And this is what makes Aruni a great teacher. He did not make an excuse. He got up and went to the king himself, as a student.

[curious] The king welcomed him with honour and offered him a gift, cows, gold, servants, whatever he wanted. Aruni said: I want only the answers to the questions you asked my son. [serious] The king hesitated. This knowledge, he said, has never been given to a priest before. Until now it has belonged only to kings and warriors. [a beat] But Aruni sat down before him as a humble student, and so the king agreed, and asked him to stay a while. Then he taught him.

[slowly] He started with the fire. The whole world, he said, is like a sacred fire, with five offerings. The sky above is a fire, and from it comes the moon. The rain cloud is a fire, and from it comes the rain. The earth is a fire, and from it comes food. A man is a fire, and from him comes seed. A woman is a fire, and from her comes a child. [softly] That is how the water of the fifth offering comes to speak with a human voice. It has become a baby.

[hushed] Then the king told him where people go when they die. They are placed on the funeral fire. And from there, there are two roads.

[with quiet awe] The first is the road of the gods. Those who live in the forest, in faith and truth, who know what I have just told you, go into the flame. From the flame into the day, from the day into the bright half of the month, when the moon is growing. From there into the light of the sun, and from the sun into lightning. And from lightning, a being who is not human leads them to the highest of all, to Brahman, the one reality behind everything. [softly] And they do not come back.

[thoughtful] The second road is the road of the fathers, the ancestors. Those who live in the village, who do good works and give gifts and make offerings, go into the smoke. From the smoke into the night, from the night into the dark half of the month, when the moon is shrinking, and from there to the moon. [slowly] They stay on the moon as long as their good deeds last, like a lamp burning as long as there is oil. Then they come back down the same way. They become space, then wind, then smoke, then mist, then a cloud, then rain. The rain falls on the earth and they become rice and barley, herbs and trees, sesame and beans. [softly] It is very hard to get out of there, the king said. Only when someone eats that food and has a child, does the soul become a person again.

[serious] And here is the heart of it. The king said: those whose conduct has been good will quickly enter a good birth. Those whose conduct has been bad will quickly enter a bad birth, even as an animal. [grave] And there is a third place, for those who take neither road. They become tiny creatures, born and dying over and over again. Live and die. That is why the world over there never fills up.

[thoughtful] Aruni went home and taught his son. And the idea spread from teacher to student. Another great teacher in the same old books, a sage named Yajnavalkya, put it in words people still remember. He was teaching a king named Janaka. As a person acts, he said, so he becomes. Do good, and you become good. Do harm, and you become harmful.

[with quiet awe] And Yajnavalkya gave a picture of the moment of death. Think of a caterpillar, crawling to the very end of a blade of grass. Before it lets go, it reaches out and takes hold of the next blade, and pulls itself across. [softly] That is how the self leaves one body. It reaches for the next one, and moves on.

## close

[warmly] So Shvetaketu's five questions were finally answered. Not by him, but by his father, who was humble enough to admit he did not know, and went to learn.

[thoughtful] For the people who told this story, it explained the whole of life. Why one person is born in a palace and another in a hut. Why good actions matter, even if no one sees them. And why there was hope: the soul does not end. It keeps going, life after life, carrying what it has done with it.

[gently] Centuries later, the Bhagavad Gita, another sacred text, gave the same idea one of its most famous images. Just as a person takes off worn-out clothes and puts on new ones, the self lets go of a worn-out body, and takes on a new one.

[tender] Picture it one last time. A tiny caterpillar at the very tip of a blade of grass. It reaches out into the air, finds the next blade, and crosses over.
