## opening

[warmly] Today we're going on a journey through the whole universe, as Hindu tradition maps it. All of it, from top to bottom.

[with wonder] In this picture of the cosmos, our world sits in the middle of a great stack. Above us rise seven worlds, climbing higher and higher, all the way up to the home of Brahma the creator. Below us sink seven more, deep underground, glowing with jewels, home to serpent kings and exiled giants. And under all of it lies something holding the entire universe up.

[warmly] We'll travel through every level, and I'll tell you what it looks like and who lives there. It is a map that Hindus have drawn and painted and sung about for centuries, and you'll find echoes of it in temples to this day.

[with a gentle smile] Our guide will be a sage called Narada, a wanderer who can travel between all the worlds. And at the centre of the whole map stands a golden mountain called Meru. [pause] So let's begin where we're standing, on the earth, and hear it properly.

## story

[warmly] Let's meet our guide first. Narada is one of the sons Brahma brought out of his own mind at the beginning of time. He is a sage, and he is always on the move, wandering from world to world, carrying a veena, a long stringed instrument, and singing the name of Vishnu as he goes. He visits the gods in heaven, the kings of the earth and the beings in the depths below. He brings news, he carries gossip, and he quite often stirs up trouble on purpose, because trouble has a way of moving a story along. If anybody has seen every corner of this universe, it is Narada.

[lightly] So let's start where people live. The earth.

[slowly] Picture the earth as the Vishnu Purana describes it. It is flat, and round, and enormous. At its very centre is a huge island continent, shaped like a circle. Its name is Jambudvipa, which means "the island of the rose-apple tree." Around it, in a ring, lies an ocean of salt water. Beyond that ocean is another ring of land, a second continent. Beyond that, another ocean. And so it goes, seven rings of land and seven rings of sea, each one wider than the last, like the ripples spreading out when you drop a stone in a pond.

[mischievously] And the oceans are not all water. The first is salt water, like ours. The second is an ocean of sugarcane juice. The third is wine. The fourth is ghee, melted butter. The fifth is curds, a kind of yogurt. The sixth is milk. And the seventh, the outermost, is sweet, fresh water. Beyond the last ocean is a land made of gold, where nobody lives. And beyond that rises a great ring of mountains called Lokaloka, "the world and no-world." On one side is light. On the other is darkness, stretching on forever.

[awed] Now come back to the centre, to Jambudvipa. In the middle of it rises Mount Meru. Meru is made of gold. It is unimaginably tall, eighty-four thousand yojanas, the old texts say, and a yojana is somewhere around eight or nine miles. It is wider at the top than at the bottom, shaped like the seed cup in the middle of a lotus flower. On its summit is the city of Brahma, and around it are the cities of the gods. Meru is the axis of the world. The sun, the moon and the stars all circle around it.

[tender] Down the sides of Meru flows the holy river Ganga. She falls from heaven onto the summit, circles the city of Brahma, and then divides into four great rivers that flow out in the four directions. And on the slopes grows the giant rose-apple tree that gives the continent its name. Its fruits are as big as elephants. When they ripen and fall, they burst on the mountainside, and their juice runs down to form a whole river.

[thoughtful] To the south of Meru, past the Himalaya mountains, lies a land called Bharata. That is the old name for India, and it is still India's official name today. And the Vishnu Purana says something special about Bharata. It is the land of action. In the other lands of the earth, beings simply enjoy the results of what they did before. Only in Bharata do people make the choices that decide their future. It is the one place in the universe where you can earn your way to heaven, or work your way to freedom. Even the gods, the text says, sing about how lucky people are to be born there.

[warmly] So that is the earth, the first world, called Bhuloka. Now let's go up.

[slowly] Above the earth, between the ground and the sun, is the second world, Bhuvarloka. This is the sky. It's the home of the air, of clouds and wind, where sages with special powers fly, and where spirits drift between earth and heaven.

[awed] Above that, from the sun all the way up to the Pole Star, is the third world, Svarloka. This is heaven, the home of the gods, Indra's glittering city with its wish-granting trees. The sun and the moon and the planets travel through this world, each in its own chariot.

[softly] And at the very top of Svarloka, fixed and still, is the Pole Star. Its name is Dhruva, and there's a story behind it. Dhruva was once a little prince, a five-year-old boy who was pushed off his father's lap by his stepmother and told he would never sit on a throne. He walked off into the forest and meditated on Vishnu, with such fierce devotion that the whole universe began to shake. Vishnu appeared and gave him a gift. A place higher than any throne. Dhruva became the Pole Star, and all the other stars are tied to him by cords of wind, wheeling around him every night while he stays perfectly still.

[thoughtful] These first three, earth, sky and heaven, are called the three worlds. Remember them, because they matter later. They are the worlds that end. When Brahma's long day finishes and he goes to sleep, these three are burned up and flooded and dissolved, and remade when he wakes.

[curious] So what lies above them, out of reach of that ending?

[awed] Four more worlds, each one higher and finer than the last. The fourth is Maharloka. It is ten million yojanas above the Pole Star, and it is home to great sages who live for an entire day of Brahma. When the fire comes at the end and the three worlds below begin to burn, the heat rises so high that even these sages move up, to the world above.

[warmly] The fifth is Janaloka. Here live the four boy sages who refused to have families when Brahma first asked, the ones who wanted only to meditate. They are still there, still young, still meditating.

[hushed] The sixth is Tapoloka, the world of austerity, home to beings who have burned away almost everything that ties them to the world below.

[slowly] And the seventh, at the very top, is Satyaloka, the world of truth. This is where Brahma lives. The souls who reach this world never fall back down. They are never born again.

[pause] So that is the way up. Now let's go down.

[hushed] Under the earth, one beneath another, lie seven more worlds. Together they are called the Patalas, the lower regions. Each one, the Vishnu Purana says, is ten thousand yojanas deep. Their names are Atala, Vitala, Nitala, Gabhastimat, Mahatala, Sutala, and the lowest, Patala itself. And each one has its own ground. One is white, one black, one purple, one yellow. One is sandy, one is rocky, and the last is made of gold.

You might expect these to be dark and grim places. They are the opposite. [warmly] The people who live down here are the Daityas and Danavas, the asuras, the gods' great rivals, along with Yakshas, and the great Nagas, the serpent people. Nagas can look like enormous cobras, or like handsome men and women with serpent hoods rising behind their heads. And on their hoods they wear jewels. Those jewels give out so much light that the underworlds are always bright.

[softly] The sun shines here too, the text says, but it gives light without heat. The moon gives light without cold. There are lakes filled with lotus flowers, forests and gardens, and the sound of music and flutes and drums. The people wear fine clothes and beautiful jewellery, and they eat and drink and enjoy themselves without a care.

[amused] And here is where our guide comes in. Narada once travelled down through all seven of these worlds. When he came back up to heaven, he gathered the gods around him, and he told them something they did not want to hear. Patala, he said, was far more delightful than Indra's heaven. You can imagine the faces around the table.

[thoughtful] One of these underworlds, Sutala, is ruled by a great asura king named Bali, who was sent down there in one of the most famous stories of Vishnu. Remember him, because we'll meet him properly in a later episode.

[slowly] Now go below the lowest of the seven, below Patala itself. And there, deep beneath everything, lies a serpent we've already met. Shesha. The endless one. The same great serpent who makes a bed for Vishnu on the cosmic ocean. Here, he has a second job. On his thousand hoods, he holds up the entire earth, and all the worlds that rest on it. The Vishnu Purana says he carries the whole world like a crown on his head. His eyes are red, he wears a dark blue robe, and garlands of white flowers lie across his hoods. [hushed] And when Shesha yawns, the earth shakes.

[darkly] And there is one more place to visit. Beneath the earth and the waters, far below, are the hells. They are called the Narakas. The Vishnu Purana lists many of them, each with its own name and its own punishment, for liars, for thieves, for those who betray a friend or harm the innocent. Yama, the king of the dead, sends souls there according to what they did in life.

[gently] But here is the important thing. The hells are not forever. Neither are the heavens. A soul goes to heaven to enjoy the reward of its good deeds, or to hell to work off the weight of its bad ones. And when that's done, it comes back. It is born again on earth to try once more. The only permanent way out is to go beyond the whole system, all the way up to the top, or beyond the worlds altogether.

[warmly] So there it is, the whole map. Fourteen worlds stacked one above another. Seven rising, with the earth at the bottom of them. Seven sinking, glowing with jewels. Golden Meru at the centre, the sun and stars wheeling around it. Shesha holding it all up from below. And Brahma watching over it from the top.

## close

[warmly] So Narada carried on wandering, up and down the fourteen worlds with his veena, singing Vishnu's name, telling the gods that the underworld was nicer than their heaven. Shesha kept holding up the earth on his thousand hoods. Dhruva stayed fixed at the top of the sky. And the souls carried on moving, up to the heavens and down to the hells and back to earth again.

[thoughtful] For the people who told this, the map explained a lot. Why the stars turn around one fixed point in the night sky. Why the earth sometimes shakes. Where the gods live, and where the dead go. And it put their own land, Bharata, right at the centre of the action. This was where the choices were made. Everything else in the universe was a place to rest, or to pay, or to wait.

[tender] It also gave them comfort. Even the worst place in the map is a stop along the way, and nobody stays there forever.

[softly] Go outside on a clear night in the northern half of the world and find the Pole Star. Watch for a while, and the other stars slowly wheel around it. [pause] That is a little prince, still sitting perfectly still, right where Vishnu put him.
