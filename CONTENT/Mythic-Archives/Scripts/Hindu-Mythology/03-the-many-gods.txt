## opening

[warmly] Hindu mythology is famous for having a lot of gods. Gods of fire and wind, of the sun and the moon, of rain and wealth and death. Gods with elephants and gods with buffaloes. Musicians, dancers, treasure guardians, and an entire race of powerful beings who are the gods' rivals.

[leaning in] Today we meet that whole cast. We'll find out who they are, what each one does, and why they so often find themselves at war. And we'll do it the way the old storytellers did, through the family they all belong to, and through the most famous battle of the oldest hymns.

[slowly] Keep three names in mind. Indra, the king of the gods. Kashyapa, the sage who is father to almost all of them. And Vritra, a monstrous serpent who holds the whole world to ransom.

By the end, you'll know your way around the gods' court as well as any temple storyteller would. [pause] So let's start with the family, and hear it properly.

## story

In the first episode, we watched Brahma create the world, and then bring out wise sons from his own mind to help fill it with life. One of those sons, the sage Marichi, had a son called Kashyapa. That makes Kashyapa Brahma's grandson. Kashyapa was a great sage, a rishi, and if Hindu mythology has a family tree, he sits right at its trunk.

[warmly] Kashyapa married many sisters, all daughters of another of Brahma's sons, a powerful figure named Daksha. And from those wives came nearly every kind of being in the universe. One wife gave birth to the birds. Another to the serpents. Another to the cattle, another to the trees. But three of the sisters matter most for our story today, because their children are the gods and the gods' rivals.

[tender] The first is Aditi. Her name means "boundless," and she is a mother of light. Her sons are the Adityas, the shining ones, and they become the devas, the gods of heaven. The word deva comes from a root that means "to shine." So when you hear devas, think of beings made of light.

[low, steady] The second is Diti, Aditi's sister. Her sons are the Daityas. The third sister, Danu, has sons called the Danavas. Together, these two families are known as the asuras.

[thoughtful] So here is the first thing to understand. The gods and their great enemies are half-brothers. They have the same father, and their mothers are sisters. Their wars are family feuds, about inheritance and power and who gets to rule. Asura, in fact, once simply meant "mighty lord." Asuras are clever, strong, often devout, and they can be every bit as impressive as the devas. What sets the two sides apart is who rules heaven, and how they use their power.

[pause] [curious] So who rules heaven? [warmly] That would be Indra.

[warmly] Indra is the king of the devas. His heaven is called Svarga, and its capital is a glittering city called Amaravati, the city of the immortals. Picture gardens full of trees that grant any wish, palaces of gold and jewels, music drifting through the air. Indra sits on a throne in his great hall, and when he rides out, he rides on Airavata, a magnificent white elephant. In many pictures, Airavata has more than one trunk and more than two tusks, as white as clouds.

[proudly] Indra is the god of storms, of thunder, and of the rain. His weapon is the vajra, the thunderbolt, a short weapon shaped like a double-ended club, crackling with lightning. And Indra loves soma. Soma is a sacred drink, pressed from a plant and offered in the old rituals. It gives the gods their strength and their joy. Indra drinks a great deal of it.

[ominous] Now for the battle that made Indra's name. It is told in the Rig Veda, the oldest collection of Hindu hymns, and it was sung with real excitement, the way you'd sing about a hero who saved your village.

[hushed] Long ago, there was a terrible creature called Vritra. Vritra was a serpent, a dragon really, and his name means "the one who holds back." He crawled up onto a great mountain and coiled his body around it, and inside his coils, he trapped all the waters of the world. The rivers. The rain clouds. Every drop.

[slowly] Imagine the world as it was then. No rain falls. The rivers dry up into cracked mud. The fields turn brown and the cattle grow thin. People look up at the sky, day after day, and see nothing but heat. And there on the mountain lies Vritra, huge and heavy, curled around everything the world needs to stay alive.

[building tension] The gods are afraid of him. But Indra decides to fight. He drinks three great bowls of soma, and he feels his strength swell until it fills heaven and earth. He takes up the thunderbolt, made for him by the gods' craftsman. And he goes to the mountain.

[intense] The battle is ferocious. Vritra has no hands and no feet, the hymn says, but he fights with everything he has, with mist and lightning and hail. Indra strikes again and again. And finally, he brings the thunderbolt down right between Vritra's shoulders, and the great serpent splits apart.

[awed] And the waters come free. The hymn describes it beautifully. The waters rush down from the mountain like cows let out of a pen, lowing as they run to the sea. Rain pours out of the sky. The rivers fill and flood across the plains. Life comes back to the earth. And Indra stands on the mountain, the god who released the waters.

[thoughtful] Every year, the people who sang this hymn waited through the burning summer for the monsoon, the season when the rains finally break. You can imagine how this story felt to them, sung while they watched the clouds build up on the horizon.

[warmly] Indra doesn't rule alone. Around his throne sit the other great devas, and each one has a job.

[warmly] There is Agni, the god of fire. He is pictured with red skin and flames for hair, sometimes with two faces, riding a ram. Agni matters enormously, because he is the messenger between people and the gods. When anybody pours butter or grain into a sacred fire, Agni carries that offering up to heaven. He is in every hearth, every lamp, every funeral pyre. He is the one god who lives right inside people's homes.

[brightly] There is Surya, the sun, who drives across the sky every day in a golden chariot pulled by seven horses. There is Vayu, the wind, swift and restless, who is also the father of the monkey hero Hanuman. There is Chandra, the moon, who shares his name with Soma, the sacred drink, because the moon was thought of as a cup of it, emptied and refilled every month.

[serious] There is Varuna, the lord of the waters and of the oceans. In the oldest days, Varuna was the great keeper of cosmic order, watching over oaths and promises. He holds a noose for binding those who break their word, and he rides a sea creature called a makara, part crocodile and part fish.

[softly] And there is Yama, the god of death. Yama was the very first mortal ever to die. He went ahead along the path that nobody had walked before, and he found the way to the land of the dead. And because he got there first, he became its king. Yama has green or dark skin, rides a black water buffalo, and carries a noose for collecting souls when their time comes. He is stern, but he is fair. He judges each soul by its deeds.

[mischievously] Then there is Kubera, the god of wealth. He is round and cheerful, often shown holding a money bag or a mongoose that spits out jewels. He lives in the north, in a city called Alaka, and he guards the treasures buried in the earth.

[thoughtful] Four of these gods have a special duty. They are the guardians of the four directions. Indra guards the east, where the sun rises. Yama guards the south. Varuna guards the west, where the sun sinks into the sea. And Kubera guards the north. [pause] So the whole world, in this way of seeing it, has a god standing at every edge.

[with a gentle smile] Below these great gods is a whole court of other beings. The Gandharvas are heaven's musicians, handsome and charming, who play and sing in Indra's hall. The Apsaras are the celestial dancers, the most beautiful women in all the worlds. Indra sometimes sends them down to earth to distract a sage whose meditations are growing powerful enough to threaten his throne. The Yakshas are nature spirits who live in trees and guard hidden treasure for Kubera. And there are the Ashvins, twin horsemen who ride out at dawn and are the doctors of the gods.

[thoughtful] And how many gods are there in total? The Vedas give a famous number. Thirty-three. Eight Vasus, the gods of the elements, like earth, fire and wind. Eleven Rudras, fierce storm gods. Twelve Adityas, the sons of Aditi. And two more to make thirty-three. Later on, that number grew in the retelling. The Sanskrit word koti can mean "kind", and it can also mean "ten million." So thirty-three kinds of gods became, in popular speech, three hundred and thirty million. Both numbers are still quoted today.

[leaning in] Now, one more pair, and they matter for everything to come. Both sides of the family, devas and asuras, have a teacher. A guru. The devas' teacher is Brihaspati, wise and learned, who is also the planet Jupiter. The asuras' teacher is Shukra, brilliant and cunning, who is also the planet Venus. And Shukra knows a secret that even the gods do not. He knows how to bring the dead back to life.

[ominous] So when the gods and asuras go to war, and they go to war again and again, the asuras have a terrible advantage. Every warrior they lose, Shukra can raise back up. The devas have Indra's thunderbolt. The asuras have a guru who can undo death.

[serious] And here is one last thing about the devas that surprises people. They are powerful, and they are immortal, but they are not all-powerful. Above them stand Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva. Indra's throne can be lost. Many times in the stories, an asura grows so strong through discipline and prayer that he drives the devas out of heaven entirely, and Indra has to go running to Vishnu or Shiva for help. Even the title of Indra is a kind of office. When one universe ends and another begins, there is a new Indra on the throne.

## close

[softly] So Vritra lies broken on the mountain, the rivers run again, and Indra sits back on his throne with his thunderbolt beside him. The devas are in their heaven. The asuras are waiting their turn. And the family feud between the children of Aditi and the children of Diti carries on, as it will through almost every story in this series.

[thoughtful] For the people who told these stories, the gods were the world, given faces. The rain was a battle won. The fire on the hearth was a messenger. Death was a king who had walked the road first and knew the way. So when they spoke to the fire, or poured an offering into it, they were speaking to someone who could carry their words.

[tender] And that is still happening. At a Hindu wedding today, the bride and groom walk around a sacred fire, step by step, making their promises.

[warmly] Agni is there, standing witness. [pause] The messenger is still carrying the words up to heaven.
