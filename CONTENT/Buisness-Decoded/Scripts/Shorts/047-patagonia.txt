## story

[leaning in] On the busiest shopping day of 2011, an outdoor clothing company took out a full-page advert in the New York Times. In huge letters, it said: Don't buy this jacket.

[warmly] The company was Patagonia. Its founder, Yvon Chouinard, was a rock climber in California in the 1950s. [curious] Climbers back then used pitons, metal spikes hammered into cracks in the rock to hold their ropes. The soft iron ones were left behind in the rock after one use. Yvon wanted pitons that could be used again and again. [determined] So he bought a second-hand forge, taught himself to be a blacksmith, and made his own from hard steel. He sold them out of the back of his car, for about a dollar fifty each.

[thoughtful] His climbing gear business grew to be the biggest in America. [serious] But then he saw something that upset him. All those pitons, hammered in and pulled out again and again, were cracking and scarring the famous cliffs he loved. [determined] So he stopped making most of them, and sold aluminium wedges instead, which slot into cracks without damaging the rock.

[with a smile] On a climbing trip to Scotland, he bought a rugby shirt, which turned out to be perfect for climbing. Back home, his friends all wanted one. So he started selling clothes, and in 1973 he called the brand Patagonia, after the wild mountains of South America. [pause] And he ran it differently from any company before.

## rise

[building] Patagonia gave a share of its sales to environmental groups. It switched to organic cotton. It repaired old clothes instead of selling new ones. And that famous advert asked people to buy only what they needed. [knowingly] It worked because customers trusted a company that cared more about the planet than about selling them another jacket. Sales went up.

[quietly impressed] Then, in 2022, Yvon Chouinard did something extraordinary. He and his family gave the whole company away, to a trust and a charity, so that every penny of profit not put back into the business goes to fighting climate change. Earth, he said, is now our only shareholder.

## close

[warmly] It took a climber who made his own gear, and then asked what it was doing to the mountains.
