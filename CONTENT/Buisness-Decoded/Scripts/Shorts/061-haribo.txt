## story

[leaning in] Every single day, Haribo makes about a hundred million gold bears. The very first ones were made in a back kitchen in Germany, by one man with a sack of sugar.

[warmly] His name was Hans Riegel. He was a sweet maker from Bonn, a city on the river Rhine. In 1920 he decided to start his own business. [curious] He named it by taking the first letters of his name and his city: HAns RIegel BOnn. Haribo.

[thoughtful] He had very little to start with. A sack of sugar, a copper kettle, a marble slab, a stool and a stove, all squeezed into a small room at the back of his house. [with a smile] And his wife, Gertrud, did the deliveries. She loaded the sweets onto a bicycle and pedalled them round the shops of Bonn.

[curious] In 1922, Hans had an idea. At the fairs and festivals of the time, entertainers sometimes led real bears on chains, and made them stand up and dance for the crowds. [delighted] So he made a soft, chewy, fruit-flavoured sweet in the shape of a little dancing bear. He called it the Dancing Bear. Children loved it.

[serious] Then came the Second World War. Hans died in 1945, and his two sons were held as prisoners of war. [pause] For a while, it looked as though the little business might not survive.

## rise

[determined] But in 1946 the sons came home, and took over. They rebuilt the factory and grew the company fast in the years after the war, when people finally had a little money for treats again. [building] In 1960, they reshaped the old dancing bear into a smaller, cuter gold bear. It became Haribo's most famous sweet. [knowingly] It worked because it was simple, cheap, fun and the same every time, and the catchy slogan, Haribo makes children happy, followed it into every country.

[quietly impressed] Today Haribo makes sweets in factories around the world and sells them in more than a hundred countries, and it is still owned by the Riegel family.

## close

[warmly] It took a sack of sugar, a bicycle, and a bear that danced at the fair.
