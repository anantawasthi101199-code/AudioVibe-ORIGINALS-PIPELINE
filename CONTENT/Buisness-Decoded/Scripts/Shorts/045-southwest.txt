## story

[leaning in] The plan for one of the most successful airlines in history was drawn on a napkin. And it took nearly four years of court battles before its first plane could take off.

[warmly] In 1966, in a bar in San Antonio, Texas, a businessman called Rollin King met his lawyer, Herb Kelleher. King had an idea. [curious] He drew a triangle, with Dallas, Houston and San Antonio, three big Texas cities, at the corners. An airline that flew only between these three, he said, cheaply and often. [with a smile] Kelleher looked at it and said, Rollin, you're crazy. Let's do it.

[thoughtful] There was a clever reason for flying only inside Texas. In those days, flights between American states were controlled by the national government, which set the fares, and kept them high. Flights inside a single state were not.

[grave] The big airlines did not want a cheap rival. They took Southwest to court, again and again, to stop it ever getting off the ground. [determined] Kelleher fought every case, all the way to the highest court in Texas, and then the highest court in America. And he won.

[delighted] On the eighteenth of June, 1971, Southwest's first flights took off. It had just three planes. [serious] Money was so tight that it soon had to sell one of its four aircraft. [pause] But it still wanted to fly the same schedule.

## rise

[building] So the staff found a way to land, unload, load and take off again in just ten minutes. Turning planes around that fast meant fewer planes could fly more trips. [knowingly] And that became the secret. Fast turnarounds, one type of plane, simple flights between cities, low fares, and staff who joked over the intercom and seemed to enjoy themselves. It worked because every part of it was designed to keep costs low and planes in the air.

[quietly impressed] Southwest made a profit for nearly fifty years in a row, an almost unheard-of record in the airline business. Today it is one of the biggest airlines in the United States, and low-cost airlines all over the world copied its recipe.

## close

[warmly] It took a triangle on a napkin, and a lawyer who refused to lose.
