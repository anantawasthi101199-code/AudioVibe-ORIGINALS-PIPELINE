## story

[leaning in] Every day in India, millions of meals are ordered through Zomato. It began with a stack of paper menus in an office kitchen in Delhi.

[warmly] In 2008, Deepinder Goyal was working as a consultant in a big company office in Delhi, the capital of India. At lunchtime, he noticed the same thing every day. People crowded around a pile of paper menus from nearby restaurants, flipping through them, passing them around, trying to decide what to order. [wry] The menus were creased, stained and often out of date.

[curious] So Deepinder did something simple. He scanned the menus and put them on the office computer network, so anyone could read them from their desk. [delighted] The page was suddenly getting huge numbers of visits. Colleagues used it every day.

[thoughtful] He realised that if people in one office wanted this, people across the whole city would want it too. With a colleague, he built a website that collected restaurant menus, photos and reviews from across Delhi. They called it Foodiebay. In 2010 they renamed it Zomato, a fresh name that was easier to remember.

[determined] They sent people from restaurant to restaurant, photographing menus by hand, and spread to other Indian cities, then to other countries. It grew into a popular guide for finding where to eat. [pause] But a guide only helps you choose a restaurant. Zomato wanted to be part of the meal itself.

## rise

[building] So, in 2015, it started delivering food. Riders in red shirts on motorbikes began picking up meals and racing them across busy cities. [knowingly] It worked because Zomato already knew which restaurants people loved and what they ordered. The guide became the shop.

[serious] Competition was fierce, especially from a rival called Swiggy, and for years it lost money. [quietly impressed] But in 2021 Zomato joined India's stock market, and investors rushed to buy shares. It has since added quick grocery delivery, and its riders now deliver across hundreds of Indian cities.

## close

[warmly] It took one man who looked at a messy pile of menus and saw a problem a whole country shared.
