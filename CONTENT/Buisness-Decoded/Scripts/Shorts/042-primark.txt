## story

[leaning in] In an age when almost every shop sells online, Primark became one of Europe's biggest clothing sellers while barely selling on the internet at all.

[warmly] The story starts in Dublin, the capital of Ireland, in 1969. A businessman called Arthur Ryan opened a clothing shop on Mary Street, with money from a Canadian-British food and retail family. [curious] He called it Penneys. The idea was simple: fashionable everyday clothes, at prices so low that almost anyone could afford them.

[delighted] It was a hit. Irish shoppers who had very little money could buy a new shirt or dress without saving up for weeks. Penneys spread across Ireland.

[serious] Then, in the 1970s, it opened in Britain. But there was a problem with the name. A huge American store, JC Penney, already owned it. [thoughtful] So outside Ireland, the shops got a new name. Primark.

[curious] How did Primark keep its prices so low? It bought clothes in enormous quantities from factories abroad. It spent very little on advertising, and let its shoppers spread the word. Its shops were huge, and packed with stock, and people came out carrying big brown paper bags full of clothes.

[pause] And then there was the internet. Every other fashion chain rushed to sell online. [knowingly] Primark decided not to.

## rise

[building] Sending single orders to people's homes, and dealing with returns, costs money. Primark's prices were so low that it would lose money on many of those deliveries. So it kept people coming to its shops instead, where they would see everything and fill a basket. [knowingly] It worked because a trip to Primark became the point, a hunt for bargains that people enjoyed.

[grave] In 2013 a factory building in Bangladesh collapsed, killing more than a thousand workers. Primark had a supplier there. It paid compensation to victims and their families, and faced hard questions about how cheap clothes are made.

[quietly impressed] Today Primark has more than four hundred shops in Europe and the United States, and has only recently started letting people reserve items online to collect in store.

## close

[warmly] It took one shop in Dublin, and the confidence to keep doing it the old way when everyone else changed.
