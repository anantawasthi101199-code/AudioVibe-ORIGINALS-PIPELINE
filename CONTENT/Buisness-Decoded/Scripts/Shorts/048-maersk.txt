## story

[leaning in] Look around the room you are in. There is a good chance something in it crossed an ocean in a steel box on a Maersk ship.

[warmly] In 1904, in a small Danish town called Svendborg, an old sea captain called Peter Mærsk Møller and his son, Arnold, bought a second-hand steamship. [curious] Arnold was the businessman. Over the next decades, he built a fleet of ships, carrying coal, oil and cargo across the world, and painted each one with a white seven-pointed star on a blue background.

[serious] Shipping back then was slow and messy. Cargo was loaded by hand, sack by sack and crate by crate. A ship could wait in port for a week while workers carried everything on and off. Goods were damaged or stolen along the way.

[with wonder] Then, in 1956, an American trucker had a simple idea. Put everything into identical steel boxes, which could be lifted straight from a lorry onto a ship, and then onto a train, without anyone touching what was inside. The shipping container.

[thoughtful] Arnold's son, Mærsk Mc-Kinney Møller, now ran the company. He saw that containers would change everything. [pause] So in the 1970s, he bet the family business on them.

## rise

[building] Maersk built huge container ships, and port terminals to load them, and spread its routes across the globe. In 1999 it bought the very American company that had started the container business. [knowingly] It worked because a container ship can carry thousands of boxes, loaded by cranes in hours, so moving goods across the world became cheap enough that it reshaped how everything is made and sold.

[serious] In 2017, a cyberattack wiped out Maersk's computers worldwide. Ports went dark. The company rebuilt its systems from a single computer that had escaped the attack, in an office in Ghana, because a power cut had knocked it offline.

[quietly impressed] Today Maersk's ships, some of them four hundred metres long, carry millions of containers a year, and it is one of the largest shipping companies on Earth.

## close

[warmly] It took one old steamship, and a family willing to bet everything on a steel box.
