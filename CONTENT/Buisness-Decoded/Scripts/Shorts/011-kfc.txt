## story

[leaning in] At sixty-five, most people are thinking about retiring. At sixty-five, Harland Sanders had just lost his business, and was living on a government pension cheque of about a hundred dollars a month. Then he built KFC.

[softly] He was born in 1890 on a farm in Indiana, in America. When he was a small boy his father died, and his mother went out to work. So young Harland cooked for his younger brother and sister. [wry] By his own count he went on to do dozens of jobs: farmhand, railway worker, insurance salesman, tyre salesman. Most of them ended badly.

[warmly] In 1930, he took over a roadside petrol station in Corbin, Kentucky. Travellers stopping for fuel were hungry, so he started cooking for them, serving meals at his own family dining table in the back. His fried chicken became famous along that road. [proudly] The state governor even gave him the honorary title of Kentucky Colonel. He took it seriously, grew a goatee, and wore a white suit.

[curious] He spent years perfecting the recipe: a secret blend of eleven herbs and spices. And he found that a pressure cooker fried the chicken much faster than a pan, and kept it moist.

[serious] Then, in the 1950s, a new motorway was built that bypassed Corbin completely. The traffic disappeared overnight. [grave] In 1956 he had to sell everything at auction, and it barely covered his debts. [pause] He was sixty-five, almost broke, with one thing left that was worth anything: his recipe.

## rise

[determined] So he put a pressure cooker and his bags of seasoning in the back of his car, and drove from restaurant to restaurant. He would cook his chicken for the owner and staff, right there in their kitchen. If they liked it, they could sell Kentucky Fried Chicken, and pay him a few cents for every chicken they cooked. [knowingly] It worked because he did not need money to grow. Other people's restaurants did the growing for him.

[quietly impressed] Within a few years there were hundreds of them. In 1964 he sold the company for two million dollars, and stayed on as its smiling face. Today there are around thirty thousand KFC restaurants, in about a hundred and fifty countries.

## close

[warmly] It took a man in a white suit who still believed in his chicken after almost everything else had gone.
