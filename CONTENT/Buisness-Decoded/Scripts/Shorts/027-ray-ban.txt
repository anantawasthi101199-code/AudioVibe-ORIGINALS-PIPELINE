## story

[leaning in] The most famous sunglasses in the world were invented to stop pilots getting headaches in the sky.

[warmly] In the 1920s and 1930s, flying was new and thrilling. American military pilots flew higher than people had ever flown before, in open cockpits, under a blazing sun. [serious] And they were suffering. The glare up there was intense. It gave them headaches and sore eyes, and made it hard to see. Some of them came back from long flights feeling sick.

[curious] The American military asked an eyewear company called Bausch and Lomb, based in Rochester, New York, to solve the problem. The company had been making lenses for decades, for glasses, microscopes and cameras. [thoughtful] It set to work on a special lens that would cut the glare without making the world too dark. In 1936 it produced green lenses that softened the bright light. The frame was shaped like a teardrop, wide at the bottom, to cover the whole of a pilot's eye when they glanced down at their instruments.

[with a smile] In 1937, the company gave its new sunglasses a name: Ray-Ban, because they banned the sun's rays. The style became known as the Aviator, the old word for a pilot.

[quietly impressed] During the Second World War, photographs of American generals and pilots wearing them appeared in newspapers. People back home saw them, and wanted a pair. [pause] But the brand's biggest moments came on cinema screens.

## rise

[building] In 1952 Ray-Ban launched the Wayfarer, a chunky plastic frame. Stars wore them in films and on stage. [delighted] Then, in the 1980s, Tom Cruise wore Wayfarers in one hit film, and Aviators in another, Top Gun. Sales soared each time. [knowingly] It worked because Ray-Ban had a real story behind it. They were made for pilots, and wearing them felt a little bit like being one.

[thoughtful] In 1999, Bausch and Lomb sold Ray-Ban to Luxottica, a huge Italian eyewear company. Today it is part of the biggest eyewear business in the world, and the Aviator still looks almost exactly as it did in 1937.

## close

[warmly] It took a real problem, solved properly, for people who needed to see clearly in the sky.
