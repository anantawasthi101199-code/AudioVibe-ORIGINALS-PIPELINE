## story

[leaning in] In 1928, a young cartoonist lost the character he had created, and most of his staff, in a single meeting. On the train home, he started drawing a mouse.

[warmly] His name was Walt Disney. He grew up on a farm in Missouri, in the middle of America, drawing animals in the margins of his schoolbooks. [serious] His first cartoon studio, in Kansas City, went bankrupt. [determined] So in 1923 he moved to Los Angeles with almost nothing, and started again with his older brother Roy, who looked after the money.

[delighted] They had a hit with a cartoon character called Oswald the Lucky Rabbit. [grave] But Walt did not own Oswald. The man who paid for the cartoons did. In 1928, Walt went to New York to ask for more money. The man refused, told him he owned the rabbit, and revealed he had already hired away most of Walt's animators. [softly] Walt had lost everything he had built.

[thoughtful] On the long train ride back to California, he started sketching a new character, one he would own himself. A cheerful mouse, with round ears and big shoes. He wanted to call it Mortimer. His wife, Lillian, suggested something friendlier. [pause] Mickey.

## rise

[building] Walt made a daring choice. Films with sound were brand new, so he made Mickey's next cartoon, Steamboat Willie, with music and sound effects matched to every movement on screen. [delighted] Audiences in 1928 had never seen anything like it, and Mickey Mouse became a star. [knowingly] It worked because Walt kept betting on the newest thing before anyone else did. In 1937, he made Snow White, the first full-length cartoon film in America, so expensive people called it Disney's Folly. It was a massive success.

[quietly impressed] In 1955 he opened Disneyland in California. Walt died in 1966, but the company kept growing, buying Pixar, Marvel and Star Wars. Today Disney is one of the biggest entertainment companies on Earth.

## close

[warmly] It took a man who lost his first star, and made sure he owned the next one.
