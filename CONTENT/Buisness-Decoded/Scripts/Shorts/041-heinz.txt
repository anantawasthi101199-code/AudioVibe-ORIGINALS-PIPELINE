## story

[leaning in] Heinz sells hundreds of millions of bottles of ketchup a year. The idea behind it began with a jar of horseradish, and a bottle you could see through.

[warmly] Henry John Heinz grew up near Pittsburgh, in America, in the 1850s. His mother kept a big vegetable garden. [with a smile] When he was eight, Henry was already selling the leftover vegetables door to door from a basket. By twelve, he had his own plot of land and a horse and cart to deliver with.

[serious] In those days, food sold in shops was often a cheat. There were almost no food safety laws. Sellers padded their horseradish with ground-up turnip and wood fibres, and hid it in dark green or brown bottles, so nobody could tell.

[determined] In 1869, Henry and a friend started bottling horseradish made the way his mother made it, pure, with nothing added. And they put it in clear glass. [knowingly] Anyone could hold it up to the light and see exactly what was inside. Customers trusted it straight away.

[grave] But in 1875 a financial crash hit America, and the young company went bankrupt. Henry was humiliated. He kept a notebook listing every person he owed money to, and he promised himself he would pay every one of them back. [pause] The very next year, he started again, with his brother and a cousin.

## rise

[building] And this time, one of their first new products was tomato ketchup. Heinz made it thick, sweet and the same every time, and sold it in that same honest clear bottle. In 1896, on a train, Henry saw an advert for shoes in twenty-one styles, and invented his own slogan: fifty-seven varieties. They already sold more than sixty products. He just liked the number.

[proudly] Henry also campaigned for America's first big food safety law, passed in 1906, when other food makers fought against it. He opened his factories to visitors, to show how clean they were. [knowingly] It worked because Heinz sold trust, before most companies understood that trust was something you could sell.

[quietly impressed] Today Heinz ketchup is sold in more than a hundred countries, and the number fifty-seven is still on every bottle.

## close

[warmly] It took a clear glass bottle, and a man who paid back every penny before he started again.
