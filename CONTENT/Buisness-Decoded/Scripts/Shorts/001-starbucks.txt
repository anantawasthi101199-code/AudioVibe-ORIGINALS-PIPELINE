## story

[leaning in] Today there are around forty thousand Starbucks cafes on the planet. In 1971, there was one small shop in Seattle, and it would not sell you a cup of coffee.

[warmly] Picture the shop. It sits by the fish stalls of Pike Place Market, a busy waterfront market in Seattle, on the west coast of America. Wooden bins full of dark roasted beans. A smell you can taste from the street. Three friends opened it: two teachers and a writer who loved good coffee and were tired of the bland powdered stuff most Americans drank. They named it after Starbuck, a sailor in the novel Moby-Dick, because the name sounded like the sea. They sold beans and coffee makers. That was the whole business.

[curious] In 1982 they hired a salesman from New York called Howard Schultz to run their marketing. A year later, they sent him to a trade show in Milan, in Italy. [pause] And that trip changed everything.

[with wonder] Schultz walked into an espresso bar. A man behind the counter pulled shots of espresso and steamed milk, and knew every customer by name. People stood at the counter, talking, laughing, in no hurry at all. Every street seemed to have one of these bars. [thoughtful] Schultz saw that Italians were buying more than a drink. They were buying a place to be, somewhere between home and work.

[determined] He flew home and begged the founders to sell drinks. They tried a small coffee bar in one shop, and it was busy, but they wanted to stay a bean shop. So in 1985 Schultz left, and opened his own espresso bar in Seattle, called Il Giornale. Then, in 1987, the founders decided to sell Starbucks. [short pause] Schultz raised the money from investors and bought it, for about three point eight million dollars.

## rise

[building] Now he could do it his way. He put the Starbucks name on his espresso bars and started opening them fast, first across America, then the world. Comfy chairs. Big windows. Your name written on the cup. He called it the third place, after home and after work, and that idea is why it worked: people paid for the coffee, and stayed for the room.

[quietly impressed] Starbucks went on the stock market in 1992. Today it serves coffee in more than eighty countries, from Tokyo to London.

## close

[warmly] It took one man noticing that the cup of coffee was only half of what Italians were buying.
