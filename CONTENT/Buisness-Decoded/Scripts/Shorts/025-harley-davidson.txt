## story

[leaning in] Harley-Davidson motorbikes have a sound people recognise from a street away. The first one was built in 1903, in a wooden shed in a back garden.

[warmly] The shed belonged to the Davidson family, in Milwaukee, a city in the American Midwest. It was small, about the size of a garden shed today. [curious] Inside, two young friends were trying to put an engine on a bicycle. One was William Harley, aged twenty-one. The other was Arthur Davidson. Before long, Arthur's brothers joined them, and someone painted the words Harley-Davidson Motor Company on the shed door.

[thoughtful] Their first machines were simple, little more than strong bicycles with a motor. But they kept improving them, making the engines bigger and the frames tougher, and they built them to last. [delighted] Riders noticed, and so did the police, who began buying them.

[serious] Then came war. In the First World War, Harley-Davidson built thousands of motorbikes for the American army. In the Second World War, it built about ninety thousand more. [with a smile] Soldiers came home having ridden them, and wanted one of their own.

[grave] But by the 1970s, the company was in trouble. Japanese makers like Honda were selling motorbikes that were cheaper, lighter and more reliable. Harley-Davidson had been bought by a big leisure company, and quality slipped badly. Bikes leaked oil and broke down. [pause] Many people thought the brand was finished.

## rise

[determined] In 1981, a group of its own managers bought the company back. They fixed the quality problems, factory by factory. [building] And they did something clever. They started a club for owners, called the Harley Owners Group. Riders met up, rode together, and went to rallies. [knowingly] It worked because a Harley stopped being just a machine. It became a way of life, and a family of people who shared it. Customers did the marketing themselves, with tattoos of the logo.

[quietly impressed] Harley-Davidson came back from the edge, and more than a century later it is still in Milwaukee, still making heavy motorbikes with that famous rumble.

## close

[warmly] It took two friends in a shed, and riders who loved the bikes enough to keep them alive.
