## hook

[warmly] In 1958, a young man who had just come home from Yemen rented a tiny office in one of Bombay's busiest market streets. [with a smile] Room for a desk, a few chairs, and not much else. He traded spices and yarn.

[building] Today, the company he started is one of the largest in India. It runs one of the biggest oil refineries on the planet, the country's largest mobile network, and its biggest chain of shops, [quietly impressed] and in a recent year it became the first Indian company to bring in more than ten lakh crore rupees in revenue.

[curious] So how do you get from a desk in a market street to that? [leaning in] Today we'll follow Dhirubhai Ambani from a clerk's job in Aden to a textile mill, a share sale that turned shopkeepers into investors, a long string of very bold bets, and the arguments that followed him the whole way.

[warmly] Hey, I'm Adrian, and this is Business Decoded.

## beginnings

[warmly] First, a quick word on who's who, because this is a family story as much as a business one, and it covers three generations. [short pause] At the centre is Dhirubhai Ambani, the founder, and his wife, Kokilaben. They had two sons and two daughters. The sons are the ones to remember: Mukesh, the elder, and Anil, the younger. [thoughtful] Both grew up inside the business, and both ended up running parts of it, which, as we'll see, did not go smoothly. And Mukesh's three children, Akash, Isha and Anant, are the third generation, who run pieces of the company today. [matter-of-fact] That's the cast. Now the story.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. Dhirajlal Hirachand Ambani, Dhirubhai to everyone, was born on the twenty-eighth of December 1932 in Chorwad, a village on the coast of Gujarat. His father was a schoolteacher, and there was never much money. [with a smile] The family story goes that as a teenager, Dhirubhai sold snacks to pilgrims at a nearby hill to earn a bit extra.

[curious] In 1949, when he was sixteen, he followed his elder brother to Aden, in what is now Yemen. Aden was then a British port, one of the busiest in the world, with ships from everywhere stopping to refuel. [matter-of-fact] He got a job as a clerk with a trading firm called A. Besse and Company, and later worked with its business distributing Shell petrol and oil.

[thoughtful] Aden was his business school. He watched traders from a dozen countries, learned how goods and money moved, and saw how much there was to gain from spotting a price difference before anyone else.

[reflective] He stayed in Aden for most of the 1950s. By then, the British were starting to lose their grip on the port, the politics were turning, and many Indians there were thinking about going home.

[matter-of-fact] In 1958 he came back to Bombay, now with a wife and a young son, Mukesh, and very little capital. With a cousin, Champaklal Damani, he started a trading firm, Reliance Commercial Corporation. [dry, amused] As the company tells it, the first office in Masjid Bunder was about three hundred and fifty square feet, with one table, three chairs and a telephone. [painting a picture] Masjid Bunder was the old trading heart of Bombay, all narrow streets, warehouses and sacks of spices, and Reliance was one small name among thousands.

They exported spices to the Gulf, where Dhirubhai already knew the buyers, and imported polyester yarn.

[leaning in] Yarn turned out to be the big one. Polyester was new in India, the government strictly controlled who could import it, and demand far outran supply. Dhirubhai learned the yarn trade inside out, and he was willing to do what his cousin wasn't. [boldly] He would buy up large stocks of yarn when he expected prices to rise, and hold them.

## struggle

[serious] But it didn't stay easy for long. That appetite for risk split the partnership. [matter-of-fact] Damani was a careful trader, Dhirubhai was not, and in 1965 they went their separate ways.

[building] A year later, in 1966, Dhirubhai made his first big jump, from trading into making things. He set up a small textile mill at Naroda, near Ahmedabad, to weave fabric from polyester yarn. [warmly] He called the brand Vimal, after his nephew.

[wry] And the cloth merchants of Gujarat shut the door on him. The big wholesalers, the middlemen who decided what reached the shops, didn't want to deal with a newcomer, and they refused to stock his cloth.

[with a grin] So he went round them. Reliance opened its own Only Vimal shops, sent salesmen directly to retailers, and spent heavily on advertising, so customers walked into shops asking for Vimal by name. [delighted] It worked. Vimal became one of the best-known fabric brands in India.

[explaining] India in those years ran on what people called the licence raj. To build a factory, expand one, or import a machine, you needed a government licence, and licences were scarce. [matter-of-fact] Dhirubhai was very good at getting them, and getting them early. [measured] Supporters saw a man who understood the system better than anyone. Critics saw a man who bent it.

[building] Then came the step that defined him. In 1977, Reliance Textile Industries offered its shares to the public. [conversational] This is called an IPO, an initial public offering: the first time a company sells pieces of itself to ordinary people on the stock market. At the time, most Indians didn't own shares. [quietly impressed] Dhirubhai went looking for those who didn't, in small towns across Gujarat and beyond, and tens of thousands of first-time investors bought in.

[warmly] And he kept them close. Reliance's annual meetings became famous, held in sports grounds instead of hotel ballrooms, with shareholders filling the stands.

[serious] In 1982, that loyalty was tested. A group of stockbrokers, many of them from Calcutta, bet heavily that Reliance shares would fall, selling shares they didn't own in the hope of buying them back cheaper. [building tension] Buyers allied with Reliance bought every share on offer and then demanded delivery. The brokers couldn't supply the shares, the price shot up instead of down, and the Bombay Stock Exchange closed for three days to sort out the mess. [dry, amused] The bears lost a great deal of money.

[measured] Through the 1980s, the arguments grew louder. The newspaper Indian Express, and the rival textile company Bombay Dyeing, accused Reliance of getting import permissions and government policy changes timed to suit it. Reliance denied wrongdoing, and the allegations were investigated without bringing the company down. [softly] And in 1986, at fifty-three, Dhirubhai had a stroke, which weakened his right hand. [matter-of-fact] From then on, his sons, Mukesh and Anil, took on more and more of the running of the company.

## rise

[building] And then came the move that changed everything. Dhirubhai worked backwards.

[conversational] Think about a shirt. It's woven from fabric, the fabric is made from polyester yarn, the yarn is spun from chemicals, and those chemicals come from oil. Most companies pick one step and buy everything before it from somebody else. [delighted] Dhirubhai wanted all of it.

[explaining] This is called backward integration: instead of buying what you need, you start making it yourself, one step further back each time. Reliance started with fabric. In 1982 it opened a plant at Patalganga, near Bombay, to make its own polyester yarn. Then it built plants to make the chemicals that go into polyester. [building] In 1991 came a huge petrochemicals complex at Hazira, in Gujarat.

[curious] Why does that matter? [thoughtful] Because every step you own is a profit margin you keep instead of paying to a supplier. And it protects you. If you control the raw materials, nobody can squeeze you by raising the price of what you need. [matter-of-fact] Each step also needed enormous amounts of money, and Reliance kept going back to its army of small shareholders to raise it.

[slowly] The last step back was oil itself. [building] In 1999, Reliance opened a refinery at Jamnagar, on the Gujarat coast, on land that had been dry scrub. It was built at great speed, it was enormous from the very start, and Reliance kept adding to it. [explaining] A refinery takes crude oil, the thick black liquid that comes out of the ground, and splits it into things people can use: petrol, diesel, jet fuel, and the raw materials for plastics and polyester. Jamnagar was designed to handle many different kinds of crude, so Reliance could buy whatever oil was cheapest on the world market that month. Much of what it made was sold abroad. [quietly impressed] Today, Jamnagar is the largest refining complex at a single site anywhere in the world.

[thoughtful] So step back and look at the whole chain. Oil comes in at Jamnagar. It becomes chemicals, then polyester, then yarn, then fabric. [emphatic] One company, from the oil tanker to the shirt.

[softly] Dhirubhai lived to see it running. He died in July 2002, at sixty-nine. [slowly] And then the family fell out. His sons couldn't agree on how to run the business, and in 2005 their mother, Kokilaben, brokered a split. [matter-of-fact] Mukesh took Reliance Industries, with the refinery and the petrochemicals. Anil took the newer businesses in power, telecoms and finance.

[curious] Mukesh Ambani then started taking Reliance in directions his father never had. In 2006, Reliance Retail opened its first shops, selling fresh food and groceries, and it kept opening more.

[building] And in September 2016, he launched Jio, a mobile network built from scratch for fast internet, at a cost of many billions of dollars. [incredulous] Jio offered calls and data free for the first few months, and after that, data at a fraction of what India had been paying. [quietly impressed] Mobile data prices across India fell dramatically. The rival networks had to slash their own prices, several of the smaller ones were swallowed up or disappeared, and within a few years Jio had hundreds of millions of customers.

[curious] Why give it away? [explaining] Because a phone network costs almost the same to run whether ten people use it or ten million. Once the towers and cables are built, every extra customer is nearly pure gain. So the fastest way to make the network pay is to fill it, quickly, and nothing fills a network faster than free. [wry] It's the same logic as a shopkeeper handing out free samples, just with a few billion dollars of towers behind it.

## ending

[thoughtful] Where does it stand now? Reliance Industries is one of India's most valuable companies, and often the most valuable of all. It runs the Jamnagar refinery, the largest mobile network in the country, and India's biggest retail chain, with thousands of shops selling everything from groceries to phones to clothes. Through Jio and its shops, it reaches a very large share of India's population every single day. [with a smile] Mukesh Ambani, who was a baby when that first office opened, is one of the richest people in Asia, and his own children now run parts of the business. [matter-of-fact] Akash leads Jio, Isha leads the shops, and Anant works on the company's new energy business.

[measured] The arguments never fully went away. People still debate how much of the rise came from skill and how much from closeness to power, and the company's size gives it enormous weight in every market it enters. [dry, amused] When Reliance arrives, rivals tend to cut prices, merge or leave.

[matter-of-fact] And the brothers' stories ended very differently. [sombre] Anil's group, which had looked like the more exciting half in 2005, ended up in debt troubles and court cases. Mukesh's half, built on the old refinery and chemicals business, became the base for Jio and the shops.

[thoughtful] What did it take? A trader's eye from the docks of Aden. A refusal to take no from the cloth merchants who shut him out. [emphatic] And the nerve to keep borrowing and building, one step further back each time, from a shirt all the way to the oil it is made from.

[warmly] In 1958 it was a rented desk in Masjid Bunder, spices and yarn. [pause] [quietly impressed] Today it's an oil refinery the size of a town, and a phone in hundreds of millions of pockets.
