## hook

[leaning in] On the fourth of May, 1904, two men sat down for lunch at the Midland Hotel in Manchester. [curious] One had sold newspapers on the street as a boy, and now built electric cranes. The other was the son of a lord, who flew balloons and raced cars for fun.

[warmly] Their names, side by side, became shorthand for the best of anything. Rolls-Royce. Today the name is on the most expensive cars in the world, and on the engines that carry millions of people through the sky every day.

[thoughtful] This is the story of how that lunch became a business, how it saved Britain in a war, went bankrupt, split in two, and somehow came back each time.

[warmly] Hey, I'm Adrian, and this is Business Decoded.

## beginnings

[warmly] A few people to know before we start. Henry Royce, the engineer. Charles Rolls, the salesman. And Claude Johnson, Rolls's business partner, who ran the company and became known as the hyphen in Rolls-Royce. [matter-of-fact] That's the cast.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. Henry Royce was born in 1863 in a village near Peterborough, in the east of England. His father, a miller, went bankrupt and died when Henry was young. [softly] At about nine, Henry was selling newspapers and delivering telegrams in London to help his family survive. He had only about a year of proper school.

[determined] An aunt paid for him to become an apprentice at the railway works in Peterborough, learning to cut and shape metal. He taught himself electricity at night classes. In 1884, with about twenty pounds, he started a small company in Manchester, F. H. Royce and Company, making electrical parts, then dynamos and electric cranes. [knowingly] He was a perfectionist. His motto was to take the best that exists and make it better.

[curious] In 1903, Royce bought a second-hand French car. It kept breaking down. So he decided to build his own. In 1904, the first Royce car rolled out of his works in Manchester, smooth, quiet and reliable.

[thoughtful] Charles Rolls was born in 1877, the third son of Lord Llangattock, a wealthy Welsh landowner. He studied engineering at Cambridge, loved speed, and set up a company in London selling fancy French cars to rich customers. [curious] But he wanted a British car good enough to sell. A friend showed him Royce's car, and arranged that lunch in Manchester.

[delighted] Rolls drove the Royce car, and was won over. They agreed that Royce would build the cars, and Rolls would sell every one of them, under both their names. In 1906 they formed Rolls-Royce Limited.

## struggle

[building] But it didn't stay easy for long. Royce worked himself so hard that his health broke down more than once. In 1907, the company launched a car later called the Silver Ghost, and drove it fifteen thousand miles in a reliability trial with almost no problems. [delighted] It made Rolls-Royce famous as the best car in the world.

[grave] Then, in July 1910, Charles Rolls was killed when his aeroplane broke up at an air show in Bournemouth. He was thirty-two, the first Briton to die in a powered flying accident. [softly] One half of the name was gone after just six years.

[thoughtful] Claude Johnson kept the business running, and Royce kept designing. In the First World War, Rolls-Royce built aircraft engines, starting with one called the Eagle. In 1919, two airmen, Alcock and Brown, made the first non-stop flight across the Atlantic, powered by two Eagle engines.

[serious] In the Second World War, its Merlin engine powered the Spitfire and the Hurricane, the fighters of the Battle of Britain, and the Lancaster bomber. Rolls-Royce became central to the country's survival. Henry Royce did not live to see it. He died in 1933.

[grave] After the war, the company moved into jet engines, and in the 1960s it bet everything on a new engine for American airliners, the RB211. To make it lighter, it tried fan blades made from carbon fibre, a new material. The blades failed in testing, and costs ran out of control. [softly] On the fourth of February, 1971, Rolls-Royce went bankrupt.

## rise

[building] And then came the move that changed everything, and it came from the government. The engine business was too important to lose, so the state took it over. [thoughtful] The car business, which was a separate thing, was split off in 1973 as Rolls-Royce Motors. From that moment, there were two different companies with the same name. Keep that in mind.

[thoughtful] The engine company finished the RB211 with metal blades, and it worked. It became the parent of a whole family of engines called the Trent, used on Airbus and Boeing airliners. [quietly impressed] In 1987 the government sold it back to private investors.

[curious] And it found a clever way to make money. Instead of just selling an engine, Rolls-Royce charged airlines for every hour the engine flew, and looked after it for them. They called it power by the hour. [knowingly] Think of leasing a car with all the servicing included. The airline knows its costs, and Rolls-Royce earns steadily for decades.

[thoughtful] Meanwhile the cars changed hands. In 1998, Volkswagen bought the car factory and the Bentley brand, but the right to use the name Rolls-Royce on cars was sold to BMW, the German carmaker. [with a smile] Since 2003, Rolls-Royce cars have been built by BMW, at a factory in Goodwood, in Sussex.

[serious] The engine company had hard times too. In 2017 it paid about six hundred and seventy million pounds to settle bribery investigations in Britain, America and Brazil, over payments made in several countries. [grave] Then, in 2020, the pandemic grounded the world's planes. Paid by the flying hour, Rolls-Royce suddenly earned almost nothing. It lost billions, cut around nine thousand jobs, and raised money from shareholders to survive. [determined] A new boss arrived in 2023, cut costs hard and renegotiated contracts, and the company's profits and share price climbed sharply.

## ending

[warmly] Today there are two Rolls-Royces. The cars, built by BMW in Sussex, are some of the most expensive in the world. [quietly impressed] And Rolls-Royce the engineering company, with its biggest base in Derby, makes engines for airliners, warships and submarines, and power systems, and is one of Britain's biggest manufacturers.

[reflective] What did it take? An engineer who would not accept anything less than the best. A salesman who knew a perfect machine when he drove one. And a name so trusted that a government saved it, and a German carmaker paid for it.

[softly] From a boy selling newspapers on a London street, and a young lord who loved speed, sitting down for lunch in Manchester, to a name that still means the best.
