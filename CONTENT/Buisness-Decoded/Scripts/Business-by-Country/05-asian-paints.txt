## hook

[warmly] In 1942, four friends started making paint in a rented garage in Bombay. The big names in Indian paint at the time were mostly British-owned, with factories, laboratories and decades of experience. These four had a garage.

Within twenty-five years, their company was the largest paint maker in India. Today, Asian Paints sells paint in more than a dozen countries, and it has led the Indian market for more than half a century.

[curious] So how does a garage beat the giants? Not with a secret formula. Today we'll see how Asian Paints won with a smart choice about who to sell to, a cartoon boy drawn by one of India's most famous cartoonists, and, of all things, an early computer.

## beginnings

So let's go back to the very beginning. In 1942, the Second World War was on, and India was still under British rule. Paint had largely been imported, or made by foreign-owned firms, and the war made imports hard to get. At the same time, the Quit India movement was at its height, and many Indians wanted to buy from Indian businesses.

Four friends in Bombay saw the gap. Champaklal Choksey, Chimanlal Choksi, Suryakant Dani and Arvind Vakil were young men without any great fortune or any deep background in chemistry. [matter-of-fact] What they had was a garage in Gaiwadi, in the Girgaum area of Bombay, and the conviction that Indians would buy Indian paint if it was good and fairly priced.

On the first of February 1942, they started the business, and called it Asian Oil and Paint Company. They began small, with a limited range, and they did a lot of the work themselves, mixing, filling tins and selling.

[thoughtful] It helps to know what paint actually is, because it explains why four friends could start making it in a garage. Paint is three things mixed together. A pigment, the powder that gives the colour. A binder, which glues that powder to the wall once it dries. And a liquid, which keeps it all runny until it's on the brush. None of that needs a huge factory to begin with. What it needs is consistency, the same colour and the same quality in every tin, every time. That turns out to be the hard part.

[matter-of-fact] Wartime made everything harder to get, including the raw materials. Pigments, oils and tins were all in short supply, and a small new company was at the back of the queue. The friends had to scrape together what they could, and learn as they went.

But the war also kept the bigger competition busy. With shipping disrupted and imports scarce, there was room for a small local maker that could actually deliver paint to a shop that needed it. Asian Paints spent those first years doing exactly that, slowly building a name with shopkeepers in and around Bombay.

The paint market of the day was dominated by established names, many of them British-owned, like Shalimar Paints, British Paints and Jenson and Nicholson. They had the reputations, the shops in the big cities, and the customers who had always bought from them.

## struggle

But it didn't stay easy for long. When the war ended, imports came back, and the established foreign companies were far bigger, with well-known brands, better technology and long relationships with the shops in the big cities.

[thoughtful] A new company in that position has a choice. It can fight the giants head-on in the places they're strongest, or it can go where they aren't.

Asian Paints went where they weren't. The big companies concentrated on the cities, on large customers and on expensive products. Asian Paints went into small towns and villages, to shopkeepers the big firms didn't bother with. And it sold paint in small packs, small enough that a family with little money could afford to paint a door, a window, or a single wall.

[delighted] It's a simple idea, and it's the same one that later sold shampoo across rural India in tiny single-use sachets. If people can't afford the big bottle, sell them a small one. You make less on each sale, but you get millions of customers nobody else is even trying to reach. And when those customers have more money one day, they already know your name.

It even sold paint for uses the big firms hardly thought about. In rural India, farmers paint the horns of their cattle for festivals like Pongal, and Asian Paints made sure its colours were there for that too.

The challenge with selling to thousands of tiny shops spread across a vast country is keeping track of them all. Every shop needs the right colours, in the right sizes, at the right moment. Too little stock and the customer goes elsewhere. Too much and the shopkeeper's money is stuck in tins on a shelf.

There was also the problem of being remembered. In 1954, the company asked the cartoonist R. K. Laxman to create a mascot. [delighted] He drew Gattu, a cheeky little boy with a paint bucket and brush, and Gattu went onto Asian Paints' ads and tins for decades.

India's independence in 1947 changed the landscape again. The new government wanted Indian industry to grow, and over the following decades it made imports harder and favoured local production. That helped Indian firms like Asian Paints, but it also meant everybody was competing for the same customers at home, under the same tight rules on licences and raw materials. [matter-of-fact] Winning wasn't a matter of being Indian. Plenty of Indian companies were. It came down to who could reach the customer best.

By 1967, the garage company had become the largest paint maker in India.

## rise

And then came the move that changed everything. Asian Paints treated selling paint as a problem of information.

In 1970, Asian Paints bought a mainframe computer, at a time when hardly any private companies in India had one. [dry, amused] For a company that made paint, it looked like an extravagance. It was nothing of the kind.

Here's why it worked. Paint sales are lumpy. People paint before festivals like Diwali, they avoid painting in the monsoon, and tastes vary from region to region. Asian Paints used its computer, and the mountains of sales data it gathered from its dealers, to forecast how much of each colour would be needed, where, and when.

[thoughtful] Think of a kitchen in a busy restaurant. If the chef knows what diners will order tonight, there's always enough of every dish, and nothing gets thrown away at closing time. Asian Paints became that kitchen for paint. Dealers could be restocked quickly, often within a day, so they didn't need to hold huge piles of stock, and Asian Paints didn't need a mountain of unsold tins in its warehouses.

That made dealers love the company. Their money wasn't tied up, they rarely ran out of a colour, and so they pushed Asian Paints to their customers. The company built a network reaching a vast number of shops across India, and every one of them fed more data back into the system.

Later, Asian Paints added tinting machines at its dealers. Instead of stocking every colour in every size, a shop keeps a few base paints, and a machine mixes the exact shade the customer wants, from thousands of options, while they wait. Less stock for the dealer, more choice for the customer.

And it kept talking to customers directly. Asian Paints' adverts became some of the best known in India, built less around the paint than around the home and the family living in it. Its line Har ghar kuch kehta hai, every home says something, ran for years. [dry, amused] The trick was simple and clever. Nobody gets excited about a tin of emulsion. Plenty of people get excited about their home.

It used its size to keep costs down, too. Big factories, careful buying of raw materials, and tight control of its supply chain meant that Asian Paints could keep prices competitive and still make strong profits.

From the 1970s, it also went abroad, and in 2002 it bought Berger International, adding paint businesses in Asia, the Middle East and the Caribbean.

It wasn't all smooth inside the company. As the founding families grew, there were disagreements about who should run it, and in the 1990s one branch of the founders' families left the management after a long dispute. But the business kept growing.

## ending

Where does it stand now? Asian Paints is India's largest paint company by a wide margin, with annual revenue of over thirty thousand crore rupees in recent years, factories across India and abroad, and a brand most Indian families know from their own walls. It has moved beyond paint, into kitchens, bathrooms and home decor. It bought the kitchen maker Sleek in 2013 and a bath fittings business, Ess Ess, the following year, and it will now send its own trained painters to do the job for you. [thoughtful] The idea is the same one it used with the dealers all those years ago. Be there at the exact moment a family makes a decision about their home, whether that's the colour of a wall or the tiles in a bathroom.

Unusually for a company this size, it is still largely controlled by the descendants of the founding families, with professional managers running it day to day.

And the competition is back, and stronger than ever. In 2024, the Aditya Birla Group launched its own paint brand, Birla Opus, with very deep pockets and plans to build some of the biggest paint factories in the country. Other big groups have moved in too. The newcomers have been offering dealers better margins and heavy discounts to win shelf space. [matter-of-fact] Asian Paints is fighting harder for its share than it has in decades, and its sales growth has slowed.

[thoughtful] In a way, the story has turned full circle. In 1942, Asian Paints was the small newcomer trying to win over dealers from established giants. Now it's the giant, and the newcomers are coming for its dealers. The question is whether the system it spent eighty years building is enough to hold them.

What did it take? Four friends without a big fortune who chose not to fight the giants where the giants were strongest. Going to the small towns the giants ignored. Selling in sizes ordinary people could afford. And understanding, earlier than almost anyone in India, that knowing exactly what your customers will want next is worth more than any secret formula.

[warmly] Four friends and a garage in Girgaum in 1942. [pause] Today, the colours on millions of Indian walls, delivered just in time for Diwali.
