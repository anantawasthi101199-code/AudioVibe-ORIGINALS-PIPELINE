## hook

[leaning in] On the fourteenth of December 1983, in New Delhi, a man named Harpal Singh was handed the keys to a small white car by the Prime Minister of India, Indira Gandhi. It was the very first Maruti 800. [a beat] He had won it in a draw, and the price was about forty-seven thousand rupees.

[curious] That little car had four doors, a tiny engine and a heater that worked. It sounds ordinary. But at that moment, the cars on India's roads were designed in the 1950s, there were waiting lists of years to buy one, and almost no ordinary family could dream of owning a car at all.

[warmly] Hey, I'm Adrian, and this is Business Decoded. Today, the story of Maruti Suzuki: how a failed political project, a government company and a small carmaker from Japan put India on wheels, and why it worked when so many people said it would not.

## beginnings

[matter-of-fact] Here are the people you need to know. Sanjay Gandhi, the younger son of Prime Minister Indira Gandhi, who dreamed up the first Maruti company. V. Krishnamurthy, a government engineer and manager who was put in charge of starting again. And Osamu Suzuki, the head of Suzuki, a Japanese company best known for motorbikes and very small cars. [short pause] Three people, three very different worlds.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. India in the early 1970s. The country made its own cars, but there were only really two of them. The Hindustan Ambassador, big, round and heavy, based on a British car from the 1950s. And the Premier Padmini, based on an old Italian Fiat. [thoughtful] The government controlled almost everything about industry. You needed a licence to make things, a licence to make more of them, and a licence to import parts. This system was nicknamed the Licence Raj, the rule of licences. Competition hardly existed.

[curious] In 1971, Sanjay Gandhi set up a company called Maruti Limited. Maruti is another name for Hanuman, the monkey god, son of the wind. The plan was a cheap, Indian-designed people's car. [serious] It was controversial from the start. Sanjay Gandhi had no real experience of building cars, and critics said the land and the licence came his way because of who his mother was. [a beat] Years went by and no real car came out of the factory.

[grave] In 1977 the company was wound up, closed down with its debts unpaid. And in 1980 Sanjay Gandhi died in a plane crash.

[thoughtful] That might have been the end of it. But in 1981 the government took over what was left and created a new state-owned company, Maruti Udyog. Udyog means industry. And it put V. Krishnamurthy in charge, with one instruction: build a modern, affordable car for India. [pause] The question was, how? India did not have the know-how to do it alone.

## struggle

[curious] Krishnamurthy and his team went looking for a partner abroad. They talked to car companies in Europe and in Japan. Most of the big names were not very interested. India was poor, the market was tiny and the rules were famous for strangling business.

[warmly] Suzuki was different. It was a smaller company, and its boss, Osamu Suzuki, saw something the others did not. Suzuki was good at one thing above all: very small, light, cheap cars, built for crowded Japanese streets. India's roads were crowded too, and money was tight. [knowingly] And Suzuki was hungry, because in Japan it was always competing against much bigger rivals. A deal that gave it a whole country to itself was worth the risk.

[matter-of-fact] In 1982 the two sides signed. Suzuki took a share in the company, at first about a quarter, and agreed to bring its car designs, its machines and its way of working. The car would be based on a small Suzuki hatchback, the Alto of its day, with an 800cc engine. That is a very small engine, the size you might find on a big motorbike today.

[serious] Then came the hard part. Building a car means hundreds of parts: seats, glass, wiring, brakes, tyres. In India, the companies that made parts were used to supplying old cars that never changed. Their quality was poor and nobody had ever demanded better. [determined] So Maruti had to build its supply chain almost from scratch, the chain of companies that make every part. Japanese engineers trained Indian suppliers on how to measure, test and reject. At first, many key parts were imported from Japan.

[curious] And there was the factory itself, at Gurgaon, just outside Delhi. Suzuki brought its own working culture. Managers and workers wore the same uniform. Everyone ate in the same canteen. Offices were open, with no private rooms for the bosses. [with a smile] In India in the 1980s, where rank meant everything, this was startling.

[grave] Many people expected it all to fail. A government company, a partner nobody had heard of, a country with no tradition of making modern cars. [pause] Then, in December 1983, the first Maruti 800 rolled out.

## rise

[building] And then came the move that changed everything. The Maruti 800 was not just a new car. It was a completely different idea of what a car in India was for.

[thoughtful] Here's why it worked. First, the price. At about forty-seven thousand rupees, it cost a lot of money, but it was within reach of a middle-class family who saved, a doctor, a shopkeeper, a government officer. Second, the running costs. It used far less petrol than the heavy old Ambassador. Third, it was simple to drive and park on narrow city streets. [knowingly] And fourth, it worked. It started in the morning. It did not need a mechanic every week.

[delighted] Demand was enormous. People booked a Maruti and waited months for it. For a while, people even sold their place in the queue for extra money.

[curious] The company kept going. In 1984 it launched the Omni, a small van that became a family carrier, a school bus and a delivery van all at once. In 1985 came the Gypsy, a tough four-wheel-drive used by the army and the police. Then saloon cars, and later the Zen and the Wagon R.

[determined] Just as important was something less exciting: service. A car is only useful if you can get it fixed. Maruti built a huge network of dealers and workshops, in big cities and then in small towns, with spare parts that were cheap and easy to find. [knowingly] An everyday comparison: it is like choosing a phone because every shop on the street can repair it. Owners trusted that, wherever they drove, someone could fix a Maruti.

[serious] Over the years Suzuki's share grew, to forty per cent in 1987, to half in 1992, and to a majority in 2002, when the government began selling its stake. In 2007 the company was renamed Maruti Suzuki. [grave] It had hard times too. A long strike in 2000. And in 2012, violence at its factory in Manesar, where a manager was killed and the plant was shut for weeks. These were dark moments, and they changed how the company dealt with its workers.

[quietly impressed] Meanwhile, in 1991, India opened its economy and foreign carmakers arrived: Hyundai, Ford, Honda, Toyota. Many people expected Maruti to be crushed. Instead, it kept around half of India's car market for years.

## ending

[warmly] Today Maruti Suzuki is India's biggest carmaker by far. It sells around two million cars a year, and roughly four out of every ten new cars sold in India carry its badge. The Maruti 800 stayed in production until 2014, more than thirty years after Harpal Singh took his keys.

[reflective] What did it take? A partner small enough to care, and good enough at the one thing India needed most: small, cheap, reliable cars. Years of patient work teaching an entire industry to make better parts. And a service network that meant nobody was ever stranded.

[softly] It began as a failed project and a company nobody believed in. [warmly] It ended up making the car that millions of Indian families still remember as their first: small, white, a little cramped, and the start of a whole new life on the road.
