## hook

[warmly] In 1946, in a district of Gujarat called Kaira, a group of dairy farmers did something drastic. [serious] For two weeks, they refused to sell a single drop of milk to the company that bought it from them. They poured it away, or kept it at home, or turned it into ghee, [with quiet satisfaction] and they let the city of Bombay go short.

[building] Today, the cooperative that grew out of that strike is owned by millions of farmers, sells under the name Amul, and is the biggest food brand in India.

[curious] And it's a strange kind of business success. There's no founder who got rich, no big investor, no takeover. [short pause] The owners are the people who milk the cows. [leaning in] Today we'll see how that happened, from a milk strike, to an engineer who never meant to stay, to the little girl in the polka-dot dress who has been selling butter since 1966.

[warmly] Hey, I'm Adrian, and this is Business Decoded.

## beginnings

[warmly] This story has no founding family, but it does have a handful of people worth knowing before we start. [short pause] Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, a leader of India's independence movement, who gave the farmers their big idea. Tribhuvandas Patel, a local farmers' leader who made it happen and became the cooperative's first chairman. [with a smile] Two Patels, by the way, but no relation; it's one of the commonest surnames in Gujarat. Then Verghese Kurien, a young engineer from the other end of India, who turned it into a giant. And his colleague H. M. Dalaya, who solved the problem everyone said couldn't be solved. [matter-of-fact] That's the cast.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. In the 1940s, the milk of Kaira district, around the town of Anand, was bought mostly by one company, Polson Dairy. Polson had a contract to supply milk to the Bombay Milk Scheme, which brought milk to the city, and a farmer with a few buffaloes had very few other places to sell.

[matter-of-fact] That meant Polson set the price. Farmers said the prices were low and unpredictable, and the middlemen who collected the milk took their own share. Milk spoils within hours in the heat, so a farmer couldn't hold it back and wait for a better offer. [grave] Every evening, he had to sell, at whatever price he was given.

[thoughtful] Think about what that does to a person's bargaining power. If you grow rice, you can store it in a sack and wait for a better price next month. If you have milk, you have until tomorrow morning. [emphatic] Whoever owns the only collection van in the village owns you.

[explaining] And Bombay was a long way off, hundreds of kilometres to the south. The milk had to be collected from villages, cooled, and sent by train, so the farmers depended completely on whoever ran that chain.

[serious] The farmers took their complaint to Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel, one of the leaders of the independence movement, who came from this part of Gujarat himself. [firmly] His advice was blunt. Stop selling to the middlemen. Form your own cooperative, a business owned by its members, and sell your milk yourselves.

[matter-of-fact] A local farmers' leader, Tribhuvandas Patel, took up the idea, and another freedom-movement leader, Morarji Desai, who would one day become Prime Minister, helped organise the farmers. [determined] In January 1946, farmers gathered at a meeting in the village of Samarkha and resolved to form their own cooperative, and to sell their milk only through it.

[matter-of-fact] This was a big ask. Most of these farmers had a couple of buffaloes and no savings. Refusing to sell meant giving up the only cash coming into the house, with no guarantee the gamble would work. [building tension] When the government at first wouldn't buy milk from a cooperative, the farmers went on strike. For about fifteen days, no milk went from Kaira to Polson, and Bombay felt it. [short pause] [quietly triumphant] The authorities gave way.

[warmly] On the fourteenth of December 1946, the Kaira District Cooperative Milk Producers' Union was registered at Anand. It started with just two village cooperatives. In June 1948, it began sending pasteurised milk to Bombay.

## struggle

[serious] But it didn't stay easy for long. The cooperative had the milk and the will, but it was short of almost everything else, from technical know-how to working machinery.

[curious] In 1949, a young engineer named Verghese Kurien was sent to Anand. He was born in 1921 in Kerala, at the other end of the country, and had trained as a mechanical engineer. He had won a government scholarship to study in the United States, at Michigan State, on the condition that he study dairy engineering, a subject he had no interest in. [wry] He studied mostly metallurgy and physics instead, and came home with the dairy part as an afterthought. In return for the scholarship, he had to serve the government for a while. They posted him to a government creamery in Anand. [wry] He did not want to be there. [dry, amused] He found the town dull and was counting the days until he could leave.

[warmly] But Tribhuvandas Patel kept asking him to help with the cooperative's old equipment. Kurien helped, then helped some more, and when his time with the government was up, he stayed with the farmers instead.

[serious] The next big problem was the seasons. Buffaloes give far more milk in winter than in summer. In winter, the cooperative had more milk than Bombay would buy. In summer, it had too little. [sombre] The surplus had to be sold off cheap or wasted, and the farmers' income swung up and down with it.

[thoughtful] The answer was to turn the extra milk into things that keep, like butter and milk powder. But almost all the milk in Kaira came from buffaloes, and the experts said buffalo milk couldn't be made into powder the way cow's milk was. [quietly impressed] Kurien's friend and colleague, the engineer H. M. Dalaya, worked out how to do it anyway. [proudly] In 1955, Anand opened a factory making butter and milk powder from buffalo milk, and Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru came to open it. [relieved] The winter surplus finally had somewhere to go.

[matter-of-fact] There was competition, too. Imported butter and milk powder, with big foreign brands behind them, were what India's shops and households were used to. The cooperative needed a name people would remember. They chose Amul. [warmly] It comes from Anand Milk Union Limited, and it also echoes a Sanskrit word, amulya, which means priceless.

[serious] And then there was the hardest part, which was the people. Kaira's villages were divided by caste and religion. A cooperative only works if everyone joins in, and from the start the rule was simple. [emphatic] Everyone stood in the same queue at the milk collection centre, whoever they were, and was paid by the same rules.

## rise

[building] And then came the move that changed everything. The farmers kept the whole chain for themselves.

[explaining] Here's how it works, and it's become known as the Anand pattern. In each village, farmers bring their milk to their own village cooperative, twice a day. The milk is tested on the spot for how much fat it contains, because fat is what makes milk valuable, and each farmer is paid according to the amount and the quality. Every village cooperative belongs to the district union, which owns the dairy plants that process the milk. And the district unions together own a marketing federation that sells everything under one brand.

[thoughtful] So think of it as a ladder where the farmers own every rung. The village society, the factory, the brand. There's no middleman at any step to take a cut, so the money from the shop shelf comes back down the ladder to the person with the buffalo. [matter-of-fact] Amul says that the farmers receive the large majority of what the customer pays.

[warmly] And the payment came fast. A farmer who brought milk in the morning could be paid for it within the day, in cash. [softly] For a family with no savings, that mattered almost as much as the price itself. It meant they could buy food, pay for school, and plan, which the old system never let them do.

[delighted] The fat test is what made it fair. Before, a buyer could simply say your milk was watery and pay you less, and you had no way to argue. Now the milk was tested in front of you, the number was written down, and the price followed from the number. [emphatic] Honest milk got an honest price.

[building] Because farmers were paid fairly and on time, more of them joined, more villages set up cooperatives, and the milk kept coming. [with a smile] In 1966, an advertising agency created a little girl with blue hair and a polka-dot dress for Amul butter, with the line Utterly Butterly Delicious. [dry, amused] She has been making jokes about the week's news on hoardings ever since.

[matter-of-fact] The government noticed. In 1964, Prime Minister Lal Bahadur Shastri visited Anand and asked for the model to be repeated across India. A National Dairy Development Board was set up in 1965, with Kurien in charge. [building] In 1970 it began Operation Flood, which helped farmers across the country to build cooperatives the Anand way.

[delighted] The way Operation Flood was paid for was clever. Europe at the time had mountains of surplus milk powder and butter oil. It was donated to India as food aid. [serious] If it had simply been handed out, it would have undercut Indian farmers and wrecked their prices. [leaning in] Instead, it was sold to the city dairies, and the money raised was used to build cooperatives, chilling centres and dairy plants across India. [quietly impressed] Europe's surplus paid for the machinery that would one day make India independent of anybody's surplus.

[explaining] The milk from all those new cooperatives was linked up with the cities by what was sometimes called a national milk grid, with rail tankers carrying milk from the regions that had too much to the cities that needed it.

[matter-of-fact] In 1973, the Gujarat unions formed a federation to sell all their products under the Amul name.

[building] The results were enormous. India had been importing milk powder. [proudly] By the end of the 1990s, it was the largest milk producer in the world.

[dry, amused] And Verghese Kurien, the man often called the father of India's White Revolution? [short pause] He said he didn't much like drinking milk.

## ending

[thoughtful] Where does it stand now? Today the Gujarat federation that sells Amul brings in tens of thousands of crores of rupees a year. It's owned by around three and a half million farmers, through some eighteen thousand village cooperatives. Amul sells milk, butter, cheese, ice cream and chocolate across India and in many other countries, [delighted] and in 2024 it even started selling fresh milk in the United States. [warmly] And it's still the farmers who own it.

[reflective] Kurien himself ran the Gujarat federation and the national dairy board for decades. Like a lot of long reigns, his ended in arguments. In 2006 he stepped down from the federation after a dispute with its board, [softly] and he died in 2012, at ninety. [matter-of-fact] The cooperatives have had their own troubles too, from local politics to fights over who controls the unions. But the basic idea, that the farmer owns the business, has outlasted all of it.

[thoughtful] And the model spread far beyond Gujarat. Across India, other states built their own cooperative milk brands on the same pattern, and people from many other countries have come to Anand to study how it works.

[thoughtful] What did it take? Farmers who refused to keep selling on someone else's terms, even at the cost of two weeks' income. An engineer who planned to leave and stayed for decades. [emphatic] And a structure that made fairness the business model, not a slogan, so the people producing the milk had every reason to keep producing more.

[warmly] In 1946, a few villages poured away their milk rather than sell it cheap. [pause] [with a smile] Today, the business they built is owned by millions of farmers, and the girl in the polka-dot dress is still on the billboards.
