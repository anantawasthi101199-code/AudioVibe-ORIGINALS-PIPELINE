## hook

[warmly] In 1868, a twenty-nine-year-old in Bombay put twenty-one thousand rupees into a small trading firm. [building] Today, the group that grew out of it makes steel, writes software for the world's biggest banks, runs an airline, owns hotels, and builds Jaguars and Land Rovers. [short pause] Its companies bring in well over a hundred and sixty billion dollars a year.

[curious] But here's the strangest part. Most of the company at the very top isn't owned by a family, or a fund, or the stock market. [short pause] [with a knowing smile] It's owned by charities.

[leaning in] Today we'll walk through how that happened. One man and his four enormous ambitions, the sons who finished what he started, the month the steelworks nearly couldn't pay its workers, and how business and charity ended up tied together so tightly you can't pull them apart.

[warmly] Hey, I'm Adrian, and this is Business Decoded.

## beginnings

[warmly] Before we start, a quick map of the family, because the names repeat and it's easy to get lost. [short pause] This story runs across five generations. At the top is Nusserwanji Tata, a trader. His son, Jamsetji, is the founder, and the main character of our first half. Jamsetji had two sons, Dorabji and Ratanji, and a younger cousin, R. D. Tata, who worked alongside him. [thoughtful] Neither of the sons had children of their own. So the family line carries on in two other ways. R. D.'s son was J. R. D. Tata, who ran the group for more than fifty years. And Ratanji's widow, Navajbai, adopted a boy called Naval, whose son was Ratan Tata, the chairman most people today have heard of. [matter-of-fact] Ratan was the last Tata to run the group. [warmly] Keep that map in your head, and the rest of the story will click into place.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. Jamsetji Nusserwanji Tata was born in 1839 in Navsari, a small town in Gujarat, into a family of Parsi priests. His father, Nusserwanji, broke with family tradition and went into trade in Bombay, and Jamsetji joined him there as a boy. He studied at Elphinstone College, and at around twenty he went into his father's business.

That business traded with China and beyond, in cotton and tea and, like most big Bombay trading houses of the time, in opium. [matter-of-fact] It was not a gentle trade, and the Tatas were part of it.

[curious] Then came a stroke of world history. In 1861 the American Civil War began, and the cotton that fed the mills of Lancashire in England stopped coming from the American South. Mills went looking for cotton anywhere else, and Bombay boomed. [quickening] Prices went mad, fortunes were made overnight, [slowing down] and when the war ended in 1865, prices collapsed and a lot of those fortunes went with them. [sombre] The Tata family business took real losses.

But Jamsetji had spent part of those years in England, and he had walked through the cotton mills of Lancashire. [thoughtful] He came home with a simple, slightly dangerous idea. India grew the cotton. India had the buyers. [incredulous] So why was the cloth being made in England and shipped back?

[matter-of-fact] In 1868 he started his own trading company with twenty-one thousand rupees. A year later he bought a bankrupt oil mill in Chinchpokli, in Bombay, turned it into a cotton mill, called it Alexandra Mill, and sold it two years later at a healthy profit. [quietly impressed] It was a test run, and it worked.

[thoughtful] Notice what he did there. He didn't need to keep the mill forever. He learned how a mill really worked, with somebody else's failed business as his classroom, and then he sold it on at a profit. [with a smile] A cheap education that paid for itself.

[confident] So he went bigger. He raised money for a proper spinning and weaving company, and he did something unusual. Instead of building in Bombay, where everybody built, he went to Nagpur, in the middle of the country. Nagpur sat close to the cotton fields, it had a railway junction, and it had water and coal nearby. On the first of January 1877, the same day Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India, his mill opened. He named it, [wry] with excellent timing, Empress Mills.

## struggle

[serious] But it didn't stay easy for long. The early years at Empress Mills were rough. The machinery he had bought was poor, the cloth wasn't good enough, and the mill lost money. [determined] Jamsetji brought in new management, threw out what didn't work, and put in newer machines, including some of the first ring spindles in India, which spin yarn faster and more evenly than the older kind. [quietly impressed] Slowly, Empress Mills became one of the best-run mills in the country.

[wry] Then he did it again, and this time it nearly went the other way. In 1886 he bought a run-down mill in Kurla and renamed it Svadeshi Mills, meaning made in our own country. [sombre] It struggled for years. Investors lost faith, and Jamsetji put his own money and shares into it to keep it alive until it finally turned round.

[leaning in] But textile mills were never really the point. By the 1890s, he had four much bigger ambitions for India. [emphatic] A steel plant, because a country without its own steel has to buy its railways and bridges from somebody else. Cheap electric power from water, to run Bombay's mills. A world-class institute for science, so Indians could be trained as scientists at home. [short pause] And a great hotel.

[dry, amused] The hotel is the one people love to tell a story about, that he was turned away from a European-only hotel and decided to build a better one. It's a lovely story. [short pause] There's no solid evidence it ever happened. [matter-of-fact] What we do know is that he paid for the Taj Mahal Palace himself, and it opened in Bombay in December 1903.

[serious] The other three were much harder. The science institute needed land, money and the approval of a colonial government that wasn't convinced. In 1898 he offered a large part of his personal fortune, including fourteen buildings in Bombay, to fund it. [exasperated] The officials took years to make up their minds.

The steel plant was harder still. Nobody had made steel at scale in India, the mining rules made it hard to even look for iron ore, and a story often told about this period has a senior British railway official promising to eat every pound of steel rail the Tatas ever made. [determined] Jamsetji travelled to the United States in 1902 and hired an American engineer, Charles Page Perin, to help find a site.

[slowly] And then, in May 1904, while travelling in Germany, Jamsetji Tata died. He was sixty-five. [softly] Of his four great ambitions, only the hotel was finished.

## rise

[building] And then came the move that changed everything. His elder son, Dorabji, and his cousin, R. D. Tata, decided to finish the job.

[curious] The search for iron ore paid off. A geologist, P. N. Bose, pointed the Tatas towards rich deposits in Mayurbhanj, in eastern India, and the surveyors found a site near a village called Sakchi, where two rivers met, with ore, coal, water and a railway all within reach. [satisfied] That was the place.

[serious] The problem was money, and a lot of it. Investors in London weren't interested. [quietly impressed] So in 1907 the Tatas did something nobody had tried on this scale. They asked ordinary Indians. India was in the middle of the Swadeshi movement, when people were being urged to buy Indian goods instead of British ones, and Tata Iron and Steel asked the public to buy shares in an Indian steelworks.

[conversational] Selling shares, by the way, just means selling small pieces of the company. Each buyer gets a slice of whatever the company earns. [delighted] And people bought. The company raised about two point three crore rupees from roughly eight thousand Indian investors, in a matter of weeks.

[proudly] On the sixteenth of February 1912, the first steel ingot rolled out at Sakchi. [matter-of-fact] Two years later, the First World War began, and suddenly the British Empire needed all the steel it could get. Tata steel went into rails for the war in Mesopotamia and elsewhere. [warmly] In 1919, the town around the works was renamed after the man who had dreamed it. [short pause] Jamshedpur.

[satisfied] The other ambitions followed. A hydroelectric company was set up in 1910, and by 1915 water power from the hills above Bombay was running the city's mills. And the Indian Institute of Science, in Bangalore, took its first students in 1911.

[serious] But success brought its own crisis. After the war, demand fell away, cheap foreign steel flooded in, and by 1924 the steel company was in serious trouble. [grave] At one point it didn't have the cash to pay its workers. [emphatic] So Dorabji Tata pledged his personal fortune to the bank, including his wife Meherbai's famous Jubilee diamond, one of the largest diamonds in the world, to get a loan. [relieved] The wages were paid. The government then put a tariff on imported steel, which is a tax that makes foreign goods dearer, and Tata Steel survived.

[leaning in] Now, here's why the company is owned the way it is. Jamsetji's sons both left their wealth to charitable trusts. Ratanji's trust was set up in 1919, and when Dorabji died in 1932, his fortune, including his shares in Tata Sons, went into a trust too. [explaining] Tata Sons is the company at the very top, the one that owns big stakes in all the others. So when the companies make profits, a share flows up to Tata Sons, and Tata Sons pays dividends, a cut of its profits, to the trusts. And the trusts spend it. On a cancer hospital in Bombay, opened in 1941. On a research institute for fundamental science, founded in 1945. [thoughtful] It's a little like a farm whose harvest belongs to the village.

[warmly] After a short spell under Jamsetji's nephew, Nowroji Saklatwala, the next chairman, J. R. D. Tata, ran the group for over fifty years, from 1938 to 1991. J. R. D. was R. D. Tata's son, the cousin's branch from our family map. [delighted] He was a pilot, and in 1932 he flew the first flight of Tata Airlines himself, carrying mail from Karachi to Bombay. [rueful] That airline was nationalised in 1953 and became Air India. [matter-of-fact] Under him the group started making trucks and chemicals, and in 1968, a small computer services unit called Tata Consultancy Services.

## ending

[thoughtful] Where does it stand now? In 1991, Ratan Tata took over, just as India opened its economy to the world. He was Naval Tata's son, from the adopted branch of the family, and the fifth generation of our story. [serious] Many of the group's companies had grown used to a protected home market, and he set about pulling them together and pushing them outwards. [quickening] Tetley Tea in 2000, [dry, amused] a British company far bigger than the Tata tea business that bought it. [building] The Anglo-Dutch steelmaker Corus in 2007, for around twelve billion dollars. Jaguar Land Rover in 2008, bought from Ford for a little over two billion.

[matter-of-fact] Not every move went well. The Nano, sold as the world's cheapest car, never found its buyers. The Corus deal left Tata Steel carrying heavy debts and loss-making plants in Europe. [grave] And in 2016, the board's very public removal of chairman Cyrus Mistry turned into a long legal fight that went all the way to India's Supreme Court, which ruled in the Tatas' favour in 2021.

[quietly impressed] But Jaguar Land Rover, which many people thought was a mistake when Tata bought it, turned into one of the group's biggest earners. Tata Consultancy Services, the little computer unit from 1968, grew into one of the largest IT services companies in the world. [warmly] And in 2022, Air India came home to the Tatas, almost seventy years after it was taken from them.

[softly] Ratan Tata died in October 2024. His half-brother, Noel Tata, now chairs the trusts. [matter-of-fact] Today the group, led by N. Chandrasekaran since 2017, has around thirty listed companies, works in more than a hundred countries and employs over a million people. And the trusts still own about two thirds of Tata Sons.

[thoughtful] What did it take? A founder who went after the things India lacked rather than the things that were easiest to sell. Sons who finished his work after he was gone, and who put their own fortunes on the line when the steelworks nearly sank. [emphatic] And a decision to hand the profits to the country instead of the family.

[warmly] Twenty-one thousand rupees in 1868. [pause] A steel town named after the man who never saw it. [softly] And a business that, more than a century and a half later, still pays for hospitals and science labs every time it makes a profit.
