## hook

[leaning in] About one pound in every four that British families spend on groceries is spent at one company. Tesco. It sells food, clothes, petrol, phones and insurance, and it employs hundreds of thousands of people.

[warmly] It began in 1919, with a young man standing behind a wooden stall in a street market in east London, shouting prices at passers-by. [curious] He had just come back from the war, he had thirty pounds to his name, and he spent all of it on food that nobody else wanted.

[thoughtful] This is the story of how that stall became Britain's biggest retailer, how it nearly lost its way more than once, and the small plastic card that changed everything.

## beginnings

[warmly] A few people to know before we start. [short pause] Jack Cohen, the founder, a market trader from London's East End. T. E. Stockwell, a tea supplier whose initials ended up in the company's name. Then, much later, two bosses who reshaped Tesco: Ian MacLaurin, who saved it in the 1970s, and Terry Leahy, who made it a giant. [matter-of-fact] That's the cast.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. Jack Cohen was born in 1898 in Whitechapel, a poor, crowded part of east London. His parents were Jewish immigrants from Poland, and his father was a tailor. [softly] Money was always tight. Jack left school young and started working with his father.

[serious] In the First World War, he joined the Royal Flying Corps, the forerunner of the air force, and served abroad, in Egypt and Palestine. He caught malaria, and nearly died.

[determined] When he came home in 1919, the government gave him a small sum for his service, about thirty pounds. [curious] He spent it on surplus food, tins and goods left over from the army, which were being sold off cheap. Then he hired a barrow, and set up a stall at a street market in Hackney.

[delighted] On his first day, the story goes, he took four pounds and made one pound of profit. He was hooked.

[thoughtful] Jack worked the London markets for years, buying cheap and selling cheap. He had one rule above all others, and he repeated it so often that it became famous: pile it high, sell it cheap. [knowingly] Make a small profit on each thing, but sell an enormous number of things.

[with a smile] In 1924, he started selling his own brand of tea. He bought it from a supplier called T. E. Stockwell. [pause] And to make a label, he took Stockwell's initials, T E S, and added the first two letters of his own surname, C O. Tesco.

## struggle

[building] The name went on shops. Jack opened his first Tesco stores around 1930 in the London suburbs, small shops with low prices, and by the Second World War he had about a hundred of them.

[curious] After the war, he crossed the Atlantic and saw something new in American shops: self-service. Customers walked around with baskets and picked what they wanted off the shelves themselves, instead of asking an assistant behind a counter for each thing. [determined] He brought the idea home, and in the late 1940s Tesco opened one of Britain's first self-service shops, in St Albans. Shoppers were suspicious at first. Then they loved it.

[serious] In the 1960s, Jack fought a long battle with suppliers. Back then, the law let manufacturers fix the price shops charged for their goods, so shops could not undercut each other. Jack hated it, and broke the rules where he could, and campaigned against them. [delighted] In 1964 the law changed, and discounters like Tesco were free to cut prices.

[grave] But by the 1970s, Tesco had a problem. It had grown big, but it felt cheap and cluttered, and shoppers associated it with piles of goods and little else. It also relied on trading stamps, little stamps given out with every purchase, which customers stuck in books and swapped for gifts. [thoughtful] Everyone's prices included the cost of those stamps, and Tesco's profits were falling.

[determined] A young manager called Ian MacLaurin believed the stamps had to go. In 1977 Tesco dropped them overnight, and used the money to cut prices on hundreds of products across every shop at once. They called it Operation Checkout. [delighted] It was a huge gamble, and it worked. Customers flooded in, so many that some stores ran out of stock.

[serious] Jack Cohen died in 1979. He had built something enormous. [pause] But Tesco was still seen as the cheap option, a long way behind the more upmarket Sainsbury's.

## rise

[curious] Then, in the 1990s, Tesco tried something new. A plastic card. Clubcard. Every time you shopped, you swiped it, and got a small reward back in vouchers.

[thoughtful] The rewards were not the clever part. The clever part was the information. For the first time, Tesco could see exactly what each household bought, how often, and when. [knowingly] Who bought nappies, so probably had a baby. Who bought dog food. Who was trying to eat healthily. It used this to send each family offers for the things they actually bought, and to decide what each store should stock.

[building] In 1995, the year Clubcard launched, Tesco overtook Sainsbury's to become the biggest food retailer in Britain. Its slogan, every little helps, became one of the most familiar phrases in the country. [quietly impressed] Under Terry Leahy, its boss from 1997, it grew into clothes, electronics, banking and petrol, and opened stores across Europe and Asia.

[grave] It overreached. A huge push into America, with a chain called Fresh and Easy, failed and cost about a billion pounds. Then, in 2014, Tesco admitted it had overstated its profits by more than two hundred and fifty million pounds, and its bosses faced investigations. Its share price collapsed.

[determined] A new boss cut costs, sold off foreign businesses, and focused on doing groceries well again. [quietly impressed] It worked. Tesco steadied, and kept its place at the top.

## ending

[warmly] Today Tesco is still the biggest retailer in Britain, with thousands of shops, and a share of the grocery market that rivals can only dream of.

[reflective] What made it? Three big bets, at three different times. Jack Cohen's pile it high, sell it cheap, which got it started. Ian MacLaurin's decision to scrap the stamps and cut prices, which saved it. And a small plastic card that let Tesco know its customers better than they knew themselves, which made it a giant.

[thoughtful] And twice, getting too big too fast nearly undid it, once in America and once in its own accounts.

[softly] From a barrow in a Hackney street market, bought with a soldier's thirty pounds, to the shop most British families walk into every week.
