## hook

[leaning in] In almost every corner shop in India, and in Indian grocery shops from London to Dubai, there is a row of bright packets with one name on them: Haldiram's. Inside, crunchy, spicy, salty things to eat with a cup of tea.

[warmly] It all started with one snack, in one desert town, made by a young man who thought his family's recipe could be better. [curious] The snack was bhujia, thin crispy strands made from bean flour and spices, fried until they crackle. The town was Bikaner, in Rajasthan, in the dry north-west of India. And the young man's name was Ganga Bishan Agarwal, though almost nobody ever called him that.

[thoughtful] This is the story of how a sweet shop's snack became a national brand, how a family grew it and then split it three ways, and how hard it is to keep a taste the same when you are making it by the tonne.

## beginnings

[warmly] Here's the family you need to know before we start. [short pause] Ganga Bishan Agarwal, known by the nickname his mother gave him, Haldiram, who is the founder of this story. Before him, his grandfather, who ran a small shop selling snacks in Bikaner. And after him, his grandsons, three of whom matter here. Shiv Kishan Agrawal, who took the brand to the city of Nagpur. And two brothers, Manohar Lal and Madhusudan Agarwal, who took it to Delhi. [matter-of-fact] There's a branch in Kolkata too. That's the family. Four generations, one recipe.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. Bikaner, in the early twentieth century, was a walled town on the edge of the Thar desert, ruled by a maharaja. It was hot, dry and dusty, and its people were famous for one thing above most others: bhujia. Families there had been making it for generations. It was cheap, it kept for days in the heat, and almost everyone ate it.

[curious] Haldiram grew up in the family shop, helping out from childhood. [thoughtful] And he noticed something about the bhujia everyone made. It was thick and quite soft. It was good, but it was the same as everybody else's.

[determined] So, as a young man, he started experimenting. The way the family tells it, he made the strands much thinner, so they were crisper and lighter. He changed the flour, using more moth bean, a small desert bean that gave a sharper, nuttier taste. And he got the spice just so. [delighted] People noticed the difference immediately. His bhujia was the one they asked for.

[with a smile] He was also a natural salesman. The family story goes that he named his bhujia after Dungar Singh, a much-loved maharaja of Bikaner, and called it Dungar Sev. It sounded grand. It sold even faster. Soon the shop was known by his own nickname: Haldiram's.

[serious] But a snack shop in a small desert town could only sell so much. Bikaner was far from India's big cities, and most of India had never tasted Bikaner bhujia. [pause] The next step was getting it out of the desert.

## struggle

[thoughtful] The first move came in the 1950s. Haldiram travelled to Kolkata, then called Calcutta, a huge city on the other side of India, to attend a wedding. He saw an opportunity, and the family opened a shop there. [delighted] City people who had never tasted Bikaner bhujia loved it.

[curious] Over the next decades, the family kept moving. In 1970, Shiv Kishan Agrawal, one of Haldiram's grandsons, opened in Nagpur, a city in central India. In the early 1980s, his brothers Manohar Lal and Madhusudan opened in Delhi, the capital, the biggest market of all.

[serious] And here was the hard part. Bhujia made in a small shop, by people who had made it all their lives, tasted wonderful. But how do you make the same taste in a factory, by the tonne, every single day, in different cities? [thoughtful] The quality of the flour changes. The weather changes how dough behaves. A new cook fries for a few seconds too long. Every batch can drift a little, and customers notice.

[determined] So the family began to turn a recipe into a process. They bought machines to cut the strands to the same thickness every time. They measured spices by weight instead of by hand. They worked out exactly how hot the oil should be and for how long.

[grave] There was a second problem: snacks go soft. A packet of bhujia that sits in a humid shop for a month is not crunchy any more. So they moved to packaging that kept the air and the damp out, so a packet opened in a shop far from Bikaner, weeks later, still crackled.

[serious] And then there was a third problem, the hardest of all: the family itself. Each branch was run by a different part of the family, in a different city, all using the name Haldiram's. [grave] Over the years, they argued, and went to court, over who had the right to the name, and where. One branch, in Kolkata, eventually lost the right to use it there and built a new brand of its own. [pause] It was the kind of family split that has destroyed many Indian businesses. But in this case, something different happened.

## rise

[building] The brand kept growing anyway. Each branch pushed into its own part of India: Delhi across the north, Nagpur across the west and south. [knowingly] And in a way, the split helped. Each part of the family was hungry to win its own region, and they built factories, distribution networks and shops faster than one company might have.

[delighted] They didn't stop at bhujia. Haldiram's added hundreds of snacks: spicy peanuts, crunchy mixtures of lentils and nuts, sweets like soft syrupy gulab jamun and rasgulla sold in tins. They opened restaurants serving north Indian street food and sweets, in shopping centres and on busy streets.

[curious] And they took the taste abroad. Indians living in Britain, America, the Gulf and Singapore wanted the snacks they grew up with, and Haldiram's packets started appearing on shelves in Indian grocery shops around the world.

[quietly impressed] By the 2010s, Haldiram's was selling more traditional Indian snacks than any other company, and competing with the biggest international snack brands on supermarket shelves. [thoughtful] It worked because it never tried to be something it wasn't. It sold the taste Indians already loved, made reliably, packed well, and available everywhere.

## ending

[warmly] In recent years, the Delhi and Nagpur sides of the family brought their businesses together into one company. And in 2025, they sold a share of it to Temasek, Singapore's government investment company, in a deal that reportedly valued Haldiram's at around ten billion dollars.

[thoughtful] Ten billion dollars. From a family shop in a desert town, selling a snack that cost a few coins.

[reflective] What made the difference? Not a secret ingredient. Haldiram's bhujia was a better version of something everyone already ate. Then his grandsons did the harder, less romantic work: turning a recipe into a process, so that a packet in Delhi or in London tasted like the shop in Bikaner. [softly] And even a family fight over the name could not stop people buying it.

[warmly] Next time you see that row of bright packets on a shop shelf, remember where it began: a young man in the desert, cutting the strands a little thinner than his grandfather did.
