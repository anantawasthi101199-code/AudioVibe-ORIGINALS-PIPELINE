## hook

[leaning in] In 1984, a man who ran a record company decided to start an airline. He had one second-hand jumbo jet, leased for a year. [curious] Most people in the business thought it was a joke.

[warmly] That man was Richard Branson, and the name on the plane was Virgin, the same name as his record shops. Over the next forty years, that one word went onto trains, phones, banks, gyms, cola, wedding dresses and rockets.

[thoughtful] This is the story of how Virgin began, how one brand jumped from industry to industry, which of those jumps worked, which crashed, and why a famous name alone was never enough.

[warmly] Hey, I'm Adrian, and this is Business Decoded.

## beginnings

[warmly] One main person to know: Richard Branson, the founder. Everyone else comes and goes. [matter-of-fact] That's the cast.

[warmly] So let's go back to the very beginning. Branson was born in 1950 in Surrey, south of London. His father was a lawyer, and his mother was a former dancer and air hostess who pushed her children to be bold. [softly] Richard had dyslexia, and struggled at school.

[determined] At sixteen, he left school to run a magazine called Student, written by and for young people. He sold the adverts himself, from a basement, on the phone. [curious] It barely made money. But he noticed something: young people spent a fortune on records, and records were expensive in shops.

[delighted] So in 1970 he started selling records cheaply by post. He and his friends needed a name. [with a smile] Someone pointed out they were all complete beginners at business. Virgins. And the name stuck.

[thoughtful] In 1971, a postal strike nearly killed the mail-order business, so Branson opened a record shop, above a shoe shop on Oxford Street in London. [serious] That same year he was caught avoiding tax on records he claimed were for export, and was arrested. He settled by paying the money back, and his parents put their house up to help. He has said it was the lesson that kept him honest.

[curious] Then a bigger idea. In 1972 he bought a country house in Oxfordshire and turned it into a recording studio. Next, a record label, so musicians who recorded there could release their music with him. [delighted] Its very first album, in 1973, was Tubular Bells, by a shy young musician called Mike Oldfield. It sold millions of copies. Virgin Records was up and running.

## struggle

[building] But it didn't stay easy for long. Records are a hit-and-miss business. For every big seller, there were flops. [with a smile] In 1977 Virgin signed the Sex Pistols, a punk band other labels had dropped, which brought headlines and trouble in equal measure. Later came stars like Culture Club and Phil Collins.

[curious] Then, in 1984, came the airline. A lawyer pitched Branson an idea for a cheap airline flying across the Atlantic. [thoughtful] Branson had tried to book a flight once, been told it was cancelled, and chartered a plane himself, selling the spare seats to the other stranded passengers. Now he wanted to do it properly. In June 1984, Virgin Atlantic flew its first flight, from London Gatwick to Newark, near New York, in that leased jumbo jet.

[serious] Airlines need enormous amounts of money, and Virgin was up against British Airways, the giant national carrier. [grave] In the early 1990s, it came out that British Airways had run what became known as the dirty tricks campaign, which included approaching Virgin's passengers to persuade them to switch. In 1993, BA apologised in court and paid damages. [with a smile] Branson shared his money among Virgin's staff.

[serious] But the airline was burning through cash. In 1992, to save it, Branson made the hardest choice of his career. He sold Virgin Records, the business that started it all, to the music company EMI, for around five hundred and sixty million pounds. [softly] He said afterwards that he wept.

## rise

[building] And then came the move that changed everything. With money from the sale, Branson used the Virgin name as a key to open any industry he liked. [thoughtful] He had learned something from the airline. People saw Virgin as the cheeky outsider, the young, fun alternative to the big, boring companies. That feeling could travel.

[knowingly] Here's how it worked. Branson would pick an industry where customers felt badly treated by big, dull incumbents. Then Virgin would arrive, often with a partner who knew the business and put in most of the money, and Virgin would bring the name, the style and the publicity, usually with Branson himself doing a stunt. [with a smile] He drove a tank through New York's Times Square to launch a cola, and wore a wedding dress to launch a bridal shop.

[delighted] Some of it worked brilliantly. Virgin Mobile, launched in 1999, used other companies' phone networks and won millions of young customers. Virgin Trains ran the West Coast line from London to Scotland for about twenty years. Virgin Money became a high-street bank. Virgin Media became one of Britain's biggest cable and broadband companies.

[serious] And some of it failed. Virgin Cola could not dent Coca-Cola, and faded away. Virgin Brides closed. The Virgin Megastores, the big record shops, were sold off in 2007 as music moved online, and soon closed. [thoughtful] The lesson was clear. A famous name can get customers to try something once. It cannot make a bad product good, or beat a rival who is better at the actual business.

[grave] Space was the riskiest bet of all. Branson founded Virgin Galactic in 2004 to fly tourists to the edge of space. In 2014, its test spaceplane broke up in the air, and the co-pilot, Michael Alsbury, was killed. [determined] In July 2021, Branson finally flew to the edge of space himself, aged seventy. But a sister company that launched satellites, Virgin Orbit, went bankrupt in 2023.

[serious] And the pandemic in 2020 nearly sank the airline again, with planes grounded around the world. It was rescued by a private deal worth more than a billion pounds, and Branson sold shares in his space company to help pay for it.

## ending

[warmly] Today the Virgin Group is a family of dozens of companies around the world, many of them partly owned by others, who pay to use the name. Virgin Atlantic still flies across the Atlantic, part-owned by the American airline Delta. [quietly impressed] And Branson, knighted in 2000, is one of the best-known businesspeople on Earth.

[reflective] What did it take? A name that made people feel they were backing the underdog. A founder willing to put himself in a wedding dress or a hot-air balloon to get attention. And the hard lesson, learned more than once, that the name gets you in the door, but only a good business keeps you in the room.

[softly] From a sixteen-year-old selling adverts from a basement, and a group of friends who admitted they were beginners, to a name on aeroplanes, trains and spaceships.
