Business Decoded: The World's Businesses, Country by Country
A 10-series global documentary project · 10 countries · Audio-first business storytelling
This version gives each country its own dedicated series, rather than grouping countries by region. The first four will be India, the UK, the USA and Canada, exactly as requested.
Each series explores the country's distinctive business history, from family businesses and industrial pioneers to modern startups, global brands and controversial corporate empires. Every episode tells one company's complete story, from its beginnings to its present-day position, in approximately 12–15 minutes.

How the series fit together
The project moves from India's industrial and entrepreneurial development to Britain's industrial and commercial heritage, then to America's mass-market business models. Canada follows as a study of building businesses alongside a larger neighbour.
The next three series explore East Asian manufacturing, technology and cultural industries. Germany and France then provide contrasting European perspectives on engineering and luxury, before Brazil brings the project back to entrepreneurship in a large emerging market.
The order is a listening journey, not a claim that one country developed in isolation from the others. Many of the companies depend on international supply chains, investment, technologies and markets.
13. Coverage and research safeguards
Before turning each outline into a script, verify the company's history and current position. The roadmap is a proposed editorial plan, not a completed fact-check of every company.
- Avoid founder mythology. Include the contributions of co-founders, employees, investors, suppliers and predecessors.
- Explain the money. Describe revenue, costs, financing, pricing and the business model in plain English.
- Include setbacks. Cover failed products, leadership disputes, market shocks, debt and strategic mistakes where the evidence supports them.
- Address controversies fairly. Distinguish legal findings, allegations, corporate statements and unresolved questions.
- Check present-day facts. Ownership, leadership, financial performance and product strategy can change after an episode is drafted.
- Keep country identity meaningful. Include companies founded or developed in the country, while explaining multinational ownership and international origins where relevant.
- Avoid repetitive stories. Even when two businesses use franchising or low prices, their episodes should explore different obstacles, decisions and outcomes.
Recommended research starting points
For the first four series, these official sources are useful entry points:
- India: Tata Group's historical timelines, Reliance's history and Infosys' company history.
- UK: Tesco's history and Rolls-Royce's history.
- USA: Walmart's corporate history, Apple Investor Relations and Amazon's corporate website.
- Canada: Shopify, Lululemon's history and RBC's history.
For the remaining countries, use the same research standard: company archives for original milestones, annual reports for financial information, reputable business journalism for independent perspectives, and court or regulatory records when controversies form a central part of the story.
14. Production format for every episode
Keep a consistent structure across all ten series so listeners recognise the Business Decoded format.
1. The hook — 30–45 seconds. Open with a striking business problem, a turning point or a decision that nearly destroyed the company.
2. The beginning — 2 minutes. Introduce the founder, the original idea and the market in which the business emerged.
3. The struggle — 3 minutes. Explore early obstacles, cash shortages, competitors and the decisions that determined whether the company survived.
4. The turning point — 3 minutes. Explain the innovation, strategy or business model that changed the company's trajectory.
5. The rise and the setbacks — 3–4 minutes. Cover expansion, competition, crises, acquisitions and the consequences of success.
6. The company today — 1–2 minutes. Explain its current position, the lessons supported by evidence and a natural reason to follow the channel.
Each full episode should have a companion three-minute short telling the same company's story in compressed form, from its beginnings through the major turning point to its present-day position.
The narration should be conversational, clear and easy to follow without visuals. Explain unfamiliar business concepts as they arise, use verified details to make the story memorable, and avoid invented dialogue or exaggerated claims.
The final vision: Business Decoded becomes a global library of company stories, organised country by country. Listeners can explore a nation's business history through individual founders and companies, while discovering the strategies, mistakes and turning points that connect businesses across the world
