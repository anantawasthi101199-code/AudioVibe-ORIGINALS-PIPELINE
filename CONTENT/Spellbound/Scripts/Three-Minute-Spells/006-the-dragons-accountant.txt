## setup

[warmly] The client was four hundred and twelve years old, and Nell Ashby, of Ashby and Daughter, Chartered Accountants, had been sent up a mountain to count his gold.

The rules of the job came in a letter, in beautiful old handwriting. A hoard may only be counted by somebody its owner trusts, and he must say so out loud. Meetings after dark. Come alone. And one line underlined twice: the heating is always on, so please don't open any windows.

[steady] Nell was twenty-nine, and nervous of most things, including mountains. But the fee would pay off her mother's mortgage. So on a cold Tuesday in November she climbed to the cave with a briefcase, a torch and a calculator, and knocked on a very large iron door.

"Come in," said a deep voice. "Mind the step. And do shut the door, I feel the cold terribly."

## turn

[warmly] It was the warmest room she had ever been in. A long hall, cut into the rock, with a red glow coming from somewhere at the far end, and gold everywhere. Gold coins in drifts up to the ceiling. Gold cups, gold crowns, a gold bathtub. The client stayed in the shadows by the glow, a tall shape in an armchair, and called himself Mr Vane.

"Is the place in order?" said Mr Vane.

Nell opened the ledgers. Four hundred years of them, bound in leather. She worked all night.

[steady] It was very strange. The money came in, century after century, from old loans, old mines, old kings who owed him things. But nothing ever went out. Not a coin. Not in four hundred years. The only spending in the whole of the books was one line, every winter, in the same old handwriting: candles, two.

"Mr Vane," said Nell, at four in the morning, "you've never spent anything. Not a thing. Why not?"

The shape in the armchair was quiet for a long time.

"Who would I spend it on?" said Mr Vane.

## twist

[pause] Then he leaned forward into the light, and Nell finally saw where the heat was coming from. It was him. A long golden head, and a slow curl of smoke from his nostrils, and a tail wrapped three times round the hall like a warm red pipe. The heating had always been on, because the heating was a dragon.

Nell did not run. She looked at the ledgers, and at the cold valley below, where the village shivered every winter.

"Mr Vane," said Nell, "say you trust me, out loud. I have a business plan."

By January, Ashby and Vane was heating the whole valley, and the dragon had a friend to spend it on.
