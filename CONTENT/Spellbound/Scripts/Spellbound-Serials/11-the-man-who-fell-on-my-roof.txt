## cold_open

[warmly] At eleven minutes past nine on a Tuesday night, something fell out of the sky and landed in Zoe Park's tomatoes.

She heard it from the kitchen, under the thunder: one long crash, a clang of plant pots, then the whole building shivering down to the flour bins. Zoe picked up the nearest heavy thing, which was a rolling pin, and went up the stairs to the roof.

[steady] Zoe was twenty-seven. She ran Park's Bakery in the seaside town of Harbor Bay. It had been her grandmother's for forty years, and it had three months left before the landlord took it back. She had a broken oven, a letter from the bank, and no time for whatever this was.

## story

[warmly] The rain came sideways across the roof. In the middle of her ruined tomato bed sat a very tall man, wrapped in the tablecloth she'd left on the line. He had no shoes on. He was holding one hand out, palm up, and watching the water land in it with deep suspicion.

"Don't move," said Zoe, and held up the rolling pin.

He looked at the rolling pin. He looked at her. He had a long, serious face, and a band of freckles across his nose.

"What is this for?" he said, and he nodded up at the sky.

Zoe lowered the pin a little. "The rain?"

"Rain," he said, as if tasting it. "Why does it fall?"

[steady] It was, she thought later, the concussion that made her feel sorry for him. It had to be concussion. He had just dropped through a storm and he didn't have a mark on him, but she decided that was a question for a day when she had slept. She asked his name.

He took a moment. "Ryan," he said. And then, carefully, like a man reading off a card, "From Ohio."

As he said the word Ohio, the freckles across his nose lit up. It lasted about a second, a faint blue, the colour of a gas ring turned down low. Zoe blinked the rain out of her eyes, and they were only freckles.

"Right," said Zoe. "Well, Ryan from Ohio, you can't stay in my tomatoes."

[warmly] She took him downstairs through the bakery, because the alternative was calling Tess Moreno. Tess was her best friend and the town's only deputy, and Tess would want paperwork. He walked behind her through the dark shop, ducking under the lamp. At the till he slowed down.

Gran's lucky stone sat there by the tip jar, where it had sat for as long as Zoe could remember. It was a smooth blue thing the size of a hen's egg, and customers rubbed it for luck. As Ryan passed it, it began to hum.

Zoe felt it before she heard it. It was a small, high note, right in her back teeth. She put a hand to her jaw. She had never had a toothache. She had never had so much as a cold, in twenty-seven years. Gran used to say she had the constitution of a brick.

"Do you hear that?" said Zoe.

Ryan had stopped dead. He was staring at the stone, and he took one step back from it, then another. The note died away.

"I hear nothing," said Ryan, and the blue flickered across his nose again.

[steady] He ended up in the attic, on Gran's old camp bed, under a poster of the Harbor Bay Regatta of 1987. He looked at the pillow for some time, then picked it up and asked what it was for. Zoe told him it was for his head. He nodded gravely and put it on his head.

She left him there. She didn't know what else to do. She went down to the kitchen and stood in front of the oven, which had been dead for nine days, and a repair quote stuck to its door said four thousand dollars. She put her forehead against it and stayed there. Tomorrow was the Founders' Day order, sixty loaves and a tray of lemon tarts. She had one working oven the size of a shoebox.

"Is this one unwell?"

[warmly] He had come down the stairs without a sound, still in the tablecloth. He stood next to her and studied the oven with the same polite concern he had given the rain. Then he laid two long fingers on the door.

There was a soft click from deep inside. The little orange light came on, the one that hadn't come on since the start of the month. The fan started. A smell of warm metal and old sugar rolled out into the room, and the dial crept round by itself to exactly the temperature Gran had always used for bread.

It had taken about ten seconds.

Zoe looked at the oven. She looked at the man. She took the repair quote off the door, folded it in half and dropped it in the bin.

"You can have the attic," said Zoe. "Rent's due on Friday, and I'm going to have questions."

"I also have questions," said Ryan. "Why is the small round one called a muffin?"

For the first time in about three months, Zoe Park laughed out loud.

## cliffhanger

[steady] She turned on the little radio to keep her company while the oven heated. Late news, the same tired voice as always. Traffic on the coast road. Storm damage at the marina. And a meteor strike, the announcer said, reported just off Harbor Bay at around nine o'clock, though the coast guard had searched the water and found no debris of any kind.

Behind her, the room went quiet.

Zoe turned round. Ryan was standing by the door, not moving, listening to the radio as if it were speaking to him by name. The long, puzzled look had gone from his face. So had the blue. His eyes had turned a flat, bright silver, like two new coins.

[pause] "They've found the crash," he said. "Which means they'll find me."
