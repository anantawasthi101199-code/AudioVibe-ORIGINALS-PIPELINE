## cold_open

[steady] "They've found the crash," said Ryan. "Which means they'll find me."

Zoe Park stood in her bakery kitchen at midnight with a rolling pin still on the counter beside her, looking at a man whose eyes had just turned silver. The radio went on talking about the storm. The oven ticked as it heated.

[warmly] Last time, a stranger fell out of a thunderstorm into the tomato bed on Zoe's roof. He said his name was Ryan, from Ohio, and he fixed her dead oven with two fingers. Zoe is twenty-seven, she runs her late grandmother's bakery in the seaside town of Harbor Bay, and she has three months to save it from the landlord. She does not need a fugitive in her attic.

"Who's they?" said Zoe.

The silver faded from his eyes. "People who ask questions," said Ryan. "Do you want me to leave?"

Zoe thought about the oven, warm for the first time in nine days. "Ask me again in the morning," she said.

## story

[warmly] In the morning, he came downstairs with a gold bar.

He put it on the counter beside the till, very carefully, the way you'd put down a sleeping cat. It was the size of a paperback and it made the counter creak.

"For the rent," said Ryan. "Is it enough?"

Zoe stared at it. "Where did you get that?"

"I had it," said Ryan, which was not an answer, and the freckles across his nose gave one faint blue flicker. Zoe had decided she wasn't going to think about the freckles yet. She had enough to think about.

[steady] It solved the landlord. It did not solve anything else. At eleven o'clock the lady at the bank turned it over in both hands and asked, very politely, where it had come from, and whether it had papers, and whether Zoe would mind waiting while she made a phone call. Zoe did mind. She took it home in a tote bag and put it under the flour sacks. So the bakery still had to earn its own way out of trouble, one loaf at a time, and the only extra help she had was a man who had never seen bread before Tuesday.

[warmly] She gave him an apron. He put it on backwards, then forwards, and stood behind the counter looking deeply pleased with himself. Every morning before work he made a cup of tea from a little silver pouch he kept in his pocket, a pale green tea that smelled of pepper and rain, and only after that would he eat anything at all. On the third morning he drank his tea, picked a whole lemon out of the fruit bowl, and ate it, peel and all, in four bites, in front of a queue of customers. A man waiting for his sourdough quietly put his hat back on and left.

"Too sour?" said Zoe, through her teeth.

"Wonderful," said Ryan. "It argues with you."

[steady] But that afternoon she gave him a piping bag, mostly to stop him eating the fruit, and he turned out to be brilliant. Not good. Brilliant. He iced a tray of birthday biscuits without once looking at what he was doing, and every one came out as fine as lace: tiny stars, tiny moons, a seagull so real Zoe wanted to shoo it off the plate. The schoolchildren came in after three o'clock and bought the lot.

"Where did you learn that?" said Zoe.

"Where I come from, we draw the sky," said Ryan. "Your sky is very simple. Only one moon."

[warmly] That evening she found him in the back room, sitting on an upturned crate with Gran's recipe notebook open on his knees. It was a fat old book with a cracked red cover and forty years of flour in its spine. Zoe had never let anybody touch it. She opened her mouth to tell him off, and didn't, because of the look on his face. He wasn't reading the recipes. He was turning the pages, very slowly, and staring at the little doodles in the margins: tiny stars, five-pointed, in Gran's blue ink, beside the scones and the plum cake and the lemon tart. There were dozens of them. Zoe had always thought they were just Gran's way of saying good.

"She drew these," said Ryan. It wasn't quite a question.

"She drew them on everything," said Zoe. "Why?"

He closed the book gently and handed it back. He didn't answer. He sat for a whole hour after that, she noticed, looking at nothing.

[steady] On Saturday morning the bell over the door rang, and in came Brenda Vance. Brenda ran Vance's Patisserie across the square, all pink paint and gold lettering. She had been telling Harbor Bay for five years that Park's Bakery was a sweet old shop that would close any day now. She came in once a month to see if it had.

"Still open!" said Brenda brightly. "Good for you, Zoe. Still doing your gran's little tarts?" She looked at Ryan in his apron. "And you've hired a giant."

"Try one," said Ryan, and held out a lemon tart on a napkin, iced with a single silver star.

Brenda took it to be polite. She took one bite. Then she stopped talking, which in five years Zoe had never seen. She finished the tart in two more bites and stood there with crumbs on her lip.

[warmly] "Who made this?" said Brenda.

"My grandmother's recipe," said Zoe. "Our kitchen."

Brenda Vance opened her handbag. "I'll take twelve," she said, very quietly, and then, as Zoe boxed them up, "for my mother. They're for my mother." She paid in cash and left without looking at either of them, and the moment the door shut Zoe sat down on the floor behind the counter and laughed until she cried.

"Was that a victory?" said Ryan, worried.

"That," said Zoe, "was the best day of my life."

[steady] Late that night she couldn't sleep, so she went up to the attic with two mugs of cocoa. Ryan was asleep on Gran's camp bed with one arm hanging off the side, palm up. On the inside of his wrist, under the skin, there was a row of tiny lights, like grains of sugar lit from inside. Zoe counted them without meaning to. Thirty. As the church clock down in the square began to strike midnight, the last light in the row went out. Twenty-nine.

She stood there holding both mugs until the cocoa went cold. Then she put his on the floor beside him, and went back downstairs, and didn't ask him in the morning. She wasn't sure she wanted the answer.

## cliffhanger

[steady] On Monday it rained. The bakery was warm and full and smelled of cinnamon, and Ryan was at the back counter rolling dough, white with flour to his elbows, humming something with no tune that made the jam jars rattle.

The bell over the door rang. A man came in out of the rain in a grey suit, a grey raincoat and a grey hat, and the queue made room for him without being asked. He came straight to the counter. He took a small leather wallet out of his inside pocket and let it fall open.

[pause] "Agent Ray Graves," he said. "I'm asking around town about unusual new arrivals. Anyone come to stay with you lately, miss?"

Zoe didn't look round. She didn't need to. Two feet behind her, covered in flour, stood the most unusual new arrival in the history of Harbor Bay.
