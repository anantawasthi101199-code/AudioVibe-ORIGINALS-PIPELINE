## hook

[warmly] On Christmas Eve 1914, five months into the worst war the world had yet seen, German soldiers began to put candles along the tops of their trenches. This is the story of the Christmas Truce, on the Western Front, in Belgium and northern France.

## story

[measured] The war had been meant to be over by then. Instead, millions of men were dug into wet ditches running from the Channel to Switzerland, sometimes less than a hundred yards apart. Between them lay no man's land, with bodies from the autumn fighting still lying out in the mud. A hard frost came in that week, and for the first time in weeks, the ground was dry.

[steady] That evening, along stretches of the line, British soldiers saw small lights appearing, and little trees set up on the edge of the German trenches. Then they heard singing. Stille Nacht, Silent Night. Some British units sang back. Voices called across. In the morning, in place after place, men climbed out, unarmed, and walked into the open.

[warmly] We know what happened next from their own letters home, many of them printed in British newspapers that January. They shook hands. They swapped cigarettes, buttons, tins of bully beef and sausages. One German who had worked as a barber in London cut hair. Above all, they used the time to bury their dead, side by side, and at least one joint service was held over the graves. There are mentions in the letters of a kickabout with a football, and historians still argue over how much of that happened, and where. It was not the whole front. In some sectors, men were shot that same day.

## close

[thoughtful] The generals on both sides were furious, and orders came down that it must never happen again. In most places it never did on that scale, and the war went on for nearly four more years. Many of the men who shook hands that morning were killed. What they left is in the letters, and in a small wooden cross, put up near Ploegsteert in Belgium in 1999, to mark the place.
