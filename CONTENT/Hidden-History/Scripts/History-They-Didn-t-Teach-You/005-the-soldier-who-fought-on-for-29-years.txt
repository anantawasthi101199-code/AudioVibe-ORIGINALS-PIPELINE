## hook

[measured] In March 1974, almost twenty-nine years after the Second World War ended, a Japanese army officer walked out of the jungle in the Philippines in his old uniform, with his rifle oiled and ready, and finally surrendered.

[steady] His name was Hiroo Onoda, and this is the story of why he kept fighting a war that was over, on Lubang, a small island south-west of Manila.

## story

[steady] Onoda was a young intelligence officer, trained in guerrilla warfare. In December 1944 he was sent to Lubang with clear orders from his commander, Major Yoshimi Taniguchi. Hold out. Never surrender. And never take your own life. American and Filipino forces landed on the island in February 1945, and Onoda took to the hills with three other soldiers.

[measured] Leaflets fell from the sky saying the war was over. Onoda decided they were enemy tricks. Over the years, newspapers and letters from his own family were left for him. He studied them, and decided they were clever fakes. One of his men walked out and surrendered in 1950. The other two were killed in clashes, in 1954 and in 1972. Onoda was left alone.

[steady] It was not a harmless war. For nearly three decades Onoda and his men lived by raiding farms, burning crops and fighting police, and the people of Lubang paid for it. They were blamed for the deaths of around thirty islanders. Then, in February 1974, a young Japanese traveller called Norio Suzuki went to Lubang on his own to find him, and did. Onoda still would not give up. He said he would only lay down his arms if his commanding officer ordered it.

## close

[thoughtful] So Japan found Major Taniguchi, who was by then a bookseller, and flew him to the island. On the 9th of March 1974 he read Onoda his orders to stop. Onoda surrendered his sword to the President of the Philippines, who pardoned him.

[steady] Onoda went home to a Japan he did not recognise, wrote a memoir called No Surrender, and later moved to Brazil to raise cattle. He died in 2014, at the age of ninety-one. On Lubang, the families of the people killed during those years remembered the jungle war very differently.
