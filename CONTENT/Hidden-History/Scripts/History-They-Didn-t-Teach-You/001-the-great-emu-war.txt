## hook

[warmly] In 1932, the Australian army went to war against emus, and lost. This is the story of the Great Emu War, in the wheat country of Western Australia.

It began with soldiers. After the First World War, Australia gave thousands of former soldiers plots of land out in the west, to grow wheat. It was hard, dry country, and wheat prices had fallen.

## story

[measured] Then, in 1932, about twenty thousand emus arrived. An emu is a huge flightless bird, nearly as tall as a person, and it can run at about thirty miles an hour. After breeding, they headed west to the farms, because the farmers had cleared the land and given them water. They ate the wheat and pushed through the fences, which let the rabbits in.

[steady] A group of the former soldiers went to the Minister of Defence, Sir George Pearce. They knew what machine guns could do. They asked for some. He agreed, partly because it would be good target practice, and good publicity. In November 1932, a Major Meredith arrived in the district of Campion with two soldiers, two Lewis machine guns and ten thousand rounds of ammunition. A newsreel cameraman came along too.

[warmly] It went badly at once. The emus did not behave like an enemy. At the first attempt, they split into small groups and ran in every direction, out of range. Two days later a thousand birds came towards a dam, and the gun jammed after killing a dozen. Meredith mounted a gun on a truck, but the truck could not keep up, and the gunner could not hold his aim over the bumps. After six days, they had used about twenty-five hundred rounds, and killed only a few hundred birds at best. The Australian press had a wonderful time. Meredith told reporters, in words the papers printed, that with the bullet-carrying capacity of these birds, a military division could face any army in the world.

## close

[thoughtful] The army pulled out, went back at the farmers' request, and pulled out again. By December, Meredith claimed about a thousand birds. In the end it was the bounty system that worked: farmers were paid for each emu they killed, and tens of thousands were handed in over the years. Today the fences do the job. The emu, meanwhile, is on Australia's coat of arms.
