## hook

[measured] On the 12th of September 1897, twenty-one soldiers in a small stone signal post on a mountain ridge refused to surrender to an army of thousands, and fought until the last man.

[steady] This is the story of Saragarhi, on the North-West Frontier of British India, in what is now Pakistan, and of the 36th Sikhs, a regiment of Sikh soldiers from the Punjab serving in the British Indian Army.

## story

[steady] Saragarhi sat on the Samana Ridge, between two bigger forts, Fort Lockhart and Fort Gulistan. Its job was to pass messages between them by heliograph, a mirror that flashes sunlight in code. In the summer of 1897 the Pashtun tribes of the region, the Orakzai and the Afridi, rose against the British, who were pushing roads and forts into their hills. They had their own reasons to fight: it was their land.

[measured] Early that morning, a huge force of tribesmen, the British later estimated about ten thousand, surrounded the post. Inside were twenty-one men, led by a sergeant, Havildar Ishar Singh. Their signaller, Gurmukh Singh, began flashing messages to Fort Lockhart, where the commanding officer could see the attack through his glass, but could not get through to help. The men were offered terms to surrender, according to the regiment's account. They refused.

[steady] They held for most of the day. They beat off two attacks. The tribesmen set fire to the scrub, and under the smoke broke through part of the wall. The soldiers fell back into the inner rooms and kept fighting. Gurmukh Singh kept signalling, reporting each stage of the fight, until at last, the British record says, he asked permission to put down his heliograph and take up his rifle. It was the last message Fort Lockhart received.

## close

[thoughtful] All twenty-one men were killed. The British estimated the tribesmen had lost around a hundred and eighty to two hundred. Two days later, a relief force retook the ruins of the post. Every one of the twenty-one was given the Indian Order of Merit, the highest award for bravery an Indian soldier could then receive.

[steady] The British built memorial gurdwaras, Sikh temples, in Amritsar and in Ferozepur, in their honour. The regiment that came after them, the 4th Battalion of the Sikh Regiment of the Indian Army, still marks the 12th of September every year as Saragarhi Day.
