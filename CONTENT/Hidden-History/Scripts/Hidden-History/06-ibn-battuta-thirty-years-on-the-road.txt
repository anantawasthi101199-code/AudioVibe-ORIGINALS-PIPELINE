## opening

[warmly] In June 1325, a twenty-one-year-old law student rode out of the city of Tangier on his own to make a pilgrimage to Mecca, and nearly thirty years passed before he finally came home to stay.

[measured] His name was Ibn Battuta. He came from a family of Muslim judges in Morocco, at the top corner of Africa, in the 1300s. By the end, he had crossed the Middle East, sailed down the East African coast, served as a judge in India, been shipwrecked, been robbed, married many times, and reached China. Modern estimates put his route at about seventy thousand miles, more than any traveller we know of before the age of steam.

[thoughtful] We know all this from one book, called the Rihla, which simply means The Journey. It is one of the greatest travel books ever written, and it is also, in places, a bit of a puzzle. So let's go back to where it began.

## story

[warmly] Tangier sits where the Mediterranean meets the Atlantic. Ibn Battuta was born there in February 1304. Every Muslim who is able is expected to make the pilgrimage to Mecca, the holy city in Arabia, once in a lifetime. It was about three thousand miles away. He left, he wrote, alone, with neither a companion nor a caravan, and his parents still alive. He admits he wept. Along the North African coast he caught a fever so bad he tied himself to his saddle so that he would not fall. Before he reached Egypt, he had married for the first time.

[steady] In the spring of 1326 he reached Egypt, and Cairo, then the largest city in the Muslim world. Here is the first thing to understand about his journey. The Islamic world in the 1300s was like one vast club. It stretched from Spain to Indonesia, and it ran on Arabic, Islamic law and a respect for scholars. A trained young judge could walk into a mosque a thousand miles from home, and find a meal, a bed and perhaps a job. A wealthy man might give him a horse. That was his passport and his bank.

[measured] He reached Mecca in the autumn of 1326, and that was the end of the plan. By his own account, he had found out what he wanted to do with his life, which was to keep going. He later claimed a rule for himself: never to travel the same road twice, if he could help it. He crossed Iraq and Persia. He sailed down the east coast of Africa, in about 1331, as far as Kilwa, a stone city on the coast of what is now Tanzania, and said it was among the most beautiful towns he had seen. He went up through Turkey and north to the steppes of Russia, to the court of a Mongol ruler, and then on to Constantinople.

[steady] In 1333 he reached the River Indus and entered India. Now a major figure arrives: Muhammad bin Tughluq, the Sultan of Delhi, ruler of most of India. He was a brilliant, learned man who liked foreign scholars, and he paid them lavishly. He gave Ibn Battuta the post of judge of Delhi, on a huge salary. He also paid off his debts, which had run to about fifty-five thousand silver dinars. Ibn Battuta's description of the sultan is one of the most remarkable in the book. He says this man loved two things above all: giving gifts and shedding blood.

[thoughtful] For about eight years he lived inside that danger. The sultan once ordered the people of Delhi to move to a new capital some seven hundred miles south, and it went very badly. He executed people on the slightest suspicion. Ibn Battuta visited a holy man whom the sultan then had killed, and he found himself under guard, fasting and reciting prayers, sure that he would be next. He survived it, resigned his post, and tried to become a hermit. Then, in 1341, the sultan called him back, and gave him a new job: lead an embassy to the emperor of China.

[steady] It was the worst moment of his life. The party set out with gifts, slaves and soldiers. At Calicut, on the south-west coast of India, a storm wrecked the great ships in the harbour while he stood watching from the shore. The gifts were gone, and many of the people were dead. He did not dare go back to Delhi. For some time he drifted down the coast, and then he took a boat to the Maldives, the coral islands in the Indian Ocean, where he was once again made a judge. He tried to make the islanders follow strict law, and married four wives from leading families. He left within about a year and a half.

[measured] He went on to Sri Lanka and climbed Adam's Peak, the holy mountain. He crossed the Bay of Bengal, passed through Sumatra, and in about 1345 he landed at the Chinese port of Quanzhou, then one of the greatest harbours in the world. He admired the silk and the porcelain and the safety of the roads, and he says he found Muslim merchants in every town.

[thoughtful] Here we should stop for a moment. His account of China is thin, and the journey north to Beijing is especially difficult to fit to the dates and the places. Ross Dunn, his modern biographer, thinks he likely saw southern China and doubts the rest. Parts of the book are also copied from an earlier traveller. So some of the journey is firm, and some of it is an old man's memory.

[steady] He turned for home. On the way he heard about the Black Death, the plague crossing the Middle East in 1348. He saw it in Damascus, where he says more than two thousand people died in a day. In November 1349 he reached Fez, in Morocco, and learned that his mother had died of it some months before. His father had died fifteen years earlier. He had been gone for twenty-four years.

## legacy

[warmly] He could not stay still even then. He crossed to Granada in Spain, and in 1352 he went south across the Sahara to the Empire of Mali, a journey of months by camel, and came back in 1354. The Sultan of Morocco, Abu Inan, then ordered him to dictate it all to a young scholar, Ibn Juzayy, who shaped it into a book. It took about two years.

[thoughtful] After that he served as a judge in a town in Morocco, and he died in about 1368 or 1369. For centuries the Rihla was barely known outside the Muslim world. European scholars discovered it in the 1800s, and today it is one of our best windows on that whole connected world, from Mali to Quanzhou.

[warmly] Tangier still has a tomb that people say is his, though no one is certain of that. It is a small, white room in the old town, a few streets from where a young man once rode out alone, and wept, and expected to be gone for a little over a year.
