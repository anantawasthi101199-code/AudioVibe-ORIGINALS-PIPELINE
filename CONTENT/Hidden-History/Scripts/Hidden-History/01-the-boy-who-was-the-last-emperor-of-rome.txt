## opening

[warmly] In the late summer of 476, the last Roman emperor of the West was a teenage boy, and the man who removed him thought him so harmless that he gave him a pension and sent him off to live by the sea.

[measured] That is how the Western Roman Empire ended. It had ruled most of Europe for five hundred years. It finished in Ravenna, a marshy town in northern Italy, with no last battle for the city and no great fire. A general simply took the throne away from a boy, and then told the world that nobody needed to sit on it again.

[thoughtful] This is the story of that boy, Romulus Augustulus, and of Odoacer, the soldier who ended his reign. It is also the story of how the most powerful state in the Western world shrank to that one quiet afternoon, and why so many people alive at the time carried on as if nothing had happened.

[warmly] So let's go back to where it began.

## story

[measured] First, the shape of the thing. By the year 400, the Roman world had been split in two. The eastern half, ruled from Constantinople, in what is now Istanbul, was rich and secure. The western half, ruled from Italy, had the longer frontiers and the smaller purse. Its emperors paid for an army out of taxes, and when the taxes failed, the army failed too. It was like a household where one failing job leaves the mortgage unpaid, and then the car goes, and then the job.

[steady] And the pressure on the frontiers kept growing. Germanic peoples from beyond the rivers Rhine and Danube, many of them fleeing the Huns from the steppes, crossed into Roman land. In 410 the Goths, under their king Alaric, sacked Rome itself. In 439 another people, the Vandals, took Carthage in North Africa, and that was the true disaster, because North Africa was the West's richest source of grain and tax. In 455 the Vandals sailed across and sacked Rome as well.

[thoughtful] By then, the western government had a strange problem. It could no longer afford Roman soldiers, so it hired barbarian ones, whole bands of them, under their own commanders. Those commanders soon became the real power. An emperor could be made or broken by the general who paid his troops. Between 455 and 476, the West had nine emperors in twenty-one years, and most of them ended badly.

[steady] Now, a who's who for the last act. Orestes was a Roman officer who had once served as secretary to Attila the Hun. Julius Nepos was the emperor in Italy in 475, and he had been sent by the eastern court. Odoacer was a soldier in the army's barbarian ranks, from somewhere along the Danube, the son of a man who may have served Attila too. And Romulus was the son of Orestes, a boy of perhaps fourteen or fifteen.

[measured] In August 475, Orestes, now commander of the army, marched on Ravenna. Nepos took a ship and fled across the Adriatic Sea to Dalmatia, on the coast of what is now Croatia. On the 31st of October, Orestes put the purple on his own son. It was a clever move. Orestes had the power and kept it, while a child wore the crown. The boy was named after two men: Romulus, the founder of Rome, and Augustus, its first emperor. People soon gave him a mocking little ending to the second one: Augustulus, the little Augustus.

[steady] The boy reigned for about ten months, and we have no record of a single thing he decided. What undid his father was money and land. The barbarian soldiers in Italy asked for what Roman law had given soldiers in other provinces: a share of the land to settle on. According to the historian Procopius, writing in the following century, they demanded a third of Italy. Orestes said no.

[thoughtful] Odoacer told the men he would get it for them. On the 23rd of August 476, they raised him up as their king. They caught Orestes at Placentia, today's Piacenza, and executed him there on the 28th. His brother Paulus was killed at Ravenna a few days later.

[pause] Then Odoacer reached the palace, and found the emperor. By custom, a deposed emperor should have died. A surviving chronicle, known as the Anonymus Valesianus, records what Odoacer did instead. He looked at the boy, took pity on him because of his youth and his good looks, and spared him. He gave him six thousand gold coins a year, and sent him south to Campania, to a grand old villa on the Bay of Naples, to live with his relatives.

[measured] The clever part came next. Odoacer did not take the title of emperor. He had the Roman Senate send the imperial robes and the crown to Constantinople, to the eastern emperor Zeno, with a message. The West, it said, no longer needed an emperor of its own. One was enough for both. Odoacer asked only to govern Italy on Zeno's behalf. Zeno was unhappy, and pointed out that Nepos was still alive in Dalmatia. But he had his own troubles, and he let it stand.

[steady] Inside Italy, life looked much the same on the following morning. The Senate still met in Rome. Consuls were still named. Roman law still ran, and Roman officials still collected tax. A farmer outside Naples that autumn would have paid the same collector. The writers of the time did not rush to record a catastrophe. The first man we know of to name 476 as the end of the Western Empire was a chronicler in Constantinople, Marcellinus, writing fifty years later.

## legacy

[thoughtful] Odoacer ruled Italy for seventeen years, and ruled it well enough. In 493 he was killed at a banquet by Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king, said to be by the king's own hand, after a peace treaty. Julius Nepos was murdered in Dalmatia in 480.

[warmly] And Romulus simply vanishes from the record. There is a letter from the early 500s, written by a Roman minister named Cassiodorus, confirming a grant to a man named Romulus and his mother. Some historians think that was him, a man in his forties, still by the bay. No one can be sure.

[measured] Historians still argue about what ended here. Some, such as Bryan Ward-Perkins and Peter Heather, say it was a real collapse: within a century or two, the pottery, the coins, the roofs and the great cities had all fallen away. Others say it was a slow change of management, with Roman ways lasting under new kings. Either way, the Latin of that lost empire became French, Italian and Spanish, and its eastern half lasted nearly another thousand years.

[warmly] The purple robes went off to Constantinople, and the last emperor of Rome went to the seaside, with a pension.
