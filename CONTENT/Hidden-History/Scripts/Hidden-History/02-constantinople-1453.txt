## opening

[measured] On the 29th of May 1453, before dawn, the last Roman emperor went to the broken walls of his city to fight as an ordinary soldier, and by the end of that morning the Roman Empire, more than fourteen hundred years after it began, was gone.

[steady] The city was Constantinople, the great capital on the water between Europe and Asia, which today is Istanbul, in Turkey. Its people called themselves Romans. We call their state the Byzantine Empire, but to them it was simply the Roman Empire, still going, a thousand years after Rome itself had fallen in the west. By 1453 that empire had shrunk to little more than one city and a few scraps of land.

[thoughtful] Outside the walls was the army of the Ottoman Turks, the rising power that already ruled most of the land around it, led by a sultan who was twenty-one years old. There are three people to know. The young sultan, Mehmed the Second. The emperor, Constantine the Eleventh. And Giovanni Giustiniani, a soldier from Genoa, in Italy, who came to help. So let's go back to where it began.

## story

[steady] Constantinople had been founded as the Roman capital by the emperor Constantine the Great in the year 330. For more than a thousand years its land walls, called the Theodosian Walls, had never been broken by an enemy army. They ran for about four miles across the neck of land on the city's west side: a moat, then a lower wall, then a great inner wall forty feet high with nearly a hundred towers. On the other sides, the city was protected by the sea, and by the Golden Horn, a long, narrow harbour on its north side.

[measured] Mehmed became sultan in 1451, at nineteen. His court had doubts about him, and he wanted the city more than anything. In 1452 he built a fortress on the Bosphorus, the narrow strait north of the city, to cut it off from the Black Sea. The emperor Constantine, who was forty-eight, sent ambassadors to ask for peace. Mehmed sent them back. Constantine begged the Catholic west for help, and the help that came was small: some Venetian ships, and Giustiniani with about seven hundred soldiers he had paid for himself.

[steady] Then there was the cannon. A Hungarian engineer called Urban had first offered his services to Constantine, who could not afford him. So Urban went to Mehmed, who could. He cast a bronze cannon so large that, according to the accounts, it took sixty oxen to pull it. It fired stone balls weighing more than half a ton. It could only fire a few times a day, because it took hours to cool and reload, but every shot smashed into walls that had been built against arrows and ladders.

[measured] The siege began on the 6th of April 1453. The numbers were very unequal. The emperor's secretary and friend, George Sphrantzes, wrote that Constantine asked him to secretly count every man able to fight. He came to about four thousand nine hundred Greeks, and perhaps two thousand foreigners. The Ottoman army is usually estimated at somewhere between fifty and eighty thousand, though the accounts vary widely. Across the mouth of the Golden Horn, the defenders had stretched a great iron chain on wooden floats, so that no enemy ship could get in.

[steady] Through April the cannons pounded the land walls, and every night the defenders, men, women and children, filled the gaps with earth, timber and barrels. Then, on the 22nd of April, the people of the city woke up to a sight they did not believe. Mehmed could not break the chain, so he went round it. His men had laid a road of greased logs over the hill behind Galata, the walled town of Genoese traders across the harbour, and dragged around seventy ships overland, on wheeled cradles, with their sails up, the crews rowing in the air, and lowered them into the Golden Horn. The Venetian ship's doctor Nicolo Barbaro, who was inside the city and kept a diary, recorded the shock of it. Now the city had to guard the harbour wall too, with men it did not have.

[thoughtful] May brought hunger, and omens. On the 22nd of May there was an eclipse of the moon, and the people of the city remembered an old prophecy that it would fall while the moon was waning. During a procession, the Greek writer Kritoboulos says, the holiest icon of the city, a painting of the Virgin Mary, slipped from its frame and fell. Then thick fog covered the city. On the Ottoman side there were doubts too. Some of Mehmed's advisers urged him to give up. The young sultan held a council and decided on one great assault.

[steady] On the evening of the 28th of May, the Christians of the city, Greek Orthodox and Italian Catholic, who had argued bitterly for years about their faith, prayed together in the great church of Hagia Sophia, the Holy Wisdom, for the last time. Constantine spoke to his commanders, and asked forgiveness of anyone he had wronged. Outside, the Ottoman camp was silent, fasting and preparing.

[measured] The attack came at about half past one in the morning. Mehmed sent his men in waves. First the irregular troops, then the regular soldiers from Anatolia, then, as the defenders tired, his finest troops, the Janissaries, the sultan's own professional infantry. Around dawn, Giustiniani was badly wounded, by a shot through his armour, and was carried from the wall to a ship. His men saw him go, and lost heart. Then, according to the Greek historian Doukas, a small gate called the Kerkoporta had been left open, and Ottoman soldiers got onto the walls through it and raised their flag. The accounts disagree on how much that gate mattered. But once the defence broke at one point, it broke everywhere.

[pause] And the emperor? The truth is, nobody who wrote it down saw him die. The Greek accounts say he took off his imperial regalia and threw himself into the fighting. His body was never certainly identified.

## legacy

[steady] Mehmed rode into the city that day. Tursun Beg, an Ottoman official who was there, wrote that the sultan was moved by the ruins of the great city. He went to Hagia Sophia and had it turned into a mosque. The soldiers were allowed to plunder, as was the custom, and thousands of the city's people were taken as slaves. Giustiniani died of his wound a few days later, on the island of Chios. Sphrantzes was captured, ransomed, and lived to write his chronicle.

[measured] Mehmed, now called the Conqueror, made the city his capital and set out to fill it again. He brought in Muslims, Christians and Jews from across his lands, and in 1454 appointed a new Greek Orthodox patriarch. He took for himself the title Caesar of Rome. The Ottoman Empire would rule from that city for almost five hundred years.

[thoughtful] The great land walls are still there, cracked and patched, running for miles through modern Istanbul. And the place where the chain once stretched across the Golden Horn is crossed today by bridges, full of people going to work.
