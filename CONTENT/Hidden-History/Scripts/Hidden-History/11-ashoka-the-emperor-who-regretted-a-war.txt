## opening

[measured] Around 261 BCE, an Indian emperor won the biggest war of his life, and then ordered his own words of regret carved into rock, in letters people can still read today.

[steady] His name was Ashoka. He ruled the Mauryan Empire, the first empire to cover most of the Indian subcontinent, from his capital at Pataliputra, near the modern city of Patna, on the river Ganges. The war was against Kalinga, a rich kingdom on the east coast, roughly where the Indian state of Odisha is now. By his own count, it killed a hundred thousand people.

[thoughtful] What makes Ashoka unusual is what came after. Conquerors usually carve their victories. Ashoka carved his shame, and then tried to rule an empire in a new way. This is the story of how much of that we can actually prove, and how much is legend. So let's go back to where it began.

## story

[steady] First, the family, because three generations matter here. Ashoka's grandfather was Chandragupta Maurya, who founded the empire around 321 BCE, not long after Alexander the Great had turned back from India. Chandragupta's son, Bindusara, pushed the empire south. Ashoka was one of Bindusara's sons, and not the eldest.

[measured] How he came to the throne is the first place the evidence gets thin. Later Buddhist texts, written centuries afterwards, say Bindusara died around 273 BCE and that Ashoka fought his brothers for four years. One story has him killing ninety-nine of them. The same texts call him Chandashoka, Ashoka the fierce, before he changed. Historians like Romila Thapar treat these stories with real caution. The Buddhist writers had a good reason to make the early Ashoka as wicked as possible: the darker the sinner, the brighter the conversion. What the record supports is a disputed succession, and a coronation around 269 BCE.

[steady] For eight years he ruled the way his father had. Then, around 261 BCE, he turned on Kalinga. It was the one large kingdom left independent on the empire's flank, and it controlled the trade routes along the eastern coast and across the sea. We have no account from the people of Kalinga. We do not know the name of their king, or of any of their soldiers. What we know comes from the winner.

[measured] And the winner tells it like this. In the inscription historians call Rock Edict Thirteen, Ashoka, who calls himself Devanampiya Piyadasi, Beloved of the Gods, Gracious of Look, says that a hundred and fifty thousand people were carried off from Kalinga, a hundred thousand were killed, and many times that number died afterwards. Then, in a voice no other king of the ancient world used, he says the conquest filled him with remorse. He writes that the killing, the deaths and the carrying away of people were deeply painful to him and weighed heavily on his mind. He adds that even the friends and families of those who survived suffered, and that this too was grief to him.

[thoughtful] Here is a detail that keeps historians awake. That edict was carved at many places across the empire. But at the two sites in Kalinga itself, at Dhauli and Jaugada, the words of regret are left out. In their place are two special edicts addressed to the officials governing the conquered land, telling them to be fair, to stop wrongful imprisonment and torture, and to treat the people as his children. One reading is that he was sincere, and simply did not want to rub salt into Kalinga's wounds. Another, which Thapar raises, is that remorse was a message for the rest of the empire, while the conquered were given rules. Both may be true.

[steady] After Kalinga, Ashoka says he turned to Dhamma, a word from his own language that means something like the right way to live. It overlapped with Buddhism, which he followed as a lay believer, but his edicts mostly preach things that any religion of the time could agree with. Obey your parents. Be generous to priests and monks of every faith. Do not kill animals needlessly. Speak well of other people's religions, because, he says, whoever praises his own faith and condemns another's harms his own. He sent officials called Dhamma ministers around the empire to spread it.

[measured] He also built things you could touch. The edicts mention wells dug every few miles along the roads, rest-houses for travellers, banyan trees planted for shade and mango groves for fruit, and medical care for people and for animals. The royal kitchen, he admits in one edict, used to kill hundreds of thousands of animals for curry, and now only three a day, two peacocks and a deer, and soon none at all. It is a very honest kind of reform.

[steady] He sent envoys west, to the Greek kings who had inherited Alexander's world, and names five of them in the edicts, including the rulers of Syria and Egypt. Buddhist tradition says his son Mahinda carried the faith to Sri Lanka. At Kandahar, in modern Afghanistan, one of his inscriptions was carved in Greek and in Aramaic, so that the people there could read it.

[thoughtful] It was not a soft empire, though, and Ashoka did not pretend it was. In the same Rock Edict Thirteen, he warns the forest peoples on his borders that the Beloved of the Gods, for all his remorse, still has power, and that they should mend their ways if they do not wish to be killed. He kept the death penalty, though he gave the condemned three days to prepare. Regret, for Ashoka, was real, and it was also a way of running an empire.

## legacy

[steady] Ashoka died around 232 BCE. Within about fifty years of his death the Mauryan Empire had broken apart, and the last Mauryan king was killed by his own general in about 185 BCE. Of the people of Kalinga, we still know no names at all.

[measured] For almost two thousand years even Ashoka's name faded in India, kept alive mostly in Buddhist stories. His edicts were written in Brahmi, a script nobody could read, until a British scholar, James Prinsep, deciphered it in 1837. Even then the author was only called Piyadasi, and it was 1915 before an inscription was found that named him outright as Ashoka.

[thoughtful] Today the four lions from the top of one of his pillars, found at Sarnath, are the national emblem of India, and the wheel beneath them sits at the centre of the Indian flag. And at Dhauli, on a low hill above the river Daya, where the battle may have been fought, his words are still cut into the rock, telling the officials of a conquered land to be fair.
