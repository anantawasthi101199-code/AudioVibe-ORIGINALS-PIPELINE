## hook

[low, steady] It's the evening of the eighteenth of December, 1914, in a rented house in Highgate, north London. Downstairs, the landlady hears the sounds of her new lodger, a young bride, having a bath upstairs. [softly] There's splashing. Then what sounds like wet hands sliding down the side of the bath. Then a sigh.

[thoughtful] And then, from the sitting room, she hears the harmonium, a small organ, being played. The lodger's husband is playing a hymn.

[serious] Not long afterwards, he comes downstairs and goes out, saying he's off to buy tomatoes for his wife's supper. When he returns, he raises the alarm. His bride, Margaret Lofty, is dead in the bath. [measured] They had been married the day before.

[thoughtful] What nobody in that house knew was that it was the third time this man's new wife had died in her bath.

## context

[serious] The man was George Joseph Smith. He was born in east London in 1872, and spent much of his youth in reformatories and prisons for theft. As an adult, he had found a different way to make money. He married women, under false names, and took what they had.

[softly] Bessie Mundy was the first of the three who died. She was the daughter of a bank manager, and when he died, he left her an inheritance of about two and a half thousand pounds, a large sum, held in a trust that paid her an income. In 1910, in Weymouth, she married a man calling himself Henry Williams. It was Smith. [grave] Within weeks he had taken what money he could, and vanished.

[thoughtful] Two years later, in 1912, Bessie happened to run into him again, in Weston-super-Mare. He told her a story, and she forgave him. They moved to Herne Bay, in Kent. [measured] There, Smith did three things. He made sure each of them wrote a will leaving everything to the other. He bought a bath, haggling the price down, for a house that didn't have one. And he took Bessie to a local doctor, saying she'd been having fits, though she didn't remember any.

[grave] On the thirteenth of July, 1912, the doctor was called to the house. Bessie was dead in the bath. An inquest found that she'd had a fit and drowned, a tragic accident. [darkly] Smith inherited her money. And he returned the bath to the shop, without paying for it.

[softly] Alice Burnham was a young nurse. She married Smith in Portsmouth in November 1913, and he took out insurance on her life. In December, on holiday in Blackpool, they took rooms in a lodging house. [grave] Alice was found dead in the bath. Again, the verdict was accidental drowning.

[thoughtful] And then, a year later, Margaret Lofty, a clergyman's daughter, married a man called John Lloyd, in Bath. Also Smith. He had insured her life too. And the next evening came that bath in Highgate.

## investigation

[curious] A national newspaper, the News of the World, ran a short report about Margaret Lofty's death: a bride found dead in her bath the day after her wedding.

[thoughtful] Two people, in two different towns, read it, and it made them go cold. [serious] One was Charles Burnham, Alice's father, who had never trusted his daughter's husband. The other was Joseph Crossley, who ran the Blackpool lodging house where Alice had died, and who remembered the husband very well. Both noticed how alike the stories were. Both wrote to the police.

[determined] The letters reached Detective Inspector Arthur Neil, in north London. He started to dig. He found that John Lloyd of Highgate and George Smith of Blackpool matched descriptions of the same man. In February 1915, Smith was arrested, at first on a smaller charge, of giving false details when he married.

[thoughtful] Neil traced him back, name by name, marriage by marriage, and found Bessie Mundy in Herne Bay too. The three women's bodies were exhumed, and examined by the most famous pathologist in Britain, a doctor called Bernard Spilsbury.

[serious] But there was a problem. Spilsbury could find almost no sign of a struggle on any of them. No bruises worth mentioning. No sign of a fight. [measured] If Smith had held them under the water, surely they would have fought back, and splashed, and marked him and the room.

## turning_point

[curious] So Spilsbury and Neil tried an experiment. They borrowed a full-size bath, filled it with water, and asked a young woman who was an experienced swimmer and diver to help, wearing a bathing costume.

[building] First, Neil tried pushing her head under. She struggled hard, as anyone would. [intense] Then, without warning, he grabbed her ankles and pulled her legs up sharply, so that her head slid down under the water. [grave] She went limp at once. Water rushed into her nose and mouth, and she lost consciousness so suddenly that it took them several minutes to revive her.

[thoughtful] That was it. A sudden pull of the legs, from the end of the bath, could make someone black out in seconds, without a struggle and without marks.

[measured] There was one more turning point, in court. Smith was put on trial only for the murder of Bessie Mundy. But the judge allowed the jury to hear about all three deaths. Any single death might look like a tragic accident. Three, in the same way, to the same husband, could not.

## aftermath

[serious] Smith's trial opened at the Old Bailey, London's central criminal court, in June 1915. He was defended by one of the most famous barristers of the age. He shouted at witnesses from the dock. [grave] The jury took about twenty minutes to find him guilty. He was hanged at Maidstone Prison on the thirteenth of August, 1915.

[thoughtful] The case made Bernard Spilsbury a national figure, and his methods became part of how British courts used medical evidence. And the judge's decision to let the jury hear about all three deaths became an important example in English law of how a pattern of similar acts can be used as evidence.

[warmly] But the people at the centre of the story were the three women. [softly] Bessie Mundy, who forgave him and believed his story. Alice Burnham, the young nurse whose father never trusted him. Margaret Lofty, the clergyman's daughter, married for just one day. [tender] It was a father who wouldn't let his daughter's death go, and a landlord who remembered a face, who made sure that what happened to them was finally understood.
