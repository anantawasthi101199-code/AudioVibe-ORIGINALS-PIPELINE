## hook

[low, steady] It's twenty to four in the morning, on the last day of August, 1888. A cart driver called Charles Cross is walking to work through Whitechapel, in the East End of London. The street is called Buck's Row. It's narrow and dark, lined with warehouses and small houses, and there's hardly a gas lamp working.

[serious] By a gateway, on the pavement, he sees a shape. At first he thinks it's a tarpaulin, dropped from a cart. Then he sees it's a woman, lying on her back. [softly] Her name was Mary Ann Nichols. Her friends called her Polly. She was forty-three. And she was dead.

[thoughtful] She was the first of five women whose murders that autumn would be linked together, and blamed on one unknown man the newspapers would soon call Jack the Ripper. [measured] This is the story of those five women, of the police who tried to find their killer, and of why, after more than a century, nobody knows who he was.

## context

[serious] Whitechapel in 1888 was one of the poorest places in Britain. Tens of thousands of people lived crammed into old houses and back alleys. [thoughtful] Many had nowhere to live at all. They slept in common lodging houses, paying a few pennies a night for a bed in a crowded room, and if they couldn't find the money by evening, they walked the streets all night.

[warmly] The five women whose deaths the police linked were not simply victims. They had whole lives behind them.

[softly] Polly Nichols had been married for years, and had five children. When her marriage broke down, she had worked as a servant, and drifted from workhouse to lodging house. On the night she died, she'd been turned away from a lodging house because she didn't have fourpence for a bed. She told the deputy she'd soon have it, and that she had a jolly new bonnet.

[thoughtful] Annie Chapman was forty-seven. She had been married to a coachman, and lived for a time in Windsor. She lost children to illness, and her life fell apart into poverty and drink. In the East End, she made crochet work and sold flowers when she could.

[softly] Elizabeth Stride was forty-four, born in Sweden as Elisabeth Gustafsdotter, and had come to London as a young woman. Catherine Eddowes, forty-six, from Wolverhampton, had just come back from weeks of picking hops in the Kent countryside with her partner. Mary Jane Kelly was the youngest, about twenty-five, and the least is known about her.

[measured] For a long time, these women were described only as prostitutes. In recent years, the historian Hallie Rubenhold went back through the records and argued that for several of them, there's little evidence of that at all, and that what they had in common was poverty and homelessness. Not every historian agrees with every part of her account. But her work put the women's own lives back at the centre of the story.

## investigation

[serious] After Polly Nichols's death, the local police, the Metropolitan Police's H Division, began their enquiries. Scotland Yard sent an experienced detective who knew Whitechapel well, Inspector Frederick Abberline.

[grave] Eight days later, early on the eighth of September, Annie Chapman was found in a back yard behind a house on Hanbury Street. The killer had worked fast, in the half-light, with people asleep only a few feet away.

[thoughtful] The East End was gripped by fear. Rumours spread about a man nicknamed Leather Apron, and an innocent local man was arrested and then cleared. Local businessmen formed a vigilance committee, led by a builder called George Lusk, to patrol the streets and push the police for answers. [measured] The police knocked on doors, took statements, and questioned dozens of men. Forensic science barely existed. There were no fingerprints on record, no blood tests that could identify a person, and no photographs of suspects to show witnesses.

[curious] Then, at the end of September, a letter arrived at a news agency in London. It was written in red ink, it boasted about the murders, and it was signed with a name nobody had heard before: Jack the Ripper. [knowingly] It was printed in the newspapers, and the name stuck. Many police officers later believed it had been written by a journalist, to sell more papers.

[grave] In the early hours of the thirtieth of September, two women were killed within an hour of each other. Elizabeth Stride was found in a yard off Berner Street, by a man arriving home with his pony and cart, who may have disturbed the killer. Less than an hour later, Catherine Eddowes was found in Mitre Square, inside the boundary of the City of London. [thoughtful] That meant a second, separate police force was now involved, the City of London Police, and the two forces did not always work smoothly together.

[serious] That night, a policeman found a torn piece of Catherine Eddowes's apron in a doorway in Goulston Street. Above it, on the wall, someone had chalked a line of writing that mentioned Jews. [grave] The head of the Metropolitan Police, Sir Charles Warren, came to see it, and ordered it wiped off before it could be photographed, fearing it would spark attacks on the area's Jewish community. It's still argued whether the killer wrote it at all.

[darkly] In October, George Lusk of the vigilance committee received a small parcel. Inside was a letter, headed From Hell, and part of a human kidney.

## turning_point

[softly] Then, on the ninth of November, Mary Jane Kelly was killed. Unlike the others, she died indoors, in the small rented room where she lived, in a court off Dorset Street. She was found that morning by a man sent to collect her unpaid rent.

[thoughtful] And then, as far as anyone knows, it stopped. There were other violent deaths in Whitechapel in the years that followed, and some were linked to the same killer at the time. But most historians and police officers came to see these five as the core of the case.

[measured] Senior officers later wrote down the names of men they suspected. But nobody was ever charged. There was no evidence that would have stood up in court then, and almost none that survives now.

## aftermath

[serious] The murders were never solved. In the years since, hundreds of books have named hundreds of suspects, from local men to doctors to members of the royal family. Some people have claimed DNA evidence, from a shawl said to have belonged to Catherine Eddowes. Scientists have questioned those claims, and the shawl's history is uncertain.

[thoughtful] The case changed how people saw the East End. Newspapers across Britain and beyond reported on the poverty of Whitechapel's streets, and the pressure led to better lighting and, slowly, to clearing some of the worst housing.

[warmly] But it's worth ending where the story really belongs. Not with the man nobody ever found. With the women he killed.

[softly] Mary Ann Nichols, who wanted fourpence for a bed and had a new bonnet. Annie Chapman, who once lived in Windsor and lost her children. Elizabeth Stride, who came from Sweden. Catherine Eddowes, just back from the hop fields. Mary Jane Kelly, the youngest, who died in her own room. [tender] Five women of Whitechapel, with names, histories and families, who deserved to be remembered for who they were.
