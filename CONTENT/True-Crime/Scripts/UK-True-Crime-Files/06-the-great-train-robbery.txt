## hook

[low, steady] It's just after three o'clock in the morning on Thursday, the eighth of August, 1963, on a quiet stretch of railway in the Buckinghamshire countryside, about forty miles north-west of London. [measured] A long Royal Mail train is running south from Glasgow towards London through the dark. In its carriages, post office workers are sorting letters by electric light as they travel.

[thoughtful] At the front, in the cab of the diesel engine, the driver is Jack Mills, fifty-seven years old, from Crewe. Next to him is his young fireman, his assistant, David Whitby. [measured] Ahead, at a place called Sears Crossing, the signal is showing amber. Then red. Jack Mills brakes, and the train stops.

[curious] Whitby climbs down to the trackside telephone to ask the signalman what's wrong. The line is dead. Someone has cut the wires. [softly] And out of the darkness, men in masks and boiler suits are coming towards the train.

## context

[thoughtful] Jack Mills had worked on the railways for most of his life. He was a steady, experienced driver, a few years from retirement. David Whitby was twenty-six. [warmly] For both of them, this was an ordinary night shift on a run they knew well.

[thoughtful] The train was called the Travelling Post Office. Every night it carried mail between Scotland and London, and in one carriage near the front, called the High Value Packages coach, it carried registered mail. That night, after a bank holiday weekend in Scotland, that coach was crammed with used banknotes being sent back to London banks.

[measured] Britain in 1963 was a country where most people were paid in cash and paid for everything in cash, and many had no bank account. Banks moved money around the country by post, by rail, guarded by a handful of unarmed postal workers. [serious] A gang in London had found out about the money, and spent months planning how to take it.

## investigation

[measured] The gang had about fifteen members. They had fixed the signal themselves, covering the green light with a glove and wiring the red light to a battery. When the train stopped, some of them climbed into the cab. [grave] Jack Mills struggled with one of them, and was hit over the head, hard, with a heavy bar. He fell, bleeding.

[thoughtful] The gang had brought a man to drive the train, but he could not work this kind of engine. So they made Mills, injured and dazed, move the train forward himself, about half a mile, to a bridge called Bridego Bridge, where their lorry was waiting below. [measured] They uncoupled the money coach from the rest of the train, where the other post office workers had no idea what was happening.

[serious] Then they smashed their way into the coach, and formed a human chain down the embankment, passing mailbags hand to hand into the lorry. In about half an hour they loaded a hundred and twenty bags. [measured] Inside them was about two million, six hundred thousand pounds. In today's money, more than fifty million.

[thoughtful] They told the postal workers not to move for half an hour. Then they drove away, along back roads, to a farmhouse they had bought for the purpose, Leatherslade Farm, about twenty-seven miles away. [measured] The plan was to hide there for days, until the search died down.

[serious] At dawn, the police began. The robbery was enormous, and the papers were full of it within hours. [thoughtful] Officers guessed the gang must be hiding nearby, and on the radio a police officer said they were searching farms within a thirty-mile radius. The gang heard it, and panicked. Instead of staying for days, they split the money and left within about two days.

[measured] They had arranged for someone to clean the farm and burn everything they had touched. It was never done. [pause] Five days after the robbery, on the thirteenth of August, a local farm worker told police about strange goings-on at Leatherslade. Officers went in.

## turning_point

[measured] The farmhouse was a mess of empty mailbags, sleeping bags and food. The gang had spent their time waiting by eating, drinking, and playing games. [thoughtful] On a table was a Monopoly board, the game where players buy streets and pay rent in toy money. The robbers had played it with real banknotes from the train.

[serious] Fingerprint experts from Scotland Yard went over everything. The Monopoly board and its pieces. A ketchup bottle. Plates. A tin of salt. [thoughtful] Fingerprints had been used in British courts for more than fifty years by then, and Scotland Yard kept prints on file for everyone with a criminal record. Many of the gang had records.

[measured] The prints began to give names. Detective Chief Superintendent Tommy Butler of the Flying Squad, London's specialist robbery detectives, led the hunt. One by one, men were found and arrested over the following months, with prints from the farm, and sometimes cash, to link them. [thoughtful] It was the plain everyday objects that undid them. The gang had worn gloves on the railway, and taken them off at the farm, to deal cards and pass the ketchup.

## aftermath

[measured] The main trial was held in Aylesbury in 1964. Most of the gang were convicted, and the judge gave many of them sentences of thirty years, far longer than for most violent crimes at the time, to show how seriously he took it. [serious] Some escaped. Ronnie Biggs, a small-time thief who had found them their substitute driver, broke out of prison in 1965 and lived abroad, ending up in Brazil. He came back to Britain in 2001 and went back to prison. The gang's leader, Bruce Reynolds, was caught in 1968.

[thoughtful] Of the two and a half million pounds, only about four hundred thousand was ever recovered. The rest vanished, spent, hidden, or lost to the people the gang paid to help them. [measured] And the man who hit Jack Mills was never named in court, and has never been identified for certain.

[thoughtful] Over the years, books and films turned the robbers into charming rogues, and the robbery into the perfect job. [softly] The record shows something else. A gang that fled in a panic, left its fingerprints on a board game, and spent decades in prison or on the run.

[gently] And it shows Jack Mills. After the attack he suffered headaches and anxiety, and he never drove a train again. He died in 1970, seven years after that night. [softly] He was the man at the front of the train, doing his job in the dark, and he is the reason it was never just a clever heist.
