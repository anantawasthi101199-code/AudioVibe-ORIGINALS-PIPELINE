## hook

[low, steady] It's the morning of the thirty-first of July, 1910, on the St Lawrence River in Canada. A passenger ship called the Montrose, eleven days out from Antwerp, has slowed down near a place called Father Point, to take on a river pilot, the local sailor who steers big ships up the river.

[thoughtful] On deck, a small, neat man in gold-rimmed glasses is waiting to watch the pilot come aboard. He has been travelling as Mr John Robinson, with his teenage son. [measured] A little boat comes alongside. Several men in pilots' uniforms climb up the ladder.

[serious] One of them walks straight up to Mr Robinson. He takes off his cap, and, as he later told it, says: good morning, Dr Crippen. Do you know me? I am Chief Inspector Dew, of Scotland Yard.

[softly] How did a detective from London get ahead of a ship in the middle of the Atlantic? And what had happened to the woman this man left behind?

## context

[thoughtful] Mr Robinson was Hawley Harvey Crippen. He was born in Michigan, in America, in 1862, and trained in homeopathy, a kind of alternative medicine. He wasn't qualified as a doctor in Britain, but everyone called him Dr Crippen. In London he worked for companies selling patent medicines and, later, for a dental practice. [softly] People who met him found him mild, polite and quiet.

[warmly] His wife was Cora. She was born in New York, and she wanted to be a star. On stage, in the music halls, the popular variety theatres of the day, she called herself Belle Elmore. [thoughtful] She was loud, cheerful and generous, fond of furs, jewels and parties. Her singing career never really took off, but she had many friends in the music hall world, and she was treasurer of a charity called the Music Hall Ladies' Guild.

[measured] By 1910 they lived at 39 Hilldrop Crescent, a tall house in Holloway, north London. They took in lodgers to make ends meet. The marriage was unhappy. And for several years, Crippen had been seeing a young typist from his office, Ethel Le Neve.

[softly] On the evening of the thirty-first of January, 1910, the Crippens had two friends to dinner, a music hall couple, the Martinettis. They left in the early hours. [pause] After that night, none of Cora's friends ever saw her again.

## investigation

[thoughtful] Two days later, on the second of February, the Music Hall Ladies' Guild received a letter, said to be from Cora, resigning as treasurer. It said she had gone to America to look after a sick relative. [measured] It was not in her handwriting.

[serious] Her friends were puzzled. Cora would never have left without saying goodbye. Then, on the twentieth of February, Crippen went to a charity ball. With him was Ethel Le Neve, wearing a brooch that Cora's friends recognised as Cora's.

[thoughtful] In March, Crippen told friends that Cora had fallen ill in America. A few days later, he said she had died there, in California. Ethel moved into Hilldrop Crescent.

[serious] Cora's friends kept asking questions. Where in California? When was the funeral? The answers were vague. [measured] At the end of June, nearly five months after she disappeared, two of her friends, John Nash and his wife, the singer Lil Hawthorne, went to Scotland Yard.

[thoughtful] The case went to Chief Inspector Walter Dew, an experienced detective. On the eighth of July, he visited Crippen at work. And Crippen surprised him. He admitted, straight away, that his story was a lie. Cora wasn't dead, he said. She had left him for another man, an American, and gone back to America. He had made up the story of her death to avoid the embarrassment.

[measured] Dew went with Crippen to the house, and looked round, including the cellar. He found nothing. He came away fairly satisfied. [softly] But the next day, Crippen and Ethel Le Neve were gone.

[serious] When Dew came back on the eleventh of July to check one detail and found they had fled, everything changed. He had the house searched from top to bottom. Over two days, he kept going back to the coal cellar, a small dark room under the front steps, with a floor of bricks.

[grave] On the thirteenth of July, he pushed a poker between the bricks, and found they were loose. Under them, buried in lime, were human remains. They were badly damaged, and there was no head, no limbs and no bones. [measured] With them was part of a man's pyjama jacket, and some hair in a hair curler.

[thoughtful] A warrant went out for Crippen and Le Neve. Their descriptions appeared in newspapers across the world. [softly] But they had vanished.

## turning_point

[thoughtful] On the twentieth of July, the Montrose left Antwerp, in Belgium, for Quebec in Canada. Among its passengers were a Mr Robinson and his son. Its captain was Henry Kendall.

[curious] Kendall had read the newspapers. And over the first days at sea, he watched the Robinsons closely. The boy's clothes didn't fit. His trousers were held up with safety pins. He had a very feminine way of walking. And father and son were seen holding hands, and the boy squeezing his father's hand in a way no son did. [measured] Kendall noticed that Mr Robinson had the marks of glasses on his nose, but wasn't wearing them. He had shaved off a moustache.

[serious] The Montrose had something few ships had at that time: a wireless telegraph, a radio set that could send messages in Morse code over the sea. On the twenty-second of July, as the ship moved out of range of Britain, Kendall had a message sent to the ship's owners in England. It said he had strong suspicions that the London cellar murderer and his accomplice were among his passengers.

[building] It reached Scotland Yard. Dew knew the Montrose was slow. So he took a faster ship, the Laurentic, from Liverpool, and raced across the Atlantic behind it. [thoughtful] And because of the wireless, the whole world could follow the chase in the newspapers, day by day. Everyone knew, except the two people on the Montrose.

[low, steady] The Laurentic overtook the Montrose and reached Canada first. And on the morning of the thirty-first of July, dressed as a river pilot, Dew climbed aboard. [softly] Crippen was arrested on deck. Ethel Le Neve was found in her cabin, still dressed as a boy. [thoughtful] It was the first time a wireless message had helped to catch a suspect.

## aftermath

[measured] Crippen was brought back to London and tried for murder at the Old Bailey in October 1910. The prosecution said he had poisoned Cora with hyoscine, a drug that can calm or sedate, and in large doses kill. He had bought a large amount of it in January, just before she disappeared, and traces were found in the remains.

[thoughtful] A young pathologist, Bernard Spilsbury, told the court that a piece of skin from the remains showed a scar, matching an operation Cora was known to have had. The pyjama jacket was traced to a set Crippen had bought. [serious] Crippen said the remains must have been there before he moved in. The jury took less than half an hour to find him guilty. He was hanged at Pentonville Prison on the twenty-third of November, 1910. Ethel Le Neve was tried separately, as an accessory, and found not guilty.

[thoughtful] The record does not settle everything. Crippen never confessed. In 2007, an American team tested a sample said to be from the remains, and reported that the DNA did not match Cora's relatives, and that it came from a man. [measured] Other scientists questioned whether the sample was really from the case, and how reliable such old tissue could be. It remains disputed.

[softly] And at the centre of it is Cora. A woman who loved music, parties and her friends, and who sang under a stage name she hoped would be famous. [gently] Her friends were the ones who would not accept the story that she had simply gone away. They kept asking, until somebody listened.
