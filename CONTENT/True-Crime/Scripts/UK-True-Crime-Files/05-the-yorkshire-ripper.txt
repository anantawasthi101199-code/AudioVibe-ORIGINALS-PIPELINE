## hook

[low, steady] It's the twenty-sixth of June, 1979, in a crowded room at police headquarters in Wakefield, West Yorkshire. Reporters, photographers and television crews are packed in. At the front sits George Oldfield, the Assistant Chief Constable leading the hunt for the man the newspapers call the Yorkshire Ripper.

[thoughtful] Oldfield presses play on a cassette recorder. And out of the speakers comes a man's voice, with a strong north-eastern accent. [measured] It says it is the killer. It taunts Oldfield by name, and says the police are no nearer catching him.

[serious] Oldfield believes the voice is real. The tape has been sent with letters that he is convinced came from the killer. It will be played on radio and television, and on telephone lines that anyone in Britain can ring and listen to.

[softly] It is a hoax. And it will send the biggest police investigation in the country looking in the wrong place, while more women die.

## context

[softly] Behind every headline about this case are the women whose lives were taken. Between 1975 and 1980, thirteen women were murdered, across West Yorkshire and in Manchester, and at least seven more survived attacks. [gently] Many of them were mothers. Some were teenagers.

[thoughtful] Wilma McCann, a mother of four in Leeds, in October 1975. Emily Jackson, from Morley. Irene Richardson. Patricia Atkinson. Jayne MacDonald, who was sixteen and had just started her first job in a shoe shop. Jean Jordan, in Manchester. Yvonne Pearson. Helen Rytka, eighteen. Vera Millward. Josephine Whitaker, nineteen, a bank clerk in Halifax. [softly] Barbara Leach, a university student in Bradford. Marguerite Walls, a civil servant. And Jacqueline Hill, a twenty-year-old student in Leeds.

[serious] It's important to say this plainly, because at the time it was not always said. Some of the women had worked in prostitution, and some police officers and newspapers spoke as if that made them lesser victims. In 1979, a senior officer publicly said the killer had now turned to innocent women. [thoughtful] Families of the earlier victims were deeply hurt by that. Every one of these women was somebody's daughter, and none of them deserved what happened.

[measured] This was the West Yorkshire of the late 1970s. Mill towns and cities, terraced streets, red-light districts in Leeds and Bradford, and a police force using paper, typewriters and card indexes. No computers. [pause] And that would matter enormously.

## investigation

[thoughtful] The first murder, of Wilma McCann, was in October 1975. Three months later, in January 1976, Emily Jackson was killed. Detectives saw the same kind of injuries, and began to suspect one man. By 1977, after the murders of Patricia Atkinson and Jayne MacDonald, they were sure.

[serious] In October 1977, after Jean Jordan was killed in Manchester, police found a brand-new five-pound note hidden in her handbag. [curious] It was traced to a batch paid out in wages by a bank in Shipley, near Bradford, to staff at a number of local firms. Detectives interviewed thousands of men who might have received it. One of them was a lorry driver named Peter Sutcliffe, who worked at one of those firms. [measured] He was interviewed. He gave an alibi, and his wife backed it up. He was not taken further.

[thoughtful] The investigation grew huge. The major incident room, the office where every piece of information was gathered, filled with card index files. Hundreds of thousands of names, vehicles and statements, all written on paper. [measured] By the end, the weight of the paper was so great that the floor of the incident room had to be reinforced. [serious] With so much information, links that should have been obvious were buried. The same man, or the same car, could appear on different cards, in different drawers, and nobody would put them together.

[grave] Then came the letters. In 1978, three letters arrived, two to George Oldfield and one to a newspaper, signed Jack the Ripper and postmarked Sunderland, in the north-east of England. In June 1979 came the tape. [thoughtful] Saliva on one envelope suggested the writer had a particular blood group. Oldfield became convinced the letters and the tape were real. Police began to rule out suspects who didn't have a north-eastern accent, or the right blood group.

[serious] Peter Sutcliffe was from Bingley, near Bradford, with a Yorkshire accent. Over the years, he was interviewed nine times in total, about the five-pound note, about his car being seen in red-light districts, and in other checks. [measured] Each time, he wasn't connected to the whole picture. And after the tape, the accent seemed to rule him out.

[grave] After the tape was played, three more women were murdered: Barbara Leach in September 1979, Marguerite Walls in August 1980, and Jacqueline Hill in November 1980. [softly] In November 1980, a friend of Sutcliffe's, Trevor Birdsall, sent an anonymous letter to the police naming him as a suspect, and then went to a police station to repeat it. It was filed. Nothing was done.

## turning_point

[low, steady] It's the evening of the second of January, 1981, in Sheffield, in South Yorkshire. On a quiet road called Melbourne Avenue, two police officers on routine patrol, Sergeant Robert Ring and Constable Robert Hydes, notice a brown car parked in the dark, with a man and a woman inside.

[thoughtful] They check the number plates. The plates don't belong to that car. They're false. [measured] The officers decide to arrest the driver. He gives his name as Peter Sutcliffe, and he asks if he can go behind a nearby wall to relieve himself before they take him in. They let him. [softly] The woman in the car was safe. The officers had very possibly saved her life without knowing it.

[serious] At the police station, Sutcliffe's name was checked, and officers saw he had been interviewed in the Ripper investigation before. He was sent to Dewsbury to be questioned. [building] And Sergeant Ring, back on duty, heard about it, and remembered that trip behind the wall. He went back to Melbourne Avenue. [grave] On the ground, by the wall, he found a hammer and a knife.

[measured] On the fourth of January, after nearly two days of questioning, Peter Sutcliffe confessed. [thoughtful] He wasn't found by the huge incident room, or the letters, or the tape. He was found by two officers checking a number plate.

## aftermath

[measured] Sutcliffe's trial was held at the Old Bailey in London in May 1981. He admitted killing the women, but pleaded guilty to manslaughter, saying he had a mental illness. The jury rejected that, and on the twenty-second of May he was convicted of thirteen murders and seven attempted murders. He was given twenty life sentences. He died in custody in 2020.

[serious] The hoaxer was finally identified by his DNA in 2005. He was a man from Sunderland named John Humble, and in 2006 he was jailed for eight years for perverting the course of justice.

[thoughtful] After the trial, the government asked Sir Lawrence Byford, a senior police inspector, to review the investigation. His report, delivered at the end of 1981, found serious failures. The incident room had been overwhelmed by paper. Information wasn't linked. Too much weight was given to the letters and tape. [measured] It found that Sutcliffe could and should have been identified earlier. The full report was kept secret until 2006. [softly] It led to changes in how major investigations are run in Britain, including a computer system, so that information would never again be lost in a room full of cards.

[gently] What the record cannot tell us is exactly which lives might have been saved, if the warnings had been joined up sooner. [softly] What it can tell us is who they were. Thirteen women, among them a girl starting her first job, a bank clerk, two students, and a mother of four from Leeds. [tender] They are the reason this case is still remembered.
