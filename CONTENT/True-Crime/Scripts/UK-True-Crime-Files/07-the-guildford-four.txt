## hook

[low, steady] It's Saturday the fifth of October, 1974, in Guildford, a market town in Surrey, south-west of London. It's a little before nine in the evening. [measured] The Horse and Groom pub is packed. Many of the young people drinking there are new army recruits from the training camps nearby, out for the evening in their own clothes.

[grave] At about ten to nine, a bomb hidden in the pub explodes. Half an hour later, a second bomb goes off at another pub in the town, the Seven Stars, which has just been cleared. [thoughtful] Five people are killed, and more than sixty are injured. A month later, a bomb is thrown into a pub in Woolwich, in south-east London, killing two more.

[softly] Within weeks, the police have arrested four young people, three from Northern Ireland and one from London. They have confessed. And none of them did it.

## context

[warmly] The five people killed in Guildford were all young. Four were army recruits. Two were women serving in the army, Ann Hamilton and Caroline Slater, both still in their teens. Two were young men, William Forsyth, eighteen, and John Hunter, seventeen. [gently] And Paul Craig was twenty-two, a civilian, out for the evening.

[thoughtful] Britain in 1974 was in the middle of what people called the Troubles, the violent conflict over Northern Ireland, which is part of the United Kingdom. The Provisional IRA, an armed group that wanted Northern Ireland to join the Republic of Ireland, was carrying out bombings in England. [serious] People were frightened, and the pressure on the police to find the bombers was enormous.

[thoughtful] Weeks later, in November, bombs in two Birmingham pubs killed twenty-one people. Within days Parliament passed the Prevention of Terrorism Act, which let the police hold suspects for up to seven days without charge.

[measured] Gerry Conlon was twenty, from Belfast, a young man who had drifted to London and lived in a hostel. Paul Hill, his friend from Belfast, was the same age. Paddy Armstrong, also from Belfast, was twenty-four. And Carole Richardson, seventeen, was from London, Armstrong's girlfriend. [thoughtful] They were living rough, in squats and hostels, and some of them were involved in petty crime. None of them had any link to the IRA.

## investigation

[measured] Surrey Police got a tip that Paul Hill might be involved. He was arrested in Southampton on the twenty-eighth of November, 1974, and held under the new law. [serious] Through him, the police arrested Conlon in Belfast, and then Armstrong and Richardson in London.

[thoughtful] All four signed confessions to the bombings, after days of questioning without lawyers. [measured] They later said they had been threatened, deprived of sleep and food, and in some cases beaten, and that they had signed to make it stop. The police denied it. Their confessions did not match each other, or the evidence from the scenes, in important ways.

[serious] There was no forensic evidence. No fingerprints, no traces of explosives, no witnesses who could place them at the pubs. [thoughtful] Carole Richardson had an alibi: on the evening of the bombing she had been at a rock concert in south London, many miles away, and witnesses said they had seen her there. It was not believed.

[measured] The trial began at the Old Bailey in September 1975. On the twenty-second of October, all four were convicted of murder. They were given life sentences. The judge said he wished they had been charged with treason, which could still carry the death penalty.

[grave] The arrests had spread wider. Gerry Conlon's father, Giuseppe, a sick man who had come over from Belfast to help his son, was arrested with members of a family called the Maguires, relatives of the Conlons in London. Seven of them were convicted of handling explosives, on scientific tests that were later discredited. [softly] Giuseppe Conlon died in prison in 1980, still saying he was innocent.

[thoughtful] In December 1975, two months after the trial, police arrested four IRA men after a siege in Balcombe Street, in London. They told police they had carried out the Guildford and Woolwich bombings, and that the four in prison had nothing to do with it. [measured] At an appeal in 1977, the judges did not accept that this cleared the four. The convictions stood.

## turning_point

[measured] For the next twelve years, families, priests, journalists and politicians campaigned. Two former Home Secretaries joined in. In 1987 the government ordered a new police inquiry, run by a different force, Avon and Somerset Police.

[serious] In 1989, those investigators found the turning point in Surrey Police's own files. They looked at the typed notes of the interviews with Paddy Armstrong. [thoughtful] Detectives had told the court these notes were written at the time, during the questioning. But the investigators found drafts showing the notes had been rewritten, with passages added and changed, after the event.

[grave] If the interview records had been altered, the confessions could not be trusted. And the confessions were the whole case. [measured] The Director of Public Prosecutions, the head of the prosecution service, told the Court of Appeal he could no longer defend the convictions.

[softly] On the nineteenth of October, 1989, the court quashed them. Gerry Conlon walked out of the front of the Old Bailey into a crowd of cameras. He and the others had spent fifteen years in prison.

## aftermath

[thoughtful] In 1991, the Maguire Seven's convictions were also quashed. Three Surrey police officers were charged with conspiring to pervert the course of justice. They were tried in 1993, and acquitted. [measured] A government inquiry led by a retired judge, Sir John May, reported in 1994 on what had gone wrong, and the case, with others like it, led to changes in how police interviews are recorded, now on tape. In 2005 the Prime Minister, Tony Blair, publicly apologised to the families.

[thoughtful] What the record also shows is this. Quashing the convictions meant the law no longer held these four responsible. It did not answer every question about the bombings. [measured] The men from Balcombe Street were convicted of other crimes and imprisoned, but nobody has ever been convicted of the Guildford bombings.

[softly] Gerry Conlon died in 2014. Paul Hill, Paddy Armstrong and Carole Richardson rebuilt their lives as they could. Carole Richardson died in 2012.

[gently] And in Guildford, a memorial remembers the five who never came home from a Saturday night in town. Ann Hamilton, Caroline Slater, William Forsyth, John Hunter and Paul Craig.
