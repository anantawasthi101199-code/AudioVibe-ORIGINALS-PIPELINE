## who_and_what

[low, steady] It's a summer afternoon, the first of July, 1874, in Germantown, a leafy part of Philadelphia, in America. Two little brothers are playing on the pavement outside their family home. Walter is six. Charley is four, with curly fair hair. [measured] A horse and wagon pulls up, with two men in it. They've stopped by before, and given the boys sweets. Today they offer to take them to buy fireworks for the Fourth of July.

[thoughtful] The boys climb in. A few miles away, the men stop outside a shop and give Walter a coin to go in and buy fireworks. [serious] When Walter comes out, the wagon is gone. And Charley with it.

[softly] Their father was Christian Ross, a shopkeeper. Days later, letters began to arrive, badly spelled and threatening. They demanded twenty thousand dollars for Charley's safe return. [thoughtful] It was the first ransom kidnapping in America to be widely reported. The police advised the family not to pay, fearing that if they did, children all over the country would be taken. Search after search followed, and arrangements to hand over money fell through.

## ending

[serious] In December 1874, five months later, two burglars were shot breaking into a house in Brooklyn, New York. One of them, dying, said they had taken Charley Ross, and that the other man knew where he was. [grave] But the other man was already dead. Walter was brought to see the bodies, and identified them as the two men from the wagon.

[thoughtful] Charley was never found. For decades, hundreds of boys and men came forward claiming to be him. None of them were. [measured] His parents spent the rest of their lives searching, and the warning not to take sweets from strangers became something parents everywhere began to say.

[softly] Charley Ross was four years old, with fair curly hair, and he only wanted to buy fireworks. [gently] Nobody knows what happened to him.
