## who_and_what
[steady] On a September morning in 1840, in a courtroom in Tulle, in central France, the country's most famous chemist opened a set of test tubes in front of a young woman accused of murder. The whole of France was waiting to see what he had found.

[measured] She was Marie Lafarge, born Marie Capelle, twenty-four years old, well educated and orphaned, and raised on the fringes of Paris high society. In 1839 her family arranged her marriage to Charles Lafarge, an iron-works owner, through an agency, and she was told he had a fine estate. When she arrived at Le Glandier in the Correze region, she found a damp, rat-filled ruin and a business deep in debt.

[thoughtful] That December, Charles went to Paris to raise money. She sent him a cake, and he fell badly ill after eating it. He came home in January 1840 and got worse, with vomiting and cramps. Members of his family said they saw Marie stirring a white powder into his food and drink. She had bought arsenic from a chemist, and she said it was for the rats. Charles died on the 14th of January.

[measured] Arsenic was then the commonest poison in Europe, and for a long time it had been nearly impossible to prove. But in 1836 a British chemist, James Marsh, had invented a test that could reveal the tiniest trace of it.

## ending
[thoughtful] At the trial, local chemists used the Marsh test on the body and found nothing. Marie's supporters cheered. The prosecution then called in Mathieu Orfila, the leading toxicologist in France, and he tested it again, in front of the court. He found arsenic, and he showed the court that the earth around the grave held none. It was one of the first times that scientific evidence of this kind had decided a major trial.

[measured] On the 19th of September 1840 she was found guilty, and sentenced to life with hard labour, a sentence later changed to life in prison. The case split France. There were those who were sure she was a cold-blooded poisoner, and those who saw an innocent young woman wronged by clumsy science. Some later experts have questioned how the samples were handled, and that doubt was never settled.

[steady] She wrote her memoirs in her cell, and she insisted on her innocence for the rest of her life. She developed tuberculosis, and she was freed in 1852, in the last months of that illness. She died that same year, at thirty-six.
