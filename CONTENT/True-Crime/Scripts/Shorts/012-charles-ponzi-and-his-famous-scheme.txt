## who_and_what
[steady] In the summer of 1920, a line of people stretched down School Street in Boston, in the heat, carrying their savings in their hands. They were waiting to give them to a small, smartly dressed man in a straw hat.

[measured] He was Charles Ponzi, an Italian who had arrived in America in 1903 with, he said, two dollars and fifty cents in his pocket. He had worked as a dishwasher and a waiter, and he had served time in a Canadian prison for forging a cheque. In 1919 he came across an international reply coupon, a slip of paper you could buy in one country and swap for stamps in another.

[thoughtful] Because of the war, money in Italy had lost much of its value, so a coupon bought cheaply there could be swapped for more stamps in America. Ponzi said he could turn that into a fortune. He set up the Securities Exchange Company, and promised investors fifty per cent profit in forty-five days. In January he had a few customers. By July, by his own later account, a million dollars a week was coming in. Police officers, shopkeepers, and immigrant families put in everything they had.

[measured] In truth, it was never done. To cash in that many coupons would have taken around a hundred and sixty million of them, and only about twenty-seven thousand were in circulation. He was buying almost none. He paid the early investors with money from the later ones.

## ending
[thoughtful] A Boston financial writer, Clarence Barron, did the sums, and the Boston Post went after him. On the 26th of July the state asked him to stop taking money. Then the newspaper printed his prison record. Auditors found him millions of dollars short. On the 13th of August 1920 he was arrested.

[measured] Six banks collapsed. Around forty thousand people had put in about fifteen million dollars, and many got back only about thirty cents in the dollar, after years of court cases. Ponzi pleaded guilty to mail fraud, served time in federal prison and then in Massachusetts, and was deported to Italy in 1934.

[steady] He died in the charity ward of a Rio de Janeiro hospital in 1949, nearly blind, and with seventy-five dollars to pay for his burial. Every year, a new scheme is given his name. The people in that line on School Street are mostly forgotten: the waiters, the clerks and the widows, holding out what they'd saved.
