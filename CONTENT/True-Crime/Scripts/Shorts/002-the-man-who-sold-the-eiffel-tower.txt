## who_and_what

[low, steady] In 1925, a con man called Victor Lustig sat in a Paris café, reading a newspaper. The article said the Eiffel Tower was costing a fortune to repair, and some people thought it should be pulled down. [curious] Lustig had an idea.

[thoughtful] He had fake government papers printed, and posed as a senior official from the ministry in charge of the tower. Then he invited six of the biggest scrap metal dealers in Paris to a meeting at one of the grandest hotels in the city.

[slyly] He told them, in strict secrecy, that the government had decided to sell the Eiffel Tower for scrap. Seven thousand tonnes of iron. Because it was so sensitive, he said, nobody must know until the deal was done. He took them on a tour of the tower. [measured] And he invited them to send him their bids.

[curious] He watched them carefully, and chose the one he thought most eager and least sure of himself, a dealer called André Poisson, who badly wanted to be taken seriously. [knowingly] When Poisson seemed doubtful, Lustig hinted that, as a poorly paid official, he could use a little extra. Poisson relaxed. A government man asking for a bribe seemed very convincing.

## ending

[serious] Poisson paid for the tower, and the bribe on top. Lustig took the cash and fled to Vienna. [wry] And then he waited, reading the Paris newspapers. Nothing appeared. Poisson was too embarrassed to tell the police he'd been fooled.

[thoughtful] So, the story goes, Lustig came back and tried to sell the tower again. This time the dealer went to the police, and Lustig escaped just in time.

[measured] Years later, he was caught printing fake money in America, and spent his last years in Alcatraz prison. [softly] The Eiffel Tower, the thing he sold, still stands over Paris.
