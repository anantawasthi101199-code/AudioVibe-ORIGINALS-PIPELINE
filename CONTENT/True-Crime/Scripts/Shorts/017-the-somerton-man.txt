## who_and_what

[steady] At about half past six on the morning of the 1st of December 1948, a couple walking on Somerton Park beach in Adelaide, in South Australia, found a man sitting against the sea wall, well dressed, in a suit and polished shoes, and dead.

[measured] He looked to be in his forties, fit and strong, with nothing to say who he was. He had no wallet and no identity papers. The labels had been cut out of his clothes. In his pockets were an unused train ticket, a bus ticket, a comb, and a packet of chewing gum. The doctors found his organs swollen and suspected poison, but they could not find any. Nobody came forward to claim him.

[steady] Months later, a police search of his trousers turned up a tiny hidden pocket, sewn in near the waist. Inside was a rolled scrap of paper with two printed words, Tamam Shud, from Persian. They mean it is finished. They are the last words of the Rubaiyat of Omar Khayyam, a famous book of old Persian poems about life and death.

[measured] After a public appeal, a man handed in a copy of that book. It had been thrown into the back seat of his car, parked near the beach, around the time of the death. The last page had been torn out, and the tear matched the scrap. On the back cover, pencilled faintly, were five lines of capital letters, which nobody has ever read for certain, and the phone number of a young nurse who lived nearby. She told police she did not know the man.

## ending

[thoughtful] He was buried in 1949 under a headstone that said only the unknown man. For seventy years he had no name. In 2021 he was exhumed, and in 2022 researchers using DNA and family trees said they had identified him as Carl Webb, an electrical engineer from Melbourne, born in 1905, who had left his wife and vanished from the records. Police in South Australia said they were working to confirm it.

[measured] What has never been settled is how he died, and why. The coroner could not say, and the letters in the back of the book have never been read.

[steady] If he was Carl Webb, he was forty-three, a man who liked to write poetry and bet on horses, who walked down to a summer beach one evening and sat with his back to the wall, a long way from home.
