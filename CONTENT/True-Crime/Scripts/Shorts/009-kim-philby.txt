## who_and_what

[low, steady] It's a wet evening in January 1963, in Beirut, Lebanon. A British journalist is supposed to be at a dinner party with his wife. He never arrives. [measured] Days later, his wife receives a note, and then nothing. He has vanished.

[thoughtful] Kim Philby was charming, clever and well connected, the son of a famous explorer. At Cambridge University in the early 1930s, he became a communist, and in 1934, as a young man, he was recruited by Soviet intelligence, the spy service of the Soviet Union, Russia's communist state.

[serious] They told him to look respectable, and to get inside British secret service. He did. In 1940 he joined MI6, Britain's foreign intelligence service, and he rose fast. [measured] By 1944 he was running the very section set up to spy on the Soviet Union. Everything it planned, Moscow knew.

[grave] In 1945, a Soviet official in Istanbul named Konstantin Volkov offered to defect to Britain, and to name Soviet spies working inside British intelligence. Philby made sure he was the one sent to handle it, and he warned Moscow. [softly] Before Philby arrived, Volkov and his wife were carried onto a plane to Moscow, bandaged, on stretchers. They were never seen again. In the years that followed, men Britain sent secretly into Albania, in eastern Europe, were met by waiting troops.

[thoughtful] In 1951, two of Philby's old friends, also spies, fled to Moscow, and suspicion fell on him. He was forced out of MI6. [pause] But nothing was proved, and in 1955 a government minister told Parliament there was no evidence he had betrayed his country.

## ending

[measured] Philby moved to Beirut and worked as a journalist, and he quietly kept helping British intelligence. Then new evidence came from a Soviet defector. In January 1963, an old MI6 friend flew out and confronted him, and Philby admitted to part of it.

[serious] Days later, he disappeared. In July 1963 the Soviet government announced he was in Moscow. He was never charged or tried. He lived there until he died, in 1988.

[thoughtful] How much damage he did, and how many people died because of what he passed on, has never been fully counted.

[gently] One of them was Konstantin Volkov, who tried to change sides in 1945, and was carried out of Istanbul on a stretcher before anyone could listen to him.
