## hook

[low, steady] It is the afternoon of Monday, the twenty-seventh of April, 1959, in Bombay, now Mumbai. In a flat on Nepean Sea Road, a smart street on a hill above the sea, a businessman named Prem Ahuja has just come out of the shower. He has a towel around his waist.

[measured] There is a man at his bedroom door. He is an officer of the Indian Navy, and he is carrying a brown envelope. Inside the envelope is a revolver.

[grave] A few minutes later, the servants hear shots. Three of them. [thoughtful] The naval officer walks out of the bedroom, past Ahuja's sister, down to his car, and drives away.

[softly] He does not run. Within the hour, he will walk into a police station and say what he has done. [pause] And yet the question of whether he was a murderer would divide a whole city, and take more than two years and three courts to settle.

## context

[thoughtful] The naval officer was Commander Kawas Manekshaw Nanavati. He was thirty-seven, a Parsi, from the small community of Zoroastrians whose families came to India from Persia centuries ago. He was a respected officer, second in command of an Indian warship, with a promising career.

[warmly] His wife was Sylvia, who was English. They had met in England, married, and had three young children. They lived in Bombay, in a flat with a view of the sea. [measured] Nanavati was often away at sea for weeks at a time.

[thoughtful] Prem Ahuja was a businessman, about thirty-four, from a Sindhi family. The Sindhis came from Sind, a region that became part of Pakistan in 1947, and many had moved to Bombay after that. Ahuja ran a car dealership, he was well known in the city's social life, and he was unmarried. He was a friend of the Nanavatis, and while Nanavati was at sea, he and Sylvia began an affair.

[serious] That Monday, the twenty-seventh of April, Nanavati was at home. Sylvia was quiet and distant. He asked her what was wrong. [pause] And she told him.

## investigation

[measured] What Nanavati did next was set out later in court, step by step, and its order mattered.

[thoughtful] First, he drove Sylvia and the children to the Metro cinema, and left them there to watch a film. Then he drove to his ship in the harbour. He told an officer there that he needed a revolver because he was going to drive alone to Ahmednagar, a town some distance from Bombay, at night. He signed out a revolver and six bullets, and put them in a brown envelope.

[measured] He drove to Ahuja's office. Ahuja was not there. He drove to Ahuja's flat, in a building called Jeevan Jyot, and went up.

[serious] What happened in the bedroom was seen by nobody else. Nanavati's account was that he asked Ahuja whether he would marry Sylvia and look after the children. Ahuja, he said, replied that he would not marry every woman he slept with. Nanavati said the two men struggled for the envelope, and the gun went off. [thoughtful] The prosecution said something different: that Ahuja was shot where he stood, unarmed, in a towel, and that Nanavati had gone there to kill him.

[measured] After the shots, Nanavati drove to the office of the Provost Marshal, the Navy's own police. Then, on their advice, he went to the police, and handed himself in to a deputy commissioner.

[thoughtful] The city took sides at once. A popular weekly paper called Blitz, edited by Russi Karanjia, took up Nanavati's cause. It printed him as a wronged husband and an officer and a gentleman, and Ahuja as a seducer who had it coming. [measured] Copies sold out. Parsi groups rallied around Nanavati. Many in the Sindhi community supported Ahuja's family, and the prosecution. It was said that street sellers sold toy revolvers named after Nanavati, and towels named after Ahuja.

[serious] The trial began in September 1959, in the Bombay Sessions Court, in front of a jury of nine. [pause] In India then, in some cities, serious crimes were still tried by juries.

## turning_point

[low, steady] On the twenty-first of October, 1959, the jury came back. By eight votes to one, they found Commander Nanavati not guilty of murder.

[measured] The crowd outside cheered. But the judge, Ratilal Mehta, did something that judges were allowed to do then, and rarely did. He decided that no reasonable jury could have reached that verdict on the evidence, and he referred the case to the Bombay High Court.

[serious] In March 1960, the High Court judges looked at the evidence again. They pointed to the order of events: the cinema, the false reason for the revolver, the drive across the city. They found Nanavati guilty of murder, and sentenced him to life in prison.

[thoughtful] That was the turning point. The verdict of the jury, which much of the public had loved, had been set aside by judges. [measured] Nanavati appealed to the Supreme Court of India. In November 1961, the Supreme Court upheld the conviction.

## aftermath

[thoughtful] Nanavati served about three years. In 1964, the Governor of Maharashtra, Vijaya Lakshmi Pandit, pardoned him. By then, Ahuja's sister, Mamie, had said she would not oppose a pardon. Nanavati, Sylvia and the children later moved to Canada. He died there in 2003.

[measured] The case is often said to have ended jury trials in India. The truth is more gradual. Juries were already used only in a few places. After the Nanavati case, more states stopped using them, and the new Code of Criminal Procedure, passed in 1973, left them out altogether. [thoughtful] The case did not do that alone. But many people saw in it what could happen when a jury was swayed by a newspaper, and by who the accused man was.

[serious] The case has been retold many times, in books and in films. In most of them, it is Nanavati's story, the wronged husband, the officer, the trial.

[softly] Prem Ahuja is usually only the man in the towel. [gently] He was thirty-four, a businessman who had made his way in a new city after his family lost their home in Sind. Whatever anyone thought of the affair, he was unarmed, in his own bedroom, on a Monday afternoon. Three courts heard the case, and the two highest of them agreed that his life had been taken from him, and that it had been murder.
