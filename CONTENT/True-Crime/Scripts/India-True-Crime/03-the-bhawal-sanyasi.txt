## hook

[low, steady] It is early in 1921, in Dhaka, in eastern Bengal, which is now the capital of Bangladesh. Along the river, on a raised embankment called the Buckland Bund, people stroll in the evenings.

[curious] For weeks, a man has been sitting there, day and night, in the open. He is a sanyasi, a Hindu holy man who has given up home and possessions. His body is covered in ash, as is the custom. His hair is long and matted. He barely speaks.

[thoughtful] People begin to stop and stare at him. Then they begin to whisper. They say he looks exactly like a prince who died twelve years ago.

[softly] That prince was Ramendra Narayan Roy, of the Bhawal estate, one of the richest families in Bengal. [pause] Everyone believed he had been cremated, in the hill town of Darjeeling, in 1909.

## context

[thoughtful] Bhawal was a huge estate just north of Dhaka, with hundreds of villages, forests and farmland. The family who held it lived in a palace in a town called Jaydebpur. The British government allowed families like this to collect rents from the land, and they were enormously rich.

[measured] Here is the family. The old head of the estate died in 1901, leaving three sons, known as the kumars, the princes. The eldest was Ranendra. The second, and the one this story is about, was Ramendra. The youngest was Rabindra.

[warmly] Ramendra, the second prince, was by most accounts a cheerful, careless young man. He loved hunting, horses and parties, and he had little interest in the estate's accounts. [thoughtful] In 1902 he married a girl named Bibhabati Devi. She had a brother, Satyendranath Banerjee, who will matter a great deal in this story.

[serious] By 1909, Ramendra was ill. He had syphilis, a disease that could not be cured then. In April, he travelled to Darjeeling, a hill town in the north of Bengal, for the cool air. With him went his wife Bibhabati, her brother Satyendranath, a family doctor and servants.

[grave] On the eighth of May, 1909, the news came back from Darjeeling that Ramendra was dead, at the age of about twenty-five. His body, it was said, had been cremated there the next morning. [measured] The family held the funeral rites. Within a few years, both his brothers had died as well, and none of the three princes left a child. The estate was taken over by the government's Court of Wards, an office that managed estates with no adult heir. Bibhabati lived on with her family, on an income from the estate.

## investigation

[thoughtful] In 1921, Ramendra's sister, Jyotirmoyee Devi, heard about the holy man on the embankment. She invited him to the palace at Jaydebpur, and he came.

[curious] Over several visits, she looked at him closely. She saw a scar here, a mark there. She said his eyes, his walk, his way of sitting were her brother's. [building] In May 1921, in front of a large crowd, the sanyasi was asked if he was the second prince. He said that he was.

[measured] His story, as he told it, went like this. In Darjeeling, he had fallen into a deep faint and been taken for dead. His body was carried to the cremation ground at night. Then a terrible storm broke, and the men carrying him ran for shelter. A group of wandering holy men found him, still breathing, and took him away. He had lost his memory, and he travelled with them for years, until his memory slowly came back.

[serious] The crowd believed him. Tenants on the estate began paying their rents to him. But his wife, Bibhabati, said he was an impostor, a stranger who had studied the family and was after the estate. Her brother Satyendranath said the same. And the government, which was running the estate, sided with them.

[thoughtful] There was something darker in the claim as well. The sanyasi's supporters said Ramendra had not simply fallen ill in Darjeeling. They said Satyendranath and the doctor had poisoned him, so that Bibhabati's family would gain from his death. [measured] Those were allegations, and nobody was ever tried for them.

[serious] In 1930, the sanyasi went to court. He sued in the district court of Dhaka, asking to be declared Ramendra Narayan Roy, and to be given his share of the estate. [pause] It became one of the longest and most famous trials in the history of India.

## turning_point

[low, steady] The trial began in 1933, in front of a judge named Pannalal Basu. Over nearly three years, more than a thousand witnesses gave evidence. Villagers, servants, doctors, photographers, people from Darjeeling.

[curious] The court had no fingerprints from the prince, and no blood tests. So it compared bodies. Photographs of the young prince and the sanyasi were laid side by side. Doctors examined the sanyasi for the marks the prince was known to have had: scars, a mole, the shape of his ears, and signs of the old disease.

[measured] And the court heard about that night in Darjeeling. Witnesses described a storm. Records were checked for which cremation had taken place, and when. Bibhabati's side brought evidence too: they said the sanyasi spoke Bengali badly, which a Bengali prince would not, and that he was really a man from Punjab.

[building] On the twenty-fourth of August, 1936, Judge Basu gave his judgment. He found that the sanyasi was Ramendra Narayan Roy. [serious] He also found that the body carried out in Darjeeling that night was never cremated.

## aftermath

[thoughtful] Bibhabati appealed. In 1940, the Calcutta High Court heard the case, three judges together. Two of them agreed with Judge Basu. One disagreed, and wrote that he thought the claimant was an impostor. [measured] Bibhabati appealed again, to the Privy Council in London, the highest court for British India. On the thirtieth of July, 1946, the Privy Council dismissed her appeal. In law, the sanyasi was the prince.

[grave] Two days later, on the first of August, 1946, he died after a stroke. He never took over his share of the estate.

[serious] So the court decided who he was, and it never decided what happened in Darjeeling. The poisoning was alleged, and never tried. Bibhabati never accepted the verdict. [thoughtful] A historian named Partha Chatterjee, who wrote a whole book about the case, pointed out that even after every judgment, people who knew the family stayed divided.

[softly] And at the start of it was a young man in a hill town in May 1909, ill, far from home, around the age of twenty-five. [gently] Whichever way you read the evidence, something was done to Ramendra Narayan Roy in Darjeeling that he never chose. Either he died there, young, and a stranger later took his name. Or he lived, and lost twelve years of his life, and most of the rest of it trying to get his name back.
