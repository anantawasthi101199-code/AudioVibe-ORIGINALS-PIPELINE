## hook

[low, steady] It is the early 1830s, somewhere beside a road in central India. A grove of trees, a little way back from the dust of the track. A British officer named William Sleeman stands under the branches with a group of labourers holding spades.

[measured] Beside him is a prisoner, a man named Feringeea. He has been captured, and to save his own life he has agreed to talk. He walks slowly through the grove, stops, and points at the ground.

[grave] The labourers dig. A few feet down, they find bones. Feringeea moves on a few paces and points again. They dig again. More bones.

[softly] These are travellers. Men who set out along this road to go home, or to trade, or to make a pilgrimage, and who never arrived. Nobody had known where they were. [pause] And the man pointing at the ground says he knows, because he helped to put them there.

## context

[thoughtful] To understand that grove, you need to picture travel in India two hundred years ago. There were no trains yet. People walked, or rode in carts, often for weeks. A soldier going home on leave carried his savings in his clothes. A merchant carried goods and coins. Pilgrims carried what they had.

[warmly] Travellers joined together for safety. If you met a friendly group heading the same way, you walked with them. You shared their fire in the evening, and their food, and their stories. That was simply how the roads worked.

[measured] And when somebody did not come home, nobody could say where they had gone. The roads were long. There were rivers, wild animals, illness, ordinary robbers. A family waited, and then stopped waiting.

[thoughtful] The British East India Company ruled much of India by then. It was a trading company that had become a government, with its own army and its own officials. William Sleeman was one of those officials. He was born in Cornwall, in the far south-west of England, in 1788, and came to India as a young army officer. He learned local languages, and he was curious about the people around him.

[measured] In the 1820s, Company officials began to hear about gangs they called Thugs. The word came from a Hindi word meaning a cheat, someone who deceives. The officials came to believe that these gangs joined travellers on the road, gained their trust over days, and then, at a chosen moment, strangled them with a cloth and buried them. [pause] Sleeman made it his work to find out.

## investigation

[thoughtful] Sleeman's method was simple, and it was new. Instead of chasing one robbery at a time, he collected information. Who was seen on which road, with whom, on which day. He wrote it all down and drew up family trees and maps of where suspected gang members lived.

[measured] The most important source was men like Feringeea. A captured man who agreed to give evidence against the others was called an approver. In return, he was spared execution. Each approver named others. Those men were arrested, and some of them became approvers too.

[serious] From their statements, Sleeman and his officers built up a picture. The approvers described a whole way of life: gangs that travelled in the cool season, a secret slang they used among themselves, signals that told the stranglers when to act, and a pickaxe, used to dig the graves, that some said was treated as sacred and offered to the goddess Kali.

[thoughtful] In 1835, the Company set up a special office to deal with it, which became known as the Thuggee and Dacoity Department. Dacoity meant robbery by armed gangs. Sleeman led it, from a base in the town of Jabalpur, in central India.

[measured] In 1836, a new law was passed. Under the Thuggee Act, simply being a member of a gang of Thugs was a crime, punishable by imprisonment for life. [serious] That was unusual. Normally, the prosecution had to prove that you had done a particular thing to a particular person. Under this law, the evidence of approvers that you belonged to a gang could be enough.

[thoughtful] Over the following years, thousands of men were tried. Many were hanged. Many more were sent to prison for life, or transported to prison islands far away. Their wives and children were often moved to Jabalpur too, where a school was later set up to train them in work such as weaving.

[measured] By the 1840s, the officials said the roads were safe again. Sleeman was praised as the man who had ended the Thugs. [pause] But the story he told about them was about to become far bigger than the roads.

## turning_point

[low, steady] In 1836, Sleeman published a book. It contained a long word list of the gangs' secret slang, and long accounts from the approvers, in their own words, of how they lived and what they did.

[thoughtful] Readers in Britain were gripped. The book and the reports around it described the Thugs as a single, ancient religious cult, spread across India, killing in the name of a goddess, passed from father to son for centuries. A popular novel followed, called Confessions of a Thug, written by another officer, Philip Meadows Taylor. [measured] The image that took hold was of India as a land where murder could be a religion, and where only British rule could put a stop to it.

[serious] That is the turning point in this story, and it happened on paper. The killings on the roads were real. The bones in the grove were real. But from the moment the reports reached Britain, the case stopped being about missing travellers, and became about a picture of India itself.

## aftermath

[thoughtful] Historians have looked at this case again, carefully, and many now see it differently. A historian named Kim Wagner, who studied the original records, concluded that the gangs were real, and that they did rob and kill travellers. [measured] But he found that the picture of one ancient, organised cult was largely built up from the statements of approvers, men who were talking to save their own lives, to officials who already believed in the cult and asked questions that fitted it.

[serious] It also matters what that picture was used for. The same idea, that some groups were criminal by birth, carried on into later laws. In 1871, a law called the Criminal Tribes Act listed whole communities as born criminals. It was repealed in 1949, after India became independent. [thoughtful] So the record leaves two things side by side. Real crimes, and a story about those crimes that went much further than the evidence could carry.

[measured] What the record mostly does not keep is the names of the people who died. In the grove, the bones were counted. They were rarely named.

[softly] They were soldiers going home with their pay. Traders with a cart of cloth. Families on a pilgrimage, walking for weeks to a holy river. [gently] People who shared their fire with friendly strangers on a long road, because that was what you did, and who were waited for at home, long after anyone could have told their families where they were.
