## mystery

[curious] Did you know that a fish scientists thought had died out with the dinosaurs was found on a fishing boat in 1938? [warmly] This is the story of the coelacanth, and the young museum worker who would not let it be thrown away. It begins in East London, a port town in South Africa, just before Christmas.

## story

[thoughtful] Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer was thirty-two, and she ran a small natural history museum in East London. The local fishermen knew her, and when they caught something odd, they called her. [curious] On the twenty-second of December, 1938, a trawler came in, and its captain sent word. She went down to the docks, and among the sharks and rays on deck, she saw a fin sticking out of the pile.

[with quiet awe] She pulled the fish out. It was about one and a half metres long, a deep shining blue, with faint silver spots. And its fins were strange. They grew out of fleshy stalks, almost like little legs. [thoughtful] She had never seen anything like it. She took it back to the museum in a taxi, though the driver was not keen on the smell.

[serious] There was a problem. It was hot, and no one had a fridge big enough to keep a fish that size. The fish began to rot. So she had it stuffed, and kept the skin. She drew a quick sketch and posted it to James Smith, a chemistry lecturer at a university in Grahamstown who knew more about fish than almost anyone in the country.

[building] Smith was away for the holidays. When he finally saw the sketch, he was stunned. Those fins, that tail. It looked like a coelacanth, a kind of fish known only from fossils in rock, which scientists believed had died out about sixty-six million years ago. [pause] He hurried to East London in February 1939, and looked at the stuffed fish.

## close

[delighted] It was a coelacanth, alive in the sea all along. Smith named it Latimeria, after her, and in 1952 a second one was caught off islands near Madagascar, proving there were more. [warmly] Today they are known to live in deep caves off eastern Africa and Indonesia, and the first one, stuffed, still stands in the museum in East London.
