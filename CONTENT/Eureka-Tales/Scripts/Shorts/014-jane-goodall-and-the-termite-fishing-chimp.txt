## mystery

[curious] Did you know that in 1960 the definition of a human being was overturned by a chimpanzee holding a twig? [warmly] This is the story of Jane Goodall, a young Englishwoman with no degree, and the small thing she saw on a hillside in Tanzania.

[thoughtful] It begins by the shores of Lake Tanganyika, in the summer of 1960, in the forest of Gombe, with a twenty-six-year-old sitting very still on a rock, and watching chimpanzees run away from her.

## story

[measured] Goodall had loved animals all her life, and she saved up by working as a waitress to go to Africa. There she met Louis Leakey, a famous fossil hunter, who hired her as a secretary. Leakey had a theory that studying wild apes could tell us about our earliest ancestors. He picked her to do it, because she had patience, and because she had no training to tell her what she ought to be seeing.

[curious] For months it was a failure. The chimps fled when they saw her, and she was running out of money and time. She got a fever, probably malaria. She kept coming back to the same spot, at the same time, in the same clothes. Slowly, they let her get closer. One older male, with a grey chin, was the first to stop fleeing. She named him David Greybeard. The scientists of the time gave animals numbers. She gave them names, and she was later criticised for it.

[warmly] Then, on a damp morning that November, she saw David crouched at a termite mound. He poked a long stem of grass into a hole in the mound, drew it out, and picked off the termites clinging to it with his lips. She was astonished, but it was what she saw next that truly mattered. He took a leafy twig and stripped off the leaves, on purpose, to make it fit.

[thoughtful] That was the point. In 1960, textbooks said that one thing set humans apart from all other animals: we were the toolmakers. "Man the toolmaker" was the phrase. And here was a chimp, not just using a tool, but making one.

## close

[measured] She sent the news to Leakey by telegram, and his reply became famous. It said we must now redefine tool, redefine man, or accept chimpanzees as humans. Scientists have since found crows, dolphins and many other animals that use tools. Goodall spent the rest of her life at Gombe and travelling the world for conservation, and she died in 2025 at the age of ninety-one. The termite mounds are still there, on the slopes at Gombe, and the chimps still go fishing.
