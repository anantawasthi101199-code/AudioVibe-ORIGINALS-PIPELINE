## mystery

[curious] Did you know the first human being ever to go into space didn't land in his spacecraft, and came down by parachute in a field, in front of a startled farmer and her granddaughter? [warmly] This is the story of Yuri Gagarin, and the hundred and eight minutes that changed what a person could do.

[thoughtful] It begins with a carpenter's son in a Russian village, in a house the German army took over during the Second World War.

## story

[measured] Gagarin was born in 1934. His family lived in a dug-out in the ground for much of the war. He trained as a moulder in a foundry, pouring hot metal into shapes, and he took flying lessons at a local club. It changed his life. By 1960 he was a Soviet air force pilot, one of twenty men picked for a secret programme. He was just five foot two, which helped, since the capsule was tiny.

[curious] Nobody truly knew what space would do to a person. Some doctors feared a human could not swallow, or might go mad, with no gravity. So the controls of the capsule, Vostok 1, were locked, and the code was in a sealed envelope, in case he lost his reason. Almost every part of the flight was automatic. Two of the test rockets in the run-up had blown up. On the morning of the twelfth of April 1961, he walked out to the rocket at Baikonur, in what is now Kazakhstan. As it lifted off, he called out, "Poyekhali!" which means, "Off we go!"

[thoughtful] He went once around the Earth, as high as about two hundred miles, travelling at about seventeen thousand miles an hour. He reported the water and pencil floating, and he ate and drank without any trouble. The sky, he said, was very dark, and the Earth was bluish. Coming back was the frightening part. A cable failed to separate, so the capsule spun as it came through the atmosphere, in a ball of fire. Then came the plan that the Soviets kept secret for years. The capsule's landing was too hard to survive. So, about four miles up, he was thrown out in his ejection seat, and came down by parachute.

## close

[warmly] He landed near the Volga River, in a field near the town of Engels. A farmer, Anna Takhtarova, and her granddaughter saw a man in an orange suit and a white helmet walking towards them, and they backed away. By his own account he called to them that he was a friend, a Soviet, and that he needed a phone. It was one hundred and eight minutes since take-off. Within eight years people had walked on the Moon. And a foundry worker had been the first to see the whole blue edge of the world.
