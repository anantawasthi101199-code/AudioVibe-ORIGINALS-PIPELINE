## mystery

[curious] Did you know that in June 1816, snow fell in New England, frost killed the crops in July, and Europe went hungry, because of a mountain on an island most of them had never heard of? [warmly] This is the story of the year without a summer, and how science finally tracked it back to a volcano.

[thoughtful] It begins in April 1815, on Sumbawa, an island in what is now Indonesia. A mountain called Tambora, which had been quiet for centuries, begins to rumble.

## story

[grave] On the tenth of April, Tambora blew apart. It was the biggest eruption in recorded history. The explosions were heard more than a thousand miles away. Ash buried villages, and tens of thousands of people on the island and its neighbours died, in the eruption and the famine after it.

[thoughtful] Then, a year later, on the other side of the world, the weather went strange. In June 1816, snow fell in the north-east of America. Lakes froze in summer. In Europe it rained and rained. Wheat rotted in the fields, prices soared, and there were food riots. [softly] At a house by Lake Geneva in Switzerland, a group of young writers were kept indoors by storms, and one of them, Mary Shelley, began to write Frankenstein.

[curious] People at the time blamed sunspots, or ice, or even the new lightning rods. Almost nobody thought of a volcano in the East Indies. News travelled slowly, and the link seemed impossible.

[thoughtful] The clues came later. In 1883 another Indonesian volcano, Krakatoa, erupted, and for months, sunsets around the world glowed red, from fine material spread through the high sky. [a beat] And in 1913, an American weather scientist, William Humphreys, put it together. Big eruptions, he showed, throw fine dust and droplets far above the clouds, where they spread round the whole planet and act like a thin sunshade.

## close

[delighted] That was the answer. Tambora had blasted vast amounts of sulphur gas high into the sky, where it formed a haze of tiny droplets that reflected sunlight back into space, and cooled the world for more than a year. [warmly] Scientists watched it happen again in 1991, when Mount Pinatubo in the Philippines erupted, and the whole planet cooled by about half a degree.
