## mystery

[curious] Did you know that until 1846 the best surgeon in any city was simply the fastest one, because every patient was awake? [warmly] This is the story of ether, and of the Boston dentist who put a man to sleep in front of a packed room.

[thoughtful] It begins on the morning of the sixteenth of October 1846, in a steep, round operating theatre at Massachusetts General Hospital, with the seats full of doctors and students.

## story

[measured] Think about what surgery meant then. There was no way to stop the pain. Patients were held down by strong men and given a shot of whisky if they were lucky. A good surgeon took off a leg in a couple of minutes. People died of the shock, or chose to die of their illness.

[curious] William Morton was a young dentist who wanted to fit false teeth, which meant pulling out the old roots first, and patients would not let him. A chemist friend told him about ether, a sweet-smelling liquid. Students at parties breathed it in to get giddy. Morton tried it on his dog, and then on himself, and he passed out. In September he pulled a tooth from a patient who felt nothing.

[thoughtful] He asked for a public test, and the senior surgeon, John Collins Warren, agreed. Warren was nearly seventy, and he had no patience for quacks. The patient was a young printer, Gilbert Abbott, with a tumour on his neck. At the set time, Morton was late. He was having the glass flask finished. Warren looked round the room and picked up his knife, and then, at the last moment, the door opened.

[warmly] Morton held the flask to Abbott's mouth, and in a few minutes, the young man's head dropped. Warren cut. The room waited for the scream. The doctors watching knew that scream very well. It did not come. Abbott moved a little and muttered, and that was all. When it was over, and he woke up, he said it had felt as if his neck had been scratched.

## close

[measured] Warren turned to the rows of silent men and said, in words that those present wrote down, "Gentlemen, this is no humbug." Within weeks the news had crossed the Atlantic, and in December a London surgeon took off a leg under ether. Surgery could now be slow and careful, and the operations we rely on today could begin. That theatre is still there in Boston. They call it the Ether Dome.
