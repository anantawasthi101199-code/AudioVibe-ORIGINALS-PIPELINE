## mystery

[curious] Did you know the first painkilling gas was discovered by a young man who breathed it for fun, wrote down that it could stop pain in operations, and then nobody listened for forty-four years? [warmly] This is the story of laughing gas, and of the chemist Humphry Davy, who came closer than anyone and walked away.

[thoughtful] It begins in 1799, in Bristol, in a house full of glass jars, bags and tubes. It is a new place for treating illness with gases, and its young chemist is twenty years old.

## story

[thoughtful] Davy's job was to test gases, to see if any could cure disease. Back then, there was nothing at all to take away pain in surgery. People were held down and operated on awake.

[curious] One gas was called nitrous oxide. A well-known doctor had claimed it was deadly. [with a smile] So Davy did what chemists of the time often did. He tested it on himself. He filled a green silk bag with the gas, put the mouthpiece to his lips, and breathed.

[delighted] He felt a thrilling, tingling warmth. Colours seemed brighter. He laughed, and walked up and down the room. He gave it to friends, including poets, and they laughed too, and wrote about it. Soon people called it laughing gas.

[thoughtful] Then something else happened. Davy was cutting a wisdom tooth, and his gums ached. When he breathed the gas, the pain faded away. [a beat] In 1800 he wrote a long book about his experiments. And in it, one sentence: that because the gas seemed able to destroy physical pain, it might be used with advantage during surgical operations.

[softly] And then he moved on. He became a famous chemist in London, found new elements, and invented a safety lamp for miners. Nobody tried his sentence. For decades, laughing gas was a party trick, breathed at fairs and shows for a laugh.

## close

[building] In December 1844, an American dentist named Horace Wells watched one of those shows. A man high on the gas gashed his leg on a bench, and did not even notice. [delighted] The next day, Wells breathed the gas himself, and had a colleague pull one of his teeth. He felt nothing. [warmly] Davy's one sentence, forty-four years late, had come true, and today the same gas is still given to people in dentists' chairs and to women in labour.
