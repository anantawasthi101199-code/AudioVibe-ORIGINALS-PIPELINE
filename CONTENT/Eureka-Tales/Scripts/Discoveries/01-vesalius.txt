## opening

[warmly] This is the story of Andreas Vesalius, a young doctor in sixteenth-century Italy, who found out that the most trusted book in all of medicine was wrong about the human body, and proved it with his own hands.

[curious] There are four people in this story. Galen, a Roman doctor who lived nearly fourteen hundred years before it, and whose books every doctor still learned from. Vesalius himself, born in Brussels, who became a professor in Padua. His old teacher in Paris, Jacobus Sylvius, who worshipped Galen. And an artist from the studio of the great painter Titian, who drew the pictures that made Vesalius famous.

[thoughtful] It begins with a strange way of teaching, a stolen skeleton, and a jawbone that didn't match the book.

## before

[warmly] Galen was a brilliant doctor in the second century, in the days of the Roman Empire. He wrote hundreds of books about how the body works, and for more than a thousand years they were treated almost like holy texts. If Galen said it, it was true.

[serious] But there was a problem nobody talked about. In Galen's time, cutting open a human body was not allowed. So Galen had dissected animals instead: pigs, dogs, oxen and especially apes. And he assumed that what he saw inside them was what was inside us.

[curious] By the 1500s, universities did allow the occasional human dissection, usually the body of an executed criminal. But picture how it was done. [with a smile] A professor sat in a tall wooden chair, high above the table, reading aloud from Galen. He never touched the body. Below him, a barber-surgeon did the cutting. And another assistant pointed with a stick at whatever the professor was reading about. If what they found didn't match the book, everyone assumed the body was odd, not the book.

[warmly] Andreas Vesalius was born in Brussels in 1514, into a family of doctors and chemists who served the royal court. As a boy, he cut open mice, moles, cats and dogs to see how they were put together. [thoughtful] He studied medicine in Paris under Jacobus Sylvius, a famous teacher who loved Galen more than anyone. But Vesalius was impatient with the high chair. He wanted to see for himself. In Paris, he and his friends went to a cemetery to study the bones of the dead, and fought off stray dogs to do it.

[intense] Back home in Leuven, he wanted a whole skeleton. So, by his own account, he went out at night to the gallows outside the town, where the body of an executed criminal had been left hanging, and took down the bones, piece by piece, and smuggled them home past the town gates.

[delighted] In 1537, he went to Padua, in northern Italy, one of the best medical schools in Europe. He passed his final exams with such distinction that the very next day, aged just twenty-three, he was made professor of surgery and anatomy.

## wrong

[determined] And the first thing he did as professor was climb down from the chair. Vesalius did the cutting himself, with students crowded around the table, so close they could see every detail. He explained as he went, and had large drawings made so they could follow.

[curious] The judge in Padua helped him, sending him the bodies of executed criminals, and the story goes that he even timed executions to suit Vesalius's lectures. For the first time, someone was studying human bodies again and again, carefully, with Galen's book open beside them.

[serious] And the more he looked, the more he found that Galen was wrong. [building] Galen said the human lower jaw was made of two bones. In people, it's one. Galen said the breastbone had seven parts. In people, it has three. Galen described a beautiful web of blood vessels at the base of the brain. Vesalius searched and searched. It wasn't there. [knowingly] It is there, in an ox, and in a sheep.

[thoughtful] Slowly, he understood. Galen had not been careless. He had been describing animals. And for thirteen hundred years, nobody had checked.

[grave] This was dangerous. Saying Galen was wrong was like saying the Bible was wrong. It could end a career. Many professors simply refused to believe their eyes. [pause] Vesalius decided to put what he had seen into a book so complete, and so clear, that nobody could argue with it.

## payoff

[with wonder] In 1543, when he was twenty-eight, his book came out. It was called On the Fabric of the Human Body, and it was enormous. Seven parts, around seven hundred pages, and more than two hundred pictures. [delighted] The pictures were the wonder of it. Carved on wooden blocks by artists from Titian's workshop, they showed skeletons and bodies posed like living people, standing in Italian landscapes, one leaning thoughtfully on a spade, another with the muscles peeled back layer by layer, as if it were walking through the countryside. Doctors had never seen anything like it.

[quietly impressed] Page after page, Vesalius showed the body as it actually is, and quietly corrected around two hundred of Galen's mistakes.

[darkly] Not everyone was pleased. His old teacher, Sylvius, was furious. He called Vesalius a madman, making a cruel joke of his name. And he suggested an extraordinary excuse for Galen: perhaps the human body had changed since Roman times.

[thoughtful] But the book was too good to ignore. Within a few years, Vesalius had become personal doctor to the Holy Roman Emperor, Charles the Fifth, one of the most powerful men in Europe. Doctors across the continent began cutting, looking and checking for themselves.

[softly] Vesalius died in 1564, aged only fifty, on his way back from a journey to Jerusalem, when his ship was wrecked and he was stranded on a Greek island.

## close

[warmly] Remember that tall wooden chair, high above the table, where a professor sat reading from an ancient book, never touching the body below. Vesalius climbed down from it. That was the whole of his revolution: he looked, and believed what he saw, even when the most famous book in the world disagreed.

[curious] And in that same year, 1543, another book was printed, by an old churchman in Poland, which did to the heavens exactly what Vesalius had done to the body.
