## opening

[warmly] This is the story of Nicolaus Copernicus, a churchman in a cathedral town in Poland, who worked out that the Earth is not the centre of the universe, but spinning and racing around the Sun, and who was so afraid of what people would say that he kept it almost secret for thirty years.

[curious] There are four people in this story. Ptolemy, an astronomer in ancient Egypt whose picture of the sky everyone still trusted. Copernicus himself. A brilliant young professor called Georg Rheticus, who arrived on his doorstep and changed everything. And a preacher called Andreas Osiander, who added a page to the book that Copernicus never agreed to.

[thoughtful] It begins with a planet that goes backwards.

## before

[warmly] Look up at the sky on any clear night, and it really does seem as if the Earth stays still and everything moves around it. The Sun rises and sets. The stars wheel overhead. The Moon crosses the sky. [knowingly] Almost every culture believed the Earth was the fixed centre of everything, because that is exactly what it looks like.

[curious] Around the year 150, an astronomer in Alexandria, in Egypt, called Ptolemy, wrote the great book that explained how it all worked. The Earth sat in the middle. The Sun, the Moon and the planets went round it in perfect circles.

[serious] But there was a problem. The planets didn't behave. [with wonder] Watch Mars over many weeks, and you'll see it drift slowly across the stars, then stop, then go backwards for a while, then turn again and carry on. How could that happen if it simply circled the Earth?

[thoughtful] Ptolemy's answer was clever. He said each planet moved in a small circle, while the centre of that small circle moved around a big circle. Circles on circles. It was complicated, but it worked well enough to predict where the planets would be. And for more than thirteen hundred years, it was simply how the heavens worked.

[warmly] Nicolaus Copernicus was born in 1473 in Torun, a trading town in Poland. When his father died, his uncle, a powerful bishop, took charge of him and paid for the best education. He studied in Krakow, and then for years in Italy, in Bologna and Padua, where he learned law, medicine, Greek, mathematics and astronomy. [thoughtful] His uncle found him a comfortable job as a canon, an official, at the cathedral in Frombork, a small town on the cold Baltic coast. He spent most of the rest of his life there, doing the church's business, treating the sick, and, in his spare time, watching the sky.

## wrong

[curious] The more Copernicus studied Ptolemy, the more it bothered him. All those circles on circles. Different tricks for each planet. It worked, but it didn't feel like the true design of the universe. [thoughtful] He went back to old Greek writers and found that a few, long ago, had suggested the Earth might move.

[building] So he tried it. What if the Sun stood still in the middle, and the Earth was a planet, spinning once a day and going round the Sun once a year, like the others? [with wonder] Suddenly, the strange backwards loops of Mars made sense. Mars wasn't really going backwards at all. The Earth, on its faster inside track, was overtaking it, the way a faster car on a motorway overtakes a slower one and makes it seem to slide backwards. The order of the planets fell into place. The whole system became simpler and more beautiful.

[serious] Around 1514, he wrote a short summary of his idea by hand and passed a few copies to friends. And then, for decades, he worked on the full version, checking every calculation against his own observations from the cathedral. [grave] But he did not publish it. He was afraid. Not mainly of the church, at first, but of ridicule. A moving Earth sounded absurd. Why didn't we feel it? Why didn't the wind blow us off? He later wrote that he feared being laughed off the stage.

[softly] By the late 1530s, he was in his sixties. His great book sat unfinished in his room, and it looked as if it might never leave Frombork. [pause] Then, in 1539, a visitor arrived.

## payoff

[warmly] Georg Rheticus was twenty-five, a young professor of mathematics from Wittenberg, in Germany, who had heard rumours of the old astronomer's idea and come all that way to learn it. He stayed for two years. He studied every page. He wrote a short, excited report on the idea, which was printed and spread. [determined] And above all, he persuaded Copernicus to let the whole book be published.

[thoughtful] Rheticus took the manuscript to a printer in Nuremberg. But he had to leave before it was finished, and a preacher called Andreas Osiander took over checking the printing. [darkly] Osiander was worried about how the book would be received. So, without asking Copernicus, he added an unsigned page at the front. It said the moving Earth was only a clever way of doing calculations, not a claim about how things really are. Readers for years assumed Copernicus had written it.

[softly] By then, Copernicus had suffered a stroke, and was lying in bed in Frombork, unable to speak. [tender] The story told by a friend is that the first printed copy of his book arrived in his hands on the very day he died, the twenty-fourth of May, 1543.

[quietly impressed] The book was called On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres. At first it caused only a small stir. But it set a slow fire burning. Within a century, astronomers like Kepler and Galileo had taken up the moving Earth, and the old picture of the universe began to fall.

## close

[warmly] Think back to Mars, sliding backwards across the stars, and the circles upon circles that Ptolemy had to invent to explain it. Copernicus saw that the strange loop was not Mars at all. It was us, overtaking.

[curious] He moved the Earth with a book he was almost too afraid to print. The next person in this story, Galileo, would build a telescope, look up, and see the proof for himself.
