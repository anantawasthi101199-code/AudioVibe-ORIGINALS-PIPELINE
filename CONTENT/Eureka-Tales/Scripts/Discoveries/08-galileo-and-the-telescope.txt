## opening

[warmly] This is the story of Galileo Galilei, an Italian professor, who in 1609 built a telescope with his own hands, pointed it at the night sky, and saw things nobody in history had seen, and of how, twenty-four years later, it ended with him on his knees in front of the most powerful court in the Catholic Church.

[curious] At the time, most educated Europeans believed the Earth sat still at the centre of everything, and the Sun, the Moon, the planets and the stars all circled around it. They believed the heavens were perfect, made of a pure, unchanging material, completely unlike the dirty, changing Earth.

[thoughtful] A Polish astronomer, Copernicus, had suggested seventy years earlier that the Earth and the planets go around the Sun. But it was an idea on paper, with no way to see it.

[warmly] By the end, you'll know what Galileo saw through his telescope, why it mattered so much, and why it led to a trial in Rome.

## before

[thoughtful] There are four people in this story. Galileo. Cosimo de' Medici, the young ruler of Florence, who gave him a job. Maffeo Barberini, a cardinal who became Pope and was first his friend. And Galileo's daughter, a nun named Maria Celeste, who wrote to him all her life.

[warmly] Galileo was born in 1564 in Pisa, in Tuscany, in what is now Italy, the son of a musician. He studied medicine, but loved mathematics, and became a professor. [with a smile] He was clever, funny, quick to argue, and quick to make people feel stupid, which made him admirers and enemies in equal measure.

[thoughtful] In 1609 he was teaching at the University of Padua, near Venice. He had three children with a woman he never married. His two daughters were sent to a convent, and the elder took the name Maria Celeste.

[curious] That summer, he heard news from the Netherlands. A spectacle maker had put two lenses in a tube, and found that it made faraway things look closer. [determined] Galileo did not have one. He worked out how it must work, and ground his own lenses. His first made things look about three times bigger. Within months, he had one that made them about twenty times bigger.

[delighted] In August 1609, he climbed the bell tower in St Mark's Square in Venice with a group of senators, and they watched ships at sea hours before anyone could see them from the ground. They doubled his salary. [thoughtful] Then, as autumn came on, he did something nobody had done in that way before. He pointed the telescope up.

## wrong

[with quiet awe] First, the Moon. Everybody had been taught it was a perfect, polished ball. Through his tube, Galileo saw mountains and craters. He watched shadows creep across its surface as the light changed, just like shadows of hills on Earth at sunrise. He even used the shadows to work out how tall the mountains were.

[curious] Then the Milky Way, the pale band across the sky. Through the telescope it broke apart into stars, countless stars, far more than anyone could count with the naked eye.

[thoughtful] On the seventh of January, 1610, he looked at Jupiter, one of the bright planets. Next to it he saw three little points of light, in a straight line, which he took for faint stars. [curious] The next night, they had moved. Night after night he watched and drew them. Sometimes there were three. Sometimes two. On the thirteenth, four.

[delighted] And then he understood. They were not stars. They were moons, four of them, circling Jupiter. [knowingly] Why did that matter so much? Because people said the Earth could not be moving around the Sun, or it would leave its Moon behind. Here was a planet moving through the sky, carrying four moons along with it. Not everything circled the Earth.

[determined] He wrote it all up in a hurry, in a short book called The Starry Messenger, published in March 1610. He named the moons after the Medici family who ruled Florence, and it worked. Cosimo de' Medici made him his court mathematician, with a big salary and no teaching. [warmly] Later that year he saw something else. Venus went through phases, like our Moon, from a thin crescent to full. That could only happen if Venus went around the Sun.

[serious] Not everybody was pleased. Some professors would not even look through his telescope. And some churchmen said that a moving Earth went against the Bible, which spoke of the Sun moving across the sky. [grave] In 1616, the Church declared the idea that the Earth moves around the Sun to be false, and Galileo was warned by a senior cardinal not to hold or defend it. He went quiet.

## payoff

[warmly] Then, in 1623, his old friend Maffeo Barberini became Pope, taking the name Urban the Eighth. He admired Galileo, and they talked. Galileo believed he now had permission to write about the two ideas, so long as he didn't claim the Sun-centred one was proven.

[thoughtful] In 1632, he published a book called the Dialogue, a long conversation between three characters about the two views of the universe. [serious] But it was plain which side won. And the character who argued for the old, Earth-centred view was called Simplicio, which sounds like simpleton. Some of the Pope's own arguments were put in his mouth. [grave] The Pope felt mocked and betrayed.

[serious] Galileo was summoned to Rome, to the Inquisition, the Church court that judged heresy, which means beliefs against the Church's teaching. He was nearly seventy and ill. He arrived in 1633, and was questioned for months. [thoughtful] At one point, it seems, he was threatened with torture, though it was never used on him.

[grave] On the twenty-second of June, 1633, Galileo knelt in a white robe before the court. The judges found him vehemently suspected of heresy. He read out a statement saying he abandoned, cursed and hated the idea that the Earth moves. [softly] His Dialogue was banned. He was sentenced to prison, which was changed to house arrest at his villa in the hills above Florence, for the rest of his life.

[thoughtful] A story spread, much later, that as he stood up, he muttered under his breath, and yet it moves. There is no good evidence that he said it. [warmly] What he did do was keep working. Under house arrest, he wrote one more great book, about the science of motion and the strength of materials, and it was smuggled out and printed in the Netherlands.

[tender] Maria Celeste lived at a convent nearby, and wrote to him almost every week. She sent him sweets and medicines, and when he was condemned, she asked to take over the prayers of penance the court had set him, to lift them off him. She died in 1634, at thirty-three. [softly] Galileo went blind in his last years, and died in 1642, aged seventy-seven.

## close

[thoughtful] In time, astronomers everywhere accepted that the Earth moves around the Sun. In 1992, Pope John Paul the Second said publicly that the Church had been wrong about Galileo.

[warmly] Those four moons of Jupiter he first saw as little points of light in January 1610 are still called the Galilean moons. [gently] On any clear night, with an ordinary pair of binoculars, you can see them yourself, lined up beside Jupiter, just as he drew them.
