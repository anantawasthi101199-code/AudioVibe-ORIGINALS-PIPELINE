## opening

[warmly] This is the story of Mary Anning, a carpenter's daughter from Lyme Regis, a small seaside town on the south coast of England, who, around 1811, at the age of twelve, helped dig the first complete skeleton of a giant sea reptile out of the cliffs, and spent the rest of her life finding creatures nobody had ever imagined.

[curious] In her time, most people believed that every kind of animal God had made was still alive somewhere. If you found strange bones, the animal must be living in some far corner of the world, not yet discovered. Mary's finds were part of what made that belief impossible to hold.

[thoughtful] She was poor, she had little schooling, and she was a woman, which meant the scientific societies of her day would not let her in. The gentlemen scientists bought her fossils, wrote papers about them, and often left her name out.

[warmly] By the end, you'll know what she found in those cliffs, how it changed what people thought about the history of life, and where you can see her work today.

## before

[thoughtful] There are four people to know. Mary herself. Her father, Richard, a carpenter and cabinet-maker. Her brother Joseph, a few years older. And William Buckland, a clergyman and geologist at Oxford University, one of the men who came to Lyme to learn from her.

[warmly] Mary was born in 1799. The Annings were poor, and of their many children only Mary and Joseph lived to grow up. When Mary was a baby, she was famously caught in a thunderstorm at a fair, with three women sheltering under a tree. Lightning struck. The three women were killed. Mary survived, and the town said afterwards that it explained why she was such a bright, lively child.

[curious] Lyme sits under tall grey cliffs made of layers of rock called the Blue Lias. They were laid down on the bottom of a warm sea about two hundred million years ago, and they are packed with the remains of the animals that lived in it. Every winter, storms and rain bring down chunks of the cliff onto the beach, and with them come fossils: curled shells called ammonites, which locals called snakestones, and long bones they called verteberries. Richard Anning walked the beach with his children, gathering them, and sold them from a table outside the house to the wealthy visitors who came to Lyme for the sea air.

[thoughtful] In 1810 Richard died, after a fall from the cliffs, and left the family deep in debt. Fossils were no longer a hobby. They were the family's food. Mary was eleven.

[curious] The next year, Joseph found something on the beach: a long skull, about four feet in length, with a pointed snout full of teeth, and an eye socket as big as a saucer. It looked a little like a crocodile. Then the sea and the cliffs took over the story. It was Mary who kept watch, month after month, and in about 1812, after a landslide, she found the rest of the body. With the help of men from the town, she dug out a skeleton about seventeen feet long, with a backbone, ribs and paddles.

## wrong

[thoughtful] Here is what people believed when they looked at it. A crocodile, some said. A strange fish. The skeleton was sold to the lord of the manor for about twenty-three pounds, and ended up in a London museum, where scholars argued over it for years. Within a few years one of them gave it a name: Ichthyosaurus, the fish lizard. It was neither a fish nor a lizard. It was a reptile that had lived in the sea, and nothing like it was alive anywhere on Earth.

[curious] That question, whether an animal could be gone completely, was the great argument of the time. In Paris, the leading anatomist in Europe, Georges Cuvier, had argued that mammoths and other giant creatures were extinct, meaning that they had died out entirely. Many people in Britain found that troubling. If God had made every creature for a reason, why would he let any of them disappear? Every new animal that came out of the Lyme cliffs, unlike anything living, made the case for extinction harder to dismiss.

[warmly] Mary got better and better at it. She taught herself anatomy from the books visitors lent her, copying out whole papers by hand, with the drawings. She cut open modern fish and cuttlefish to compare their bodies with the fossils. She worked the beach in winter, in the worst weather, because that was when the cliffs fell, and she had to get to a fossil before the next tide broke it up. It was dangerous. In 1833 a landslide missed her by inches and killed her dog, Tray, who went everywhere with her.

[thoughtful] By her twenties, men of science were coming to her. Buckland walked the beach with her. Visitors wrote that she understood the science better than most of the professors who bought from her. And yet the family stayed poor. In 1820 one of her customers, a collector named Thomas Birch, sold his entire collection of her fossils and gave the money to the Annings, because, he wrote, they had found almost everything that was being studied and had nothing to show for it.

[pause] Then, in December 1823, she found something stranger still. A skeleton with a small head, four long flippers, and a neck so long it was longer than the rest of its body.

## payoff

[curious] It was the first complete skeleton of a plesiosaur, a sea reptile that had been known only from scraps. When drawings of it reached Paris, Cuvier himself doubted it. A neck like that, with so many bones, looked impossible, and he suspected it had been faked by joining two animals together. There was a meeting at the Geological Society in London to discuss it. Mary, as a woman, was not allowed to attend. In the end, Cuvier looked at the evidence again and accepted that it was real, and that he had been wrong. Mary's reputation was made.

[thoughtful] She went on. In 1828 she found the first skeleton in Britain of a pterosaur, a flying reptile, with long fingers that held up its wings. She worked out, with Buckland, that the strange stones called bezoar stones found inside some skeletons were fossilised dung. They are now called coprolites, and when you break them open, you find the fish scales and bones of what the animal had eaten. That was the moment the past started to look like a real, working world, with animals eating and being eaten.

[warmly] She did not get the credit she deserved. The men who wrote up her finds often named the collector who bought them, not the woman who found them. She wrote in a letter that the world had used her ill. In the 1830s a bad investment lost most of her savings, and her scientific friends, Buckland among them, raised money to give her a small yearly income. That was rare recognition, and it came from the people who knew best what she had done.

[thoughtful] Mary Anning died in 1847, of breast cancer, at the age of forty-seven. The Geological Society, which had never let her in, did something it had never done before. Its president read a tribute to her at a meeting, the first for anyone who was not a member. The Society did not admit women until 1919.

## close

[warmly] Twelve years after she died, Charles Darwin published his theory of how living things change over time. He needed the history of life to be very long, and he needed animals to be able to vanish. By then, extinction was no longer a shocking idea. A girl on a winter beach at Lyme had helped to make it ordinary.

[curious] Her ichthyosaurs and her plesiosaur are on show today at the Natural History Museum in London. And in 2022, after a campaign started by a young girl from the area, a bronze statue of Mary Anning was put up in Lyme Regis, looking out to the sea, with a hammer in her hand and her dog Tray at her feet.
