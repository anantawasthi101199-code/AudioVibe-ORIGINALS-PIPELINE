## moment

[warmly] You hear a few seconds of a song in a shop, or an advert on the telly. And then it's in your head. All day. Brushing your teeth, on the bus, trying to get to sleep. [with a smile] Often it's not even a song you like, and it's never the whole song. Just the same few lines, round and round.

[gently] There's nothing wrong with you. Almost everybody gets this. In surveys, nine out of ten people say it happens to them at least once a week. It even has a lovely name: an earworm. Psychologists call it involuntary musical imagery, which means music your mind plays without you asking.

## why

[thoughtful] Here's the one thing that explains it. Your brain is very good at finishing things, and a short bit of music feels like an unfinished thing. Think of a tune on a loop as a tab your brain has left open. It keeps flicking back to it, the way your eyes keep going back to a crooked picture on a wall.

[curious] The songs that get stuck tend to be the same kind: a simple, catchy tune, a bit faster than usual, with a jump or a repeat that surprises you slightly. And it's usually only the chorus, because that's the part you know. [knowingly] Earworms also love it when your mind is free: walking, showering, doing the washing up. When your attention has nothing else to do, the loop plays.

[thoughtful] The part of your mind that rehearses sounds, the little inner voice you use to repeat a phone number, is the one doing the replaying. And that gives you a way in.

## one_thing

[warmly] So here's one thing to try, next time a song won't leave. Chew some gum. [gently] It sounds odd, but in a study at the University of Reading, people chewing gum heard their earworm less often, because chewing keeps that inner voice busy. It's a small thing, and your jaw might just switch the song off.
