## moment

[warmly] You're watching something, or reading, or thinking hard about a problem. And then you look down, and you realise you've been biting your nails. Again. You don't remember starting. [a beat] Your thumb is sore, and there's that little flash of annoyance at yourself.

[gently] If that's you, it's not a lack of willpower, and it's not a disgusting habit to be ashamed of. Lots of people do it, and lots of people pick their skin or pull at their hair in the same way. Psychologists call these body-focused repetitive behaviours: habits where your hands go to your own body, often without your noticing.

## why

[thoughtful] Here's the one thing that explains it. Nail biting isn't really about nails. It's about your hands finding something to do when your mind is restless, bored, stressed or deep in thought. [curious] Think of it like a thermostat in a house. When things get a bit too tense, or a bit too flat, the thermostat clicks on and does something to bring things back to normal. Biting is that click. It gives a tiny bit of calm, or a tiny bit of stimulation.

[softly] And that's why telling yourself to stop rarely works. The thermostat still clicks. Your hand just needs somewhere else to go. [gently] The approach with the best evidence is called habit reversal: noticing the moment your hand moves, and giving it a different job.

## one_thing

[warmly] Here's one thing to try today. The moment you notice your hand drifting up, make a gentle fist and hold it for one minute. [gently] Just one minute. That's your hand learning a new place to go. And if your skin is sore, bleeding, or it's really upsetting you, a GP can help.
