## moment

[warmly] You're holding a baby, and you hear yourself. Your voice has gone up high. Your words are stretched out and sing-song. Whooo's a lovely baby? [a beat] And part of you thinks, why am I talking like this? I sound ridiculous.

[gently] You don't. You're doing something parents all over the world do, in every language, usually without being taught. And it turns out that voice is doing real work. Researchers have a name for it: parentese. It's slower, higher and more musical than how we talk to adults, but it uses real words and real sentences.

[thoughtful] A baby is born able to hear the sounds of every language on Earth. By about their first birthday, they've tuned in to the sounds of the language around them, and started to lose the ones they never hear. Every voice they hear is helping them tune.

## why

[curious] Here's the one thing that explains it. Babies don't learn to talk from hearing language. They learn from being talked with. Think of it like learning to play catch. Throwing a ball at a child from across the room teaches them very little. Gently tossing it back and forth, waiting for their throw, teaches them the whole game.

[thoughtful] Parentese does that. The stretched-out vowels make each sound easier to hear. Your face and voice hold their attention. And the pauses leave room for them to answer, with a coo, a babble, a squeal. [warmly] In one study in America, parents were coached to use more parentese and more back-and-forth. Their babies babbled more, and by eighteen months, they knew more words.

## one_thing

[warmly] So here's one thing to try today. Say something to the baby, then stop, and wait. [gently] Count to three in your head and let them have their turn, even if it's only a gurgle. Then answer it, as if it was a word. That little pause is where the talking starts.
