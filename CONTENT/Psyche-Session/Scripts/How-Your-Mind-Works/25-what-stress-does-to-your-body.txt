## opening

[warmly] An email arrives. Four words: can we talk later? And before you have even thought about it, your heart is thumping, your stomach drops, and your shoulders climb up towards your ears. For the rest of the afternoon you can't really eat, and that night you lie awake running through every possible version of the conversation.

[steady] It's like a car sitting in the driveway with the engine running and your foot pressed on the accelerator, revving and revving, with nowhere to go. If that sounds familiar, you're not weak, and you're not overreacting on purpose. Your body is doing exactly what it was built to do. And you're very much not alone.

[thoughtful] I'm Sarah. That thing has a name: the stress response, sometimes called fight or flight. In this one we'll look at what's actually happening in your body, why it feels so exhausting when it never switches off, and what helps it settle.

## picture

[warmly] So let's stay with that car for a moment. Imagine it's built for one job: getting you away from danger fast. Foot down, engine roaring, and off you go. It's a brilliant car for that. Then, once you're safe, you take your foot off, turn the key, and the engine cools.

[steady] Now imagine that car in your actual life. The danger isn't a bear. It's a bill, a deadline, a text you haven't answered, a sick parent, a train that's late again. You can't drive away from any of those. So the engine revs, and you sit there in the driveway, with the fuel burning and the dashboard lights flickering, still holding the shopping.

[thoughtful] That's what stress really is. It's your body getting ready to act, physically, when the thing you're facing can't be solved by acting physically. It's not you being dramatic. It's not a lack of willpower. It's a very old system meeting a very modern kind of problem.

[warmly] And I'll be honest, mine revs at the sight of my own inbox. We all have a driveway like that.

## brain

[curious] People often think stress is all in the mind, a mood you could just decide not to have. But it's actually a whole-body event, and it starts before you've had a single conscious thought.

[steady] When your brain spots a threat, it signals your adrenal glands, two small glands that sit on top of your kidneys. Within seconds they release adrenaline, the fast stress hormone. Your heart beats faster to push blood to your muscles. Your breathing speeds up. Sugar is released into your blood for quick energy. And things your body considers optional in an emergency, like digesting your lunch, are put on pause. That's the thumping heart and the dropped stomach.

[thoughtful] A little later, over minutes, comes the second wave: cortisol, the slower stress hormone, which keeps you fuelled and alert for the long haul. In a short burst, that's useful. It's what gets you through an exam or a hard day. Normally, once the problem has passed, cortisol falls again, and the body goes back to repair and rest.

[steady] The trouble is when the problem never passes. When the threat is constant, the engine never gets turned off. And a body that spends weeks or months revving starts to show the wear: tense muscles and headaches, poor sleep, an upset stomach, catching every cold going, and over time, raised blood pressure. That's not your body giving up on you. It's your body trying to protect you for far longer than it was built to.

## real_life

[warmly] So where does this show up? You get to the end of the week and you're tired in a way a lie-in doesn't touch. You sleep, but you wake at four in the morning with your mind already at work.

[steady] Your jaw aches in the morning, because you've been clenching it all night. Your shoulders are so tight that someone touches them and you jump. You get a headache every Sunday evening, right on time.

[thoughtful] You snap at someone you love over something tiny, the wrong kind of milk, the dishes, and you know as you're saying it that it isn't about the milk. And then, when the busy stretch finally ends and you get a week off, you get ill on the first day of the holiday. That's the one that feels so unfair. But it makes sense: you've finally taken your foot off the pedal, and your body is catching up with everything it postponed.

## feelings

[thoughtful] And on top of all that, there's the voice. Everyone else seems to manage. Why can't I just cope? It's not even that bad. Other people have it worse.

[steady] So you hide it. You smile in the meeting, you say you're fine when someone asks, you keep going. On the outside it looks like you're coping. On the inside, the engine is roaring.

[warmly] Here's the thing about that voice. Telling yourself off for being stressed is another stress. It's one more thing for the alarm to react to. So now there are two loads: the original problem, and the shame about not handling the problem well enough. That's why it can feel so much heavier than the situation looks from outside. You're carrying both at once.

## helps

[warmly] So let's start with what your body is doing for you. It isn't trying to ruin your week. It's trying to keep you safe, with the only tool it has. What it needs is not a lecture. It needs a signal that, for now, the danger has passed.

[steady] The principle is simple. You can't always remove the problem, but you can show your body, physically, that you're safe right now. Pushing through and ignoring it doesn't do that. Small physical signals do.

[thoughtful] The first is your breath. When you breathe out more slowly than you breathe in, your heart rate tends to come down, and your body reads that as a sign of safety. So instead of telling yourself to calm down, which never works, try this. Breathe in for a count of four, and out for a count of six, five or six times. Before: sitting at your desk with your heart thumping at that email. After: the same email, the same desk, and a body that's a notch quieter.

[steady] The second is movement. Your body has been getting ready to run, so let it do something with that fuel. A ten-minute walk at lunch, up and down the stairs, a dance in the kitchen. Before: stewing on the sofa, replaying the conversation. After: the same worry, but you've used some of the fuel, and the engine is running slower.

[warmly] The third is other people. A real conversation with somebody who's on your side is one of the strongest signals of safety a human body can get. Before: carrying it all on your own and saying you're fine. After: one text to a friend that says, honestly, it's been a hard week.

[thoughtful] And the fourth is making the problem smaller. Not solving it, just shrinking the next step. Not sort out my finances, but open that one letter. You don't have to fix everything today. You only have to turn the engine down for a few minutes.

[steady] And if stress has been going on for a long time, if it's stopping you sleeping, eating or getting through the day, please talk to your GP. That isn't failing. It's exactly what they're there for. And if you ever feel you can't keep yourself safe, call 999, or the Samaritans, any time, on 116 123.

## close

[warmly] So think back to that car in the driveway, engine revving, foot down. It was never a broken car. It's a car doing its best to get you out of danger, waiting for someone to tell it the danger has gone.

[thoughtful] And you've been carrying a lot. The fact that you feel it doesn't mean you're weak. It means you've been strong for a long time.

[steady] So here's one tiny thing for today. Once, just once, breathe in for four and out for six, and let your shoulders drop as you breathe out.

[warmly] Foot off the pedal. Key turned. Engine cooling.
