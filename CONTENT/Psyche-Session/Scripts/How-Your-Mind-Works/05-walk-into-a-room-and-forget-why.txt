## opening

[warmly] You're on the sofa, and you think, I need my phone charger. You get up, walk down the hall, step into the bedroom. [a beat] And you stand there. Completely blank. What did I come in here for?

[gently] It's like closing a tab on your computer by accident, and the page you were reading just vanishes. You're not losing your mind. You're not getting forgetful in some worrying way. Almost everybody does this, at every age.

[thoughtful] It even has a name. Researchers call it the doorway effect. [warmly] I'm Sarah, and in this one, we'll look at what's actually happening when you go blank in a doorway, why it feels so silly, and the small things that help.

## picture

[warmly] Picture your day as a book. Not one long page, but chapters. Making breakfast is one chapter. Sitting on the sofa is another. Going upstairs is the start of a new one.

[curious] Your mind keeps each chapter open while you're in it, holding everything that matters right now: the kettle's on, the charger's upstairs, you need to reply to that text. [thoughtful] When the chapter ends, your mind closes it and files it away, ready for the next one. And the thing that tells your brain a chapter has ended, more than almost anything, is walking through a door.

[gently] So when you go blank in the bedroom, your memory hasn't failed. It's done exactly what it's built to do. It closed the chapter. The charger was written on the last page.

[with a smile] And yes, it is a little bit ridiculous that a door frame can do this to us. But here we are, all standing in bedrooms, staring at the wardrobe.

## brain

[curious] People often think forgetting like this means your memory is weak, or that it's a sign of something going wrong. But it's actually about how memory is organised.

[thoughtful] In 2011, a psychologist in America, Gabriel Radvansky, tested it. He had people walk through a room on a computer screen, picking up an object from a table and carrying it to another table. Sometimes the second table was in the same room. Sometimes they walked through a doorway to get there. [knowingly] Then he asked them what they were carrying. People who had gone through a door forgot more often, even when the distance was exactly the same. He found the same thing with people carrying real boxes between real rooms.

[thoughtful] The idea behind it is called event segmentation, which just means your mind chops your life into separate events. A doorway is a boundary between events. Once you cross it, the old event is filed, and things from it are harder to reach, like a document you've saved and closed.

[gently] Not every study finds it every time. In some later experiments, the doorway made little difference, until people were also juggling other things in their heads. [warmly] And that's the clue. When your mind is busy, the doorway is what tips the thought over. That's not a broken memory. It's a full one, being tidy.

## real_life

[warmly] You go upstairs to get a jumper, and come down with a book, and no jumper.

[curious] You open the fridge, stare into it, and close it again, with no idea what you wanted. [thoughtful] You walk into a meeting room at work, and the brilliant point you were going to make in the corridor is gone.

[gently] And the one that stings a bit. Someone asks you to bring them something from the kitchen, and you come back empty-handed, and they look at you. You laugh it off. [softly] But a little part of you feels a flash of embarrassment, and wonders if something's wrong with you.

## feelings

[gently] And then come the thoughts. Why do I keep doing this? I'm so scatterbrained. Am I losing it? [softly] If you're older, or someone in your family has had dementia, that little blank in the doorway can feel genuinely frightening.

[thoughtful] So you hide it. You pretend you came in for something else. You grab a random thing off the shelf so nobody notices. [warmly] And the worry itself takes up room in your mind, which leaves even less space for the next thing you need to remember.

[gently] That's why something so small can feel so heavy. It's the blank, and then the worry about the blank, both at once.

## helps

[warmly] Here's the kind way to see it. Your brain is clearing the desk when you move from one task to the next, so it has room for whatever is in the new room. That's useful. It just sometimes sweeps up the one thing you needed.

[thoughtful] So the trick isn't trying harder to remember. It's carrying the thought across the door in a way that survives the tidy-up.

[warmly] First, say it out loud. Instead of thinking charger as you get up, say, quietly, phone charger, bedroom. [gently] Words you hear yourself say stick better than a passing thought.

[curious] Second, picture it in your hands. Before you stand up, imagine yourself holding the charger, the cable wrapped round it. A picture carries across a doorway better than an idea.

[thoughtful] Third, if you've gone blank, don't search the new room. Go back in your head. Picture where you were sitting, and what you were doing just before you got up. [knowingly] Memory is tied to place, so the old scene is often where the thought is waiting.

[warmly] And when you have a lot on your mind, use something outside your head. A note on your phone, a list on the fridge. You don't have to hold everything.

[serious] One honest note. Forgetting why you walked into a room is ordinary. But if forgetting is new, is getting worse, or other people are noticing that you forget important things, like conversations or appointments, that's worth talking to a GP about. [gently] Asking isn't overreacting. It's looking after yourself.

## close

[warmly] So back to that bedroom, and you, standing there, blank, by the wardrobe. That wasn't your memory failing. That was your mind closing one chapter neatly, so it could start the next.

[gently] Here's one tiny thing to try today. The next time you get up to fetch something, say its name out loud, once, before you reach the door. [softly] Same mind. Same door. You just carried the page across.
