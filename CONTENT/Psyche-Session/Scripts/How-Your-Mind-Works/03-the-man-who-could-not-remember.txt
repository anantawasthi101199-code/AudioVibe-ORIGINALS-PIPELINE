## opening

[warmly] You walk into the kitchen. You stand there. And you have absolutely no idea why you came in. You look at the kettle, the fridge, the window. Nothing. [with a smile] Then you go back to the sofa, sit down, and remember straight away: your glasses.

[gently] If that happens to you a lot, you're not losing your mind, and it's almost certainly nothing to panic about. It's a tiny glimpse of something your brain does all day, every day, so quietly that you only notice it when it slips: turning what's happening now into a memory you can keep.

[thoughtful] The story of how we learned where that happens comes from one man, known for most of his life only by his initials, H.M. I'm Sarah, this is Psyche Session, and today we'll look at what happened to him, why one kind of memory can vanish while another stays, and what that means for your own forgetful moments.

## picture

[warmly] Imagine your memory as a big library. Old books on the shelves, everything you've ever learned or lived. [curious] And at the front desk sits a librarian. All day long, new things arrive: a conversation, a name, what you had for lunch, where you put your keys. The librarian takes each one, decides whether it's worth keeping, and files it on the shelves.

[thoughtful] Now imagine the librarian is gone. The old books are still there. You can still read them. But nothing new can be filed. Everything that arrives sits on the desk for a minute, and then it's swept away.

[gently] That's what happened to Henry. And here's the surprising part, the part that changed how we understand memory. Some things still got learned without the librarian at all. [softly] Memory, it turns out, isn't one thing. It's several, working side by side.

## brain

[curious] People often think of memory as one big store in your head. But Henry's story shows it's more like a group of different systems, each with its own job.

[thoughtful] Henry Molaison was born in 1926, in Connecticut, in America. As a child, he started having seizures, a kind of electrical storm in the brain, after a bicycle accident when he was young. By his twenties, the seizures were so bad he could hardly work or live a normal life. [serious] So in 1953, when he was twenty-seven, a surgeon removed part of the brain on both sides, deep inside, where the seizures seemed to start.

[grave] The seizures got much better. But something else had gone. Henry could no longer make new memories. He could hold a conversation, as long as he kept it in mind. But if he was distracted for a minute or two, it was gone. He would meet the same doctor, again and again, for decades, and greet her as a stranger every time.

[thoughtful] The surgeon had removed most of a part of the brain called the hippocampus, a small curved structure deep inside each side of your head. It's the librarian. It's what takes the things happening now, and turns them into memories you can keep.

[curious] But then a psychologist named Brenda Milner gave Henry a task. He had to trace the outline of a star while he could only see his hand and the paper in a mirror. That's very hard at first, because everything is back to front. [with quiet awe] Henry practised over three days. Each day he had no memory of ever having done it before. And each day, he got better. [softly] His brain was learning a skill without the librarian. Scientists call that procedural memory, the memory for how to do things, and it's kept in different parts of the brain.

[gently] Henry lived until 2008, and a psychologist named Suzanne Corkin studied him for more than forty years. He was, by all accounts, polite, gentle and good-humoured. And everything we now teach about how memory is built started with him.

## real_life

[warmly] You use those two kinds of memory every day, often without noticing which is which. You can type your password perfectly, fast, without looking. But ask you to say it out loud, letter by letter, and you have to watch your fingers.

[curious] You drive home and arrive at your front door, and you can't remember a single thing about the journey. That's your skill memory doing the driving, while your librarian was busy with something else.

[thoughtful] You walk into a room and forget why. That's the librarian, distracted for a moment, not filing the reason before you moved on.

[gently] And the one that worries people. You forget a name you've heard twice. You lose your keys again. And a small cold thought arrives: is something wrong with my brain? Alongside the relief of finding the keys comes a little fear, both at once.

## feelings

[softly] Those thoughts can get loud. I'm getting worse. This isn't normal. What if this is the start of something?

[gently] And because it feels frightening, people often hide it. They laugh it off with friends. They don't mention it. And they lie awake checking every little slip against a list of fears.

[thoughtful] But fear doesn't help memory. Worry fills up the front desk. The more anxious and stretched you are, the less room the librarian has to file anything. [softly] So the forgetting can feel twice as heavy: the slip itself, and the dread you pile on top of it.

## helps

[warmly] Here's the kind way to see it. Most everyday forgetting is your brain choosing what to keep. Your librarian is very busy, and she can't file everything. She lets the unimportant things go so the important ones fit.

[thoughtful] So the trick is not to force your memory to work harder. It's to make the librarian's job easier.

[curious] One: give things a home. Not "I'll remember where I put my keys", but a bowl by the door, every time. Then the keys don't need a memory at all. Your skill memory takes over.

[gently] Two: when something new matters, say it out loud. Not just hearing a name, but saying it back: nice to meet you, Priya. That's you handing the librarian the book and asking her to file it.

[warmly] Three: when you forget why you walked into a room, go back to where you were. Very often, the reason is waiting there, because memories are filed along with the place you were in.

[softly] And four, for the worry. You don't have to test yourself all day. You only have to notice the pattern over a few weeks, not every single slip.

[gently] And here's when to talk to a doctor. If forgetting is getting steadily worse over months. If it's starting to affect your daily life, like getting lost on familiar roads or missing bills. Or if someone close to you is worried about you. [warmly] Your GP can help, and it's always fine to ask.

## close

[warmly] So think back to that library. The front desk gets busy. Books go missing on the way to the shelves. That's not your brain breaking. That's a librarian doing her best on a busy day.

[thoughtful] And remember Henry, tracing that star in the mirror, getting better every day at something he couldn't remember doing. [softly] Your mind is cleverer than any single memory you lose.

[gently] Today, just one thing. Choose one spot, a bowl, a hook, a shelf, and put your keys there every time. [warmly] One home. One less thing to remember.
