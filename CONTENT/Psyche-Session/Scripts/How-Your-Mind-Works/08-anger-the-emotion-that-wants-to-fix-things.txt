## opening

[warmly] Someone cuts in front of you in the queue. Or your partner says that thing again, in that tone. [a beat] And it's like a switch. Your face goes hot, your jaw clenches, your heart thumps, and before you know it, you've snapped.

[gently] It's like a pan of milk on the hob. It sits there, fine, and then in one second it's boiling over the edge. If that happens to you, you're not a bad person. Anger isn't a flaw in your character. Everybody feels it, and lots of people struggle with what to do with it.

[thoughtful] I'm Sarah. In this one, we'll look at what anger actually is, what it does in your body, why the usual advice to let it all out backfires, and what really helps.

## picture

[warmly] Picture a referee on a football pitch. Most of the game, they just jog about. Then someone commits a foul, and they blow the whistle, hard, and stop everything.

[curious] Anger is your inner referee. It blows the whistle when it thinks something unfair has happened, when you've been treated badly, or blocked from something that matters, or someone you love has been wronged. [thoughtful] Its whole job is to stop play, and get the thing put right.

[gently] So anger is not the opposite of caring. It's what caring feels like when something has gone wrong. That's why we're often angriest with the people closest to us, the ones whose behaviour matters most.

[with a smile] The trouble is, our referee is a bit keen. It'll blow for a foul when someone simply walks too slowly on the pavement.

## brain

[curious] People often think anger is a kind of pressure that builds up inside you, like steam in a kettle, and has to be let out, or you'll explode. But that's not what the research shows.

[thoughtful] When something feels unfair, a small part deep in your brain called the amygdala, your threat detector, reacts in a fraction of a second, before you've had time to think. It sets off your body's alarm. Your heart speeds up, your blood pressure rises, your muscles tense, ready to stand your ground. [knowingly] The thinking part of your brain, the bit that weighs things up, takes a moment longer to catch up. That gap is why you sometimes say things you didn't mean.

[curious] In 2002, a psychologist in America, Brad Bushman, tested the steam idea. He made six hundred students angry by giving them rude, insulting comments on an essay they'd written. Then one group hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who'd insulted them. Another hit the bag while thinking about getting fit. A third group just sat quietly for two minutes. [thoughtful] Afterwards, they all had a chance to blast the insulter with loud noise through headphones. The punching-bag group who thought about the insulter were the angriest and the most aggressive. The group who sat quietly were the calmest.

[gently] Letting it out, it turns out, is more like practising it. A big review in 2024 found the same thing across many studies. What calms anger is things that slow your body down, like slow breathing. Getting more worked up doesn't.

## real_life

[warmly] You're driving, someone pulls out in front of you, and you're shouting at your windscreen at a person who can't hear you.

[curious] You come home tired, the kitchen is a mess, and you slam a cupboard door, and the whole house goes quiet. [thoughtful] A colleague takes credit for your work, and you say nothing in the meeting, then replay it all evening in the shower, getting angrier each time.

[softly] And the hardest one. You snap at your child, or your partner, over something tiny. And afterwards you sit on the edge of the bed, ashamed, with that awful mix of still feeling cross and feeling terrible about it at the same time.

## feelings

[gently] Then the inner voice. Why did I do that? What's wrong with me? I'm turning into my dad. [softly] Why can't I just stay calm like everyone else?

[thoughtful] So people hide their anger. They smile and say it's fine when it isn't. They swallow it at work and let it out at home. Some people never get angry on the outside at all, and carry it round as a tight knot in their chest for years.

[warmly] And shame does something sneaky. It makes you feel attacked, by yourself, and feeling attacked is exactly what sets the referee off again. That's why anger can feel so heavy. It's the anger, and the shame about the anger, feeding each other.

## helps

[warmly] Here's the kind way to see it. Your anger is trying to protect you, and the things and people you care about. It's on your side. It just needs a moment before it acts.

[thoughtful] And the principle is this. You don't need to get the anger out. You need to let your body come down, so the thinking part of you can catch up.

[warmly] First, slow your breathing, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath. Before, you'd fire off the angry text straight away. After, you put the phone face down, breathe in for four and out for six, a few times, and then decide. [gently] The text will usually be different, or not sent at all.

[curious] Second, step away, and say you're coming back. I need ten minutes, then let's talk. Walking out in silence feels like punishment to the other person. Saying when you'll be back is what makes it a pause.

[thoughtful] Third, ask what the whistle was for. Underneath anger there's usually something else. Feeling disrespected, ignored, tired, scared, or hurt. [knowingly] Once you find it, you can say that instead. Not you never help, but I'm exhausted and I need a hand.

[serious] And if your anger frightens you, or the people around you, or it ever turns into hurting someone or breaking things, please talk to your GP. Anger management courses and talking therapies really do help. [gently] Asking for that isn't weakness. It's taking your own whistle seriously.

## close

[warmly] So back to that referee on the pitch. Anger isn't the foul. It's the whistle. It's telling you something matters to you.

[gently] Here's one tiny thing to try today. The next time you feel your face go hot, before you say anything, breathe out slowly, once, for a count of six. [softly] One long breath. That's you, taking the whistle back.
