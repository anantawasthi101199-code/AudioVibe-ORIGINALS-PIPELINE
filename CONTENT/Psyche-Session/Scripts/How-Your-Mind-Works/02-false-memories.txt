## opening

[warmly] You and your sister are laughing about a family holiday. You remember it so clearly. The blue tent, the rain, your dad falling in the stream. And then she stops and says, that wasn't you. That was me. You weren't even there. [a beat] And you go quiet, because you can see it. You were sure.

[gently] If that has ever happened to you, you're not lying, and you're not losing your mind. Every human memory works this way. Memory is not a recording you play back. It's more like a story you rebuild, every single time you tell it.

[thoughtful] Psychologists call this false memory: remembering something, vividly and honestly, that did not happen the way you remember it. I'm Sarah, this is Psyche Session, and today we'll look at three things. What's actually happening when a memory changes, why it feels so real, and what helps you hold on to what's true.

## picture

[warmly] Imagine your memory as a box of old photographs, but every photo has been torn into pieces. A colour here, a face there, a feeling, a smell, the weather. [thoughtful] When you want to remember something, you don't pull out one whole picture. You reach into the box, grab the pieces that seem to fit, and glue them together, fast, without noticing you're doing it.

[curious] Most of the time, you get it right, or close enough. But sometimes a piece from another day slips in. A detail from a story someone told you. A scene from a photo you saw later. Something you imagined, once, very clearly. [softly] And once it's glued in, it looks just like all the other pieces.

[gently] So a false memory is not a lie. It's not a sign that you're careless or dishonest. It's what happens when a very good, very fast rebuilding system picks up the wrong piece. [with a smile] And honestly, if I had to rebuild every memory from scratch every time, I'd want to do it fast too.

## brain

[curious] People often think a vivid memory must be an accurate one. If you can see it, feel it, hear it, it must be true. But the research says vividness and accuracy are two quite different things.

[thoughtful] The person who showed this most clearly is a psychologist named Elizabeth Loftus. In 1974, she and her colleague John Palmer showed students short films of car crashes. Then they asked them one question: about how fast were the cars going when they hit each other? [a beat] But they changed one word for different groups. For some it was hit. For others, smashed, or collided, or bumped, or contacted. [curious] The students who heard smashed guessed the cars were going much faster than the ones who heard contacted. One word changed what they thought they saw.

[serious] And then, a week later, the students were asked: did you see any broken glass? There was no broken glass in the film. But the students who had heard smashed were more than twice as likely to say yes. [softly] The word had become a piece of the memory.

[thoughtful] Later, Loftus went further. Her team asked people about real events from their childhood, described by their families, and slipped in one that never happened: getting lost in a shopping centre as a small child, and being found by an older stranger. [gently] About a quarter of the people came to remember it, at least in part. Some added details of their own.

[curious] So here's what's going on. Each time you remember something, your brain rebuilds it. Scientists call this reconstruction: putting a memory back together, rather than playing it back. And each time you rebuild it, it can change a little, and the changed version is the one that gets stored for next time. [softly] Your memory is not failing when it does this. It's doing what it was built to do: keep the gist, the meaning, the feeling, and fill in the rest.

## real_life

[warmly] You'll see this everywhere once you know it. You tell a funny story at a party, and each time you tell it, it gets a little funnier and a little tidier. A year later, the tidy version is the only one you can find.

[thoughtful] You argue with your partner about who said what last week. You are both completely sure. And you are both being honest. [softly] You each rebuilt that evening a little differently, around what mattered most to you.

[curious] You see an old photo of yourself at the seaside, and from then on, you remember that day from the outside, as if you were watching yourself. That's the photo, glued into the memory.

[gently] And the hard one. Someone tells you that you said something unkind years ago. You can't remember it. Or you remember saying it differently. And there's a strange mix of feelings: relief that maybe you didn't, and guilt that maybe you did, both at once.

## feelings

[softly] That can be unsettling. The thoughts come quickly. If I can't trust my own memory, what can I trust? Was I wrong about other things too? What's wrong with me?

[gently] And because it feels embarrassing, people often hide it. They argue harder for their version, just to avoid feeling foolish. Or they go quiet and stop telling stories at all.

[thoughtful] But that worry makes it worse. When you feel ashamed of your memory, you don't check it, you defend it. You stop being curious, and curiosity is exactly what helps. [softly] So it feels heavy for two reasons. There's the wobble of not being sure, and then there's the shame on top of it.

## helps

[warmly] Here's the kind way to see it. Your memory isn't trying to trick you. It's trying to give you a useful story of your life, quickly, so you can make sense of today. Most of the time, it gets the important things right.

[thoughtful] So the goal isn't a perfect memory. Nobody has one. The goal is a little more humility, and a few simple checks for the things that really matter.

[curious] One: write it down soon. Not "I'll remember the meeting", but three short lines that evening: who was there, what was agreed, what happens next. A memory written on the day is far harder to rebuild later.

[gently] Two: when something matters, check it outside your head. Not "I'm sure we booked the hotel", but a quick look at the email. Not "you always say that", but "can we check the message?" [softly] It's not about who wins. It's about getting it right together.

[warmly] Three: when you're asking someone else what happened, ask open questions. Not "was he angry?", which puts the word angry into their memory, but "what was he like?" That's the lesson of the car crash film. Your words can become their memory.

[softly] And four, for the feelings. When you find out you remembered something wrong, you don't have to defend it, and you don't have to feel stupid. You can just say: huh, I remembered that differently. That's all. Everybody does.

[gently] And if a memory is very painful, or you're struggling with things you think may have happened to you, that's worth talking through with a doctor or a trained therapist, someone who knows how memory works and won't put words in your mouth.

## close

[warmly] So think back to that box of torn-up photographs. You're not broken because a piece from another day slipped in. You're human. Your memory is a storyteller, not a camera.

[thoughtful] And you're someone who's willing to check. That's rare, and it's good.

[softly] Today, just one thing. Tonight, write down three short lines about something that happened today, a conversation, a moment, anything. [warmly] Not because you'll forget. Because tomorrow's version of you deserves the real one.
