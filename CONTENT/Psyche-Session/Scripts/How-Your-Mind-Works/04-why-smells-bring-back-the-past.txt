## opening

[warmly] You're walking past a house, and someone inside is cooking. And suddenly you're not on the pavement any more. You're seven, in your grandmother's kitchen, and it's Sunday, and you can almost feel the chair under your legs. [softly] It lasts two seconds. And it hits you harder than any photograph ever has.

[gently] If that happens to you, and it catches you off guard, sometimes happy, sometimes with a lump in your throat, you're not being silly or oversensitive. It's how smell and memory are wired together. They're closer than almost any other two things in your head.

[thoughtful] Writers sometimes call this the Proust effect, after a French novelist who described a taste of cake bringing back his whole childhood. I'm Sarah, this is Psyche Session, and today we'll look at three things. What's happening when a smell brings back the past, why those memories feel so strong, and how you can use it gently.

## picture

[warmly] Imagine your senses are travellers arriving at a big house where your memories live. [curious] Sight, sound, touch and taste come to the front door. They knock, wait, and get shown through the hallway, checked and sorted, before anyone lets them into the rooms.

[thoughtful] Smell doesn't use the front door. Smell has a key to the back door, the one that opens straight into the kitchen, where the feelings and the old memories are sitting round the table. [softly] It walks right in. No waiting, no hallway.

[gently] So when a smell brings back a memory with a rush of feeling, that's not you being dramatic. It's not nostalgia getting the better of you. It's the most direct route into memory that you have. [with a smile] Honestly, I think it's one of the loveliest things about being a person.

## brain

[curious] People often think all our senses go into the brain the same way. But smell is different.

[thoughtful] Most of what you see, hear and touch first passes through a kind of relay station in the middle of the brain, which sorts it before sending it on. That's the front door and the hallway. Smell skips it. The signal from your nose goes to the olfactory bulb, the small part of the brain that handles smell, just above the nose. [curious] And from there it goes almost straight to the parts that deal with emotion and with memory. Including the amygdala, a small part of the brain that gives memories their emotional charge.

[with quiet awe] So a smell arrives already tangled up with feeling. Researchers in Sweden found that the memories brought back by smells tend to be older than the ones brought back by words or pictures. They often come from the first ten years of life. And people describe them as more emotional, and as feeling more like being back there.

[softly] There's one thing to be careful of. Feeling more real doesn't always mean being more accurate. A smell can bring back a feeling perfectly, and get some of the details wrong. [gently] But the feeling is real. That's the part smell is so good at keeping.

## real_life

[warmly] You'll recognise this. You open a box of old clothes, and the smell of the house you grew up in comes out with them.

[curious] You walk into a school for a parents' evening, and that mix of floor polish and dinners puts you straight back at your own desk, slightly nervous about a test.

[thoughtful] Someone walks past wearing the perfume your mum used to wear, and you turn round before you've even thought about it.

[gently] And the hard one. Someone you loved has died, and you find their jumper, or their soap, and you breathe in, and for a second they're right there. And then they're not. And it's comfort and grief, arriving together, in the same breath.

## feelings

[softly] Those moments can be strong enough to knock you sideways. The thoughts come fast. Why am I crying in the middle of a supermarket? I thought I was over this. What's wrong with me?

[gently] And because it can happen anywhere, people often hide it. They blink it back. They hurry on. They don't tell anyone that the smell of oranges still makes them think of a person.

[thoughtful] But being ashamed of it makes it harder. When you push the feeling away, it doesn't leave. It just waits for the next smell. [softly] So it can feel heavy twice over: the sudden memory, and the effort of pretending it didn't happen.

## helps

[warmly] Here's the kind way to see it. Your brain is keeping your most important early memories safe, and smell is the key it kept for them. When a smell takes you back, it's showing you something you cared about.

[thoughtful] So the goal isn't to block these moments. It's to make room for them, and even to use them.

[curious] One: let it land. Not "pull yourself together", but stop for a moment, breathe in, and notice where you've gone. Ten seconds is enough. It usually passes on its own.

[gently] Two: keep the smells that matter. Not a box of things you never open, but one small thing you can reach for. A bottle of the perfume, a bar of the soap, a jar of the spice from that kitchen. [softly] Some people find it a real comfort on hard days.

[warmly] Three: make new ones on purpose. Starting a new chapter of life? A new job, a new home, a new baby? Choose a smell for it, a candle, a tea, a hand cream. Years from now, that smell will bring this time back.

[softly] And four, for the feelings. You don't have to be over anyone or anything. A smell that brings tears is a smell that kept something precious.

[gently] And if a smell keeps pulling you back to something frightening, a memory you don't want, and it's getting in the way of daily life, that's something a doctor or a therapist can help with. You don't have to manage it on your own.

## close

[warmly] So think back to that house, and smell walking in by the back door, straight into the kitchen. That's not your mind being oversensitive. It's your mind keeping the warmest rooms unlocked.

[thoughtful] And you're someone who felt those moments deeply. That's not a weakness. That's what it looks like to have loved things.

[softly] Today, just one thing. Pick one smell from your life right now, your tea, your soap, the air after rain, and breathe it in slowly, on purpose. [warmly] You're making a memory for later.
