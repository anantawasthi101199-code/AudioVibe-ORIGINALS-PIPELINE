## opening

[warmly] Your phone rings, and it's a number you don't know. Your chest goes tight, your hands go cold, and you just watch it ring until it stops. Or there's a party on Saturday, and from Wednesday onwards your stomach knots every time you think about walking through that door.

[steady] It's like a smoke alarm that goes off every time somebody makes toast. Loud, urgent, completely convinced, and nothing is actually on fire. If that sounds familiar, you're not being silly, and you're not doing it on purpose. It happens to a great many people.

[thoughtful] I'm Sarah. The thing you're feeling is anxiety, the close cousin of fear. In this one we'll look at what's really going on when your alarm goes off, why it feels so real, and what actually helps turn it down.

## picture

[warmly] So, picture that smoke alarm on your kitchen ceiling. When there's a real fire, it's the most useful thing in your house. It screams, you get out, you live. You'd never want to take the battery out.

[steady] But this one is set a little too sensitive. It goes off for toast. For a hot shower. For a candle on a birthday cake. And every time it goes off, your whole body reacts as if the house were on fire, because how is your body supposed to tell the difference? The noise is the same.

[thoughtful] That's the difference between fear and anxiety. Fear is the alarm going off because there's a fire in front of you right now: a car coming at you as you step off the kerb. Anxiety is the alarm going off for a fire that might happen: the phone call that might be bad news, the party where people might judge you. It's not weakness. It's not a flaw in your character. It's an alarm doing its job a little too keenly.

[warmly] And between us, mine goes off at voicemail. I still don't really know why.

## brain

[curious] People often think anxiety means something has gone wrong in your brain. But actually, it's your brain's safety system working, just with the setting turned up too high.

[steady] Deep in your brain there's a small, almond-shaped area called the amygdala, your threat detector. It's fast. It reacts in a fraction of a second, before the thinking part of your brain has caught up. When it senses danger, it sets off your body's alarm: heart speeding up, breathing quick and shallow, muscles tense, stomach churning. All of that is your body getting ready to run or to fight.

[thoughtful] Here's why it misfires. For our ancestors, a false alarm cost very little. You jump at a rustle in the grass, it's only the wind, no harm done. But a missed alarm, the one time it was a snake, could cost everything. So the system is built on one rule: better safe than sorry. It would much rather go off for toast a hundred times than miss one real fire.

[steady] And then there's the part almost nobody tells you. Your alarm learns. Every time you avoid the thing that scares you, you feel relief, instantly. And your brain takes a note: that was dangerous, and avoiding it is what kept us safe. So the next time, the alarm goes off a little earlier and a little louder. Avoiding the fear is the very thing that makes it grow. You're not getting worse because you're weak. You're getting worse because your brain is learning exactly what you're teaching it.

## real_life

[warmly] So where does this show up? You see someone you know at the end of the street, and you cross the road and look at your phone, so you don't have to say hello.

[steady] You let calls go to voicemail, and then you put off listening to the voicemail, and then you put off ringing back, until it's been a week and that feels even harder.

[thoughtful] You say yes to the party, and on Saturday afternoon you send the message: so sorry, not feeling great. And the moment you press send, there's this huge wave of relief, and right behind it, the guilt. Both at once. You're glad you're not going, and you're sad that you're not going, and you're on the sofa feeling both.

## feelings

[thoughtful] And then the voice comes in. It's only a phone call. Why can't I just do it? Everyone else finds this easy. What is wrong with me?

[steady] So you hide it. You make up reasons. You're busy, you're tired, you're not really a party person. On the outside, it looks like a choice. On the inside, it's an alarm going off that nobody else can hear.

[warmly] And shame does something sneaky here. It's another thing to be anxious about. Now you're not just worried about the call, you're worried about being the kind of person who worries about calls. The alarm goes off about the alarm. That's why it can feel so exhausting. It's the fear, plus the shame about the fear, both at once.

## helps

[warmly] So let's start with what your alarm is trying to do for you. It isn't trying to ruin your life. It's trying to keep you safe, the only way it knows how. It just needs some new information: that toast is not a fire.

[steady] And here's the principle. The way to turn an alarm down is not to avoid what sets it off. It's to go towards it, in steps so small that you can manage them, and stay long enough for the alarm to learn there was no fire.

[thoughtful] So make the step smaller. Before: ring the dentist back, which feels impossible. After: today, just write down what you're going to say. Tomorrow, dial the number and stay on the line for the hold music. The day after, have the conversation. Each step is small enough to do, and each one teaches your brain something new.

[steady] Then stay with it. When the alarm goes off, it rises, peaks and then falls by itself, if you give it time. Before: you feel the panic rising at the party door and you leave straight away, and your brain learns that leaving saved you. After: you stand in the hallway for five minutes, breathing out slowly, and you feel it start to come down on its own. That's your brain learning, in real time.

[warmly] Name it, too. When your heart starts racing, say in your head: this is my alarm. It's going off for toast. That small sentence puts a little space between you and the noise.

[thoughtful] And use other people. Ask a friend to come with you to the first part of the party, or sit next to you while you make the call. You don't have to trust that the whole evening will be fine. You only have to trust the next five minutes.

[steady] And if anxiety is getting in the way of your daily life, your work, your sleep or the people you love, it's worth talking to your GP. In England you can also refer yourself to NHS Talking Therapies, which uses exactly these step-by-step methods. That's not giving up. It's getting help with something that's very treatable.

## close

[warmly] So think back to that smoke alarm on the kitchen ceiling, going off every time someone makes toast. It was never broken. It was just set a little too sensitive, by a brain that loves you and wants you to survive.

[thoughtful] And you've been living with that noise for a long time, and getting on with things anyway. That's not weakness. That's a lot of quiet courage.

[steady] So here's one tiny thing for today. Pick one small thing you've been avoiding, and do the very first, smallest piece of it. Not the call. Just write down what you'd say.

[warmly] It's only toast. Let the alarm learn.
