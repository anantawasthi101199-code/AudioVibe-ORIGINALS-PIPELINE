## opening

[steady] On the 2nd of November 1917, a British foreign secretary signed a letter of sixty-seven words, and two peoples are still arguing about it.

[measured] The letter is called the Balfour Declaration. It promised British support for a national home for the Jewish people in Palestine, a small strip of land on the eastern shore of the Mediterranean. When it was signed, about nine in every ten people living in that land were Arabs, Muslim and Christian.

[thoughtful] This is the first chapter of the story of Israel and Palestine. It covers the years from the 1880s to 1939, before there was any state of Israel, and before anyone had heard of Hamas. Every argument you hear in the news today has a root in these years: who was there, who was promised what, and who had the right to decide.

[steady] So let's go back to the land itself, before the letter.

## roots

[measured] In the 1880s, this land was part of the Ottoman Empire, a huge Muslim state ruled from Istanbul in what is now Turkey. There was no country called Palestine on the map, and no country called Israel. Jews call this land Eretz Israel, the Land of Israel. Arabs call it Filastin, Palestine. Most of the people living there were Arab farmers and townspeople, and a small, old Jewish community lived mainly in Jerusalem, Hebron, Safed and Tiberias.

[thoughtful] At the same time, in Russia and Eastern Europe, Jews were being attacked in waves of violence called pogroms. In 1897 a Jewish journalist from Vienna, Theodor Herzl, gathered a meeting in Basel in Switzerland. It launched Zionism, the movement to build a homeland for the Jewish people in the land of their ancient history. Jewish settlers began to arrive, buying land and starting farms. Some Arab farmers who had worked that land for generations lost it when absent landlords sold it.

[steady] Then came the First World War, and the Ottoman Empire joined the losing side. Britain wanted allies against it and made promises. In 1915 and 1916 a British official in Cairo, Henry McMahon, wrote to the Sharif of Mecca, Hussein, an Arab leader. He promised support for Arab independence in return for a revolt. Arabs say that promise included Palestine. Britain later said it did not. In 1916 Britain and France secretly agreed to divide the region between themselves. And in 1917 came Balfour's letter to the Jewish community in Britain.

[thoughtful] Think of a landlord who promises the same flat to two tenants, while privately planning to keep the keys. That is roughly what the Arab side and the Jewish side both came to feel, from different ends of the same building. The letter itself tried to cover this. It said nothing should be done to harm the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. It did not mention their political rights.

[measured] After the war, in 1922, the League of Nations, the body before the United Nations, gave Britain the job of running Palestine. This was called the Mandate, and it wrote the Balfour promise into its text. A British census that year counted about seven hundred and fifty thousand people. About eleven in every hundred were Jewish.

[steady] Then the numbers began to move. Jewish immigration rose through the 1920s, and rose sharply in the 1930s after the Nazis took power in Germany in 1933. By 1939 about a third of the people in Palestine were Jewish. Violence rose with it. There were riots in 1920 and 1921, and in August 1929 a dispute at the holy wall in Jerusalem, the Western Wall to Jews and the Buraq Wall to Muslims, spread across the country. A British inquiry counted one hundred and thirty-three Jews killed, including dozens in Hebron, and one hundred and sixteen Arabs, most of them by British police.

[measured] In 1936 Arabs began a general strike that became a three-year uprising against British rule and Jewish immigration, the Arab Revolt. Britain crushed it with soldiers, house demolitions and hangings. Historians usually estimate that about five thousand Arabs were killed, along with several hundred Jews and British. In 1937 a British commission, the Peel Commission, said for the first time that the two sides could not share one state and proposed dividing the land. In 1939, with war coming, Britain changed course and limited Jewish immigration, just as Jews in Europe had nowhere left to go.

## sides

[steady] Each side tells this period in its own way, so here is each, in its own terms.

[measured] Israel's founders and its government today say this. The Jewish people have lived in and prayed towards this land for three thousand years. They were a people without safety anywhere, and the years that followed proved how true that was. They say Zionists bought land legally, drained swamps, built towns, and accepted the principle of sharing the land when it was offered, while Arab leaders refused it and turned to violence, as in Hebron in 1929.

[measured] Palestinian leaders and historians say this. Their people were living on this land for centuries, they were the great majority, and nobody asked them. They say a European empire gave away a country that was not its to give, to people arriving from another continent. They say the Mandate blocked the self-rule that was promised to Arabs elsewhere, that their revolt was a fight for independence, and that it was crushed with force.

[thoughtful] Historians from both communities disagree on much of this. They largely agree on one thing: both peoples had a real attachment to the land, and Britain made promises it could not keep to both.

## now

[steady] As of November 2026, the argument that began here has become something far larger. On the 7th of October 2023, Hamas, the armed Islamist movement that has governed Gaza since 2007, and which the US, the UK and the EU designate a terrorist organisation, attacked southern Israel. Israel's government says about twelve hundred people were killed and about two hundred and fifty taken hostage. Gaza's health ministry, run by Hamas, says more than seventy thousand Palestinians have been killed in the war since. The UN says it considers those figures broadly reliable.

[measured] A ceasefire brokered by the United States took effect in October 2025, and the living Israeli hostages were released. It has held in name. Gaza health officials say at least fourteen hundred Palestinians have been killed since it began, and Israel says five of its soldiers have died. Israel says Hamas is rearming, and Hamas denies it. Talks on Hamas giving up its weapons and on Israel withdrawing its troops are stalled, and Hamas has rejected a disarmament plan from the Board of Peace, the body set up under the American plan.

[thoughtful] What to watch, in the words of those involved, is whether those talks move at all. The next chapters follow how we got from here to there. And it all still runs back to the same place: a letter of sixty-seven words, signed in London, about a land its author would not see for years.
