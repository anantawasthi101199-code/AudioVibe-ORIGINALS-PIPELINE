## opening

[measured] In June 2020, soldiers of the world's two most populous countries fought each other on a Himalayan ridge in the Galwan Valley with clubs, rocks and bare hands, and when it was over, India said twenty of its soldiers were dead, and China later said four of its soldiers had died.

[steady] They fought with clubs because of an agreement not to fire guns along this border. And they fought at all because India and China have never agreed where their border is. It runs for thousands of kilometres across the Himalayas, the highest mountains on Earth. India says it is about three thousand, four hundred and eighty kilometres long. China says it is about two thousand. They cannot even agree on its length.

[thoughtful] This episode goes back to where the lines were drawn, from the British Empire in the 1800s to the 1950s, when the new Republic of India and the new People's Republic of China found they had inherited the problem. Why should you care? Because these are two nuclear-armed countries of more than a billion people each, home together to more than a third of all the people alive. So let's go back to where it began.

## roots

[steady] In the 1800s, Britain ruled India. To the north lay Tibet, a high, cold Buddhist land, ruled from Lhasa by the Dalai Lama and his government. China's Qing dynasty, the last imperial dynasty, claimed authority over Tibet, but how much control it really had changed a great deal over the years. Between them were mountains so high and empty that, for most of history, nobody needed a line at all. Herders crossed the passes in summer. Nobody lived up there in winter.

[measured] The British began drawing lines, for their own reasons. They were worried about the Russian Empire pushing south through Central Asia. In the west, in 1865, a British surveyor called William Johnson drew a line that put Aksai Chin, a high, dry plateau almost empty of people, inside Kashmir, then a princely state under British protection. In 1899 the British offered China a different line, further south, that would have left much of Aksai Chin to China. China never replied. So two British lines existed in the west, and neither was agreed.

[steady] In the east, the story is about the Simla Convention of 1914. In 1911 the Qing dynasty fell, and in 1913 Tibet declared that it governed itself. The British called a conference at Simla, their summer capital in the hills of India, with representatives of Britain, China and Tibet. During it, the British foreign secretary for India, Henry McMahon, and Tibet's representative agreed on a line along the crest of the eastern Himalayas. It became known as the McMahon Line. It put Tawang and the land south of the high ridges, about the size of Austria, inside British India.

[thoughtful] Here is the problem in one picture. Imagine two neighbours whose gardens meet in a wood. Long ago, a third person drew a line through the wood on a map. One neighbour's great-grandfather signed the map. The other's never did. Now both families have built houses close to the trees. China's representative at Simla initialled a draft, but the Chinese government refused to accept the convention, and no Chinese government since has accepted the McMahon Line. For years even the British left it out of their own official maps.

[measured] Then everything changed in a few years. India became independent in August 1947 and took over the British maps and claims. In October 1949 the Communist Party won China's civil war and founded the People's Republic. In 1950 its army entered Tibet. Suddenly the two new states were neighbours along the whole length of the Himalayas. In 1954 they signed an agreement on trade with Tibet that set out five principles of peaceful coexistence, and in India people said Hindi Chini bhai bhai, Indians and Chinese are brothers.

[steady] But in the west, China had quietly built a road across Aksai Chin, linking its regions of Xinjiang and Tibet. It was finished in 1957. India found out in 1957 and 1958, from Chinese maps and then from its own patrols. To India, that road ran across Indian territory. To China, it ran across Chinese territory that it had used for years. In 1959, after a failed uprising in Tibet, the Dalai Lama fled to India and was given shelter, and soldiers on the two sides clashed for the first time. The disagreement about lines on a map had become a disagreement between armies.

## sides

[measured] India's government says the boundary is well established by treaty, custom and geography. It says the McMahon Line was agreed with Tibet, which had the right to agree it, and that the watershed of the high Himalayas is the natural border. It says Aksai Chin is part of Ladakh, in the Indian union territory of that name, and is occupied by China. It calls the land south of the McMahon Line the state of Arunachal Pradesh, home to more than a million Indian citizens, who vote in Indian elections.

[steady] China's government says the boundary was never formally delimited, and that the McMahon Line was drawn by British imperialists at a conference China rejected, with a Tibetan government that had no right to sign treaties. It says Aksai Chin has always been part of China and was used by its people and its road long before India objected. China calls much of Arunachal Pradesh Zangnan, meaning southern Tibet, and says it belongs to China. China says the dispute should be settled through negotiation, taking history into account.

[thoughtful] In 1962 these two cases led to a short war, which is the story of the next episode. The human cost of the disagreement since then has fallen on soldiers posted at heights where the air is thin and winter reaches minus forty, on herders who have lost their old grazing grounds, and on the families of the soldiers killed in Galwan in 2020: twenty on the Indian side, by India's count, and four on the Chinese side, by China's.

## now

[steady] As of November 2026, the two countries still have no agreed border. They work along what is called the Line of Actual Control, the line each side's troops actually hold, which neither side has fully marked on a shared map. In October 2024 they agreed to pull troops back from the last two points of the 2020 standoff, at Depsang and Demchok in Ladakh. Since then, direct flights between them have resumed, and their leaders have met.

[measured] In 2026 officials from both countries have said they want to speed up talks on drawing the boundary, starting with what they call an early harvest, the easier stretches first. After the latest round of talks, the two governments said they would keep seeking what they describe as a fair, reasonable and mutually acceptable settlement, and that the next round would be held in India in 2027.

[thoughtful] And up in the Galwan Valley, the river still runs grey with glacier water past the ridge where, in June 2020, two armies fought without firing a single shot.
