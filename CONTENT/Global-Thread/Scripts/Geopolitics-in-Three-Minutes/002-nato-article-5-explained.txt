## hook

[steady] In more than seventy-five years, NATO's most famous promise has been used only once, and it was not used to defend Europe. This is the story of Article 5, the line at the heart of the Western alliance.

## explain

[measured] NATO, the North Atlantic Treaty Organisation, was founded in April 1949 by twelve countries in North America and Western Europe, with Stalin's Soviet Union as the worry in the room. Today it has thirty-two members. Article 5 is one short paragraph of that treaty. It says an armed attack against one member shall be considered an attack against them all.

[thoughtful] Here is what people often miss. It does not say every member must send soldiers. It says each will take the action it thinks necessary, which can include armed force. American senators insisted on that wording in 1949, because they did not want to hand their right to decide on war to a treaty. Think of neighbours on one street who agree that if one house is broken into, everyone responds. One might come round with a cricket bat. Another might phone the police. The promise is real, and the size of the response is left to each house.

[steady] Each side reads that promise in its own way. Member governments say it keeps the peace, because no attacker can count on facing one country alone. Moscow says NATO's growth to its borders is itself a threat. NATO says its members chose to join, and that it threatens nobody who does not attack.

## close

[measured] The only use came on the 12th of September 2001, the day after the attacks on New York and Washington. The allies invoked Article 5 for the United States, and NATO planes went on to patrol American skies. Its strength has always rested on something a treaty cannot write down: every country believing that the others will really come.
