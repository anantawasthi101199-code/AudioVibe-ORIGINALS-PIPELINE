## hook

[steady] About a fifth of the world's oil has passed through a channel of water just twenty-one miles wide at its narrowest. This is the Strait of Hormuz, the gap between Iran on one side and Oman on the other, at the mouth of the Persian Gulf.

## explain

[measured] Look at it on a map and you see why it matters. The Gulf is almost a closed lake. Saudi Arabia, Iraq, Kuwait, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates all sit on it. For most of their oil and gas, there is one way out to the open sea. Before the current crisis, about twenty million barrels a day went through, most of it heading to Asia. The lanes the tankers actually use are only a couple of miles wide in each direction.

[thoughtful] Think of a busy restaurant kitchen with one door. Nothing has to be destroyed to bring the whole place to a halt. Somebody only needs to stand in the doorway. Pipelines can carry some of the oil around the strait, but the US Energy Information Administration puts that at less than a quarter of the usual flow.

[steady] The two sides read the law of this water differently. Iran says the strait runs through its own coastal waters, and that it has the right to control passage, especially when it is under attack or under sanctions. The United States says it is an international strait, where ships of every nation have a right to pass, and its navy has patrolled there for decades. In the 1980s, during the war between Iran and Iraq, both sides attacked tankers, and American warships escorted them.

## close

[measured] That old argument became real in 2026, when war broke out between Iran and the United States and Israel, and traffic through the strait collapsed. As of November 2026, shipping trackers report that most traffic is still badly disrupted. Iran says the strait will stay restricted until its conditions are met, and Washington says tankers are coming back. Either way, when a door twenty-one miles wide narrows, you meet it at the petrol pump.
