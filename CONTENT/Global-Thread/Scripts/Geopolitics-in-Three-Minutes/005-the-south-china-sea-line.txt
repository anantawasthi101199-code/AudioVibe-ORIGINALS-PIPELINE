## hook

[measured] A dashed line first drawn on a Chinese map in 1947 now encloses about nine tenths of the South China Sea, a stretch of water that carries trillions of dollars of the world's trade every year.

[steady] This is the story of the nine-dash line, and of the 2016 ruling that the Philippines won and China rejected.

## explain

[steady] The South China Sea lies between China, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia, Brunei and Taiwan. It is full of small islands, reefs and rocks, with rich fishing grounds and oil and gas under the seabed. In 1947 the government of the Republic of China, then ruling mainland China, published a map with eleven dashes looping around most of the sea. After the Communist victory, the People's Republic kept the line, and in the early 1950s it reduced it to nine dashes. China says it has historic rights inside that line.

[measured] Most of the other countries rely instead on the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea, a treaty from 1982. Under it, a country gets a zone of two hundred nautical miles from its coast to fish and drill in. Picture a fence drawn on an old map, running round most of a shared lake and close to your neighbours' front steps. The neighbours say the modern rules give each house its own stretch of shore. In 2013 the Philippines took China to an international tribunal under that treaty. On the 12th of July 2016 the tribunal ruled that China had no legal basis to claim historic rights to resources inside the nine-dash line. China did not take part, and its government said the ruling was null and void and had no binding force.

[steady] China's government says the islands have been Chinese since ancient times and that the dispute is for the countries directly involved, without outside interference. The Philippines' government says the 2016 award is final and binding under international law, and calls the waters off its west coast the West Philippine Sea. Vietnam, which calls it the East Sea, has its own claims, as do Malaysia, Brunei and Taiwan.

## close

[measured] The most watched place is Second Thomas Shoal, which the Philippines calls Ayungin and China calls Ren'ai Jiao. There, the Philippines keeps a handful of marines on an old warship it deliberately ran aground in 1999, the BRP Sierra Madre. In September 2026, according to the US Naval Institute, Chinese coast guard ships and warships blocked a Philippine mission bringing them supplies. Each side blamed the other.

[steady] As of November 2026 the standoff continues, and every few weeks a small boat sets out to bring food and water to the marines on that rusting ship.
