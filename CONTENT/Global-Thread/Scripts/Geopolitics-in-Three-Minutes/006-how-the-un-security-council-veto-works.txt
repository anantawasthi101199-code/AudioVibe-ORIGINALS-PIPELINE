## hook

[measured] The UN Security Council has fifteen members, but just five of them can stop any decision it makes, even if all fourteen others vote yes.

[steady] This is the story of the Security Council veto, and of why it was written into the United Nations Charter in 1945.

## explain

[steady] The Security Council is the part of the United Nations that can order sanctions, approve peacekeeping missions and authorise the use of force, and its decisions bind every member country. Five members sit on it permanently: China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States, the main winners of the Second World War. Ten more are elected by the General Assembly for two years at a time. A decision needs nine votes, and no "no" vote from any of the five permanent members. One "no" from any of them is a veto.

[measured] Why build it that way? The League of Nations, the world body before the UN, had failed to stop the Second World War, partly because great powers left it or never joined. When the leaders of the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union planned the UN in 1944 and 1945, they wanted the big powers inside the tent. Think of a club where five founding members each hold a key that can lock the door. The club works only when none of them uses it, but none of them would have joined without it. The Charter was signed in San Francisco on the 26th of June 1945, and the Soviet Union cast the first veto the following year.

[steady] Supporters, including the five permanent members, say the veto keeps the great powers talking inside the UN instead of fighting outside it. Many other countries say it is unfair that five nations can block action, even on wars they are involved in. The Soviet Union and then Russia have used the veto more than any other member, and the United States second, many times on resolutions about Israel. Since 2022, under a resolution of the General Assembly, every veto triggers a debate there within ten working days, so the country using it has to explain itself to everyone.

## close

[measured] As of November 2026, the Security Council has been unable to agree on decisions about the war in Ukraine, because Russia has vetoed resolutions it says were one-sided, and on parts of the war in Gaza, because the United States has vetoed resolutions it says were unbalanced.

[steady] The five keys are still in the same five hands they were given in San Francisco in 1945.
